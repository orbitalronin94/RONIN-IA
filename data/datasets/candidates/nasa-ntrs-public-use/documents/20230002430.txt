<p>The Active Response Gravity Offload System (ARGOS) at NASA Johnson Space Center 
</p>
<p>(JSC) is an analog environment that can offload pressurized suited subjects for various 
</p>
<p>reduced gravity simulations. The suit is suspended from a robotic overhead crane by a cable 
</p>
<p>connected to the suit via a gimbal with an adjustable pivot point (i.e. offload attachment). 
</p>
<p>There has been increased interest in providing planetary pressurized suited training at 
</p>
<p>ARGOS in preparation for lunar missions. Determination of the appropriate gimbal pivot 
</p>
<p>point location for a given subject is vital for a high-fidelity functional lunar simulation. 
</p>
<p>Interactions between the pivot point location and human-spacesuit center of gravity (CG) can 
</p>
<p>result in righting moments that may lead to artificially stable or unrealistically challenging 
</p>
<p>configurations. Changing the pivot point location is time consuming and repeated adjustment 
</p>
<p>can result in loss of valuable pressurized suited time. This paper aims to share knowledge 
</p>
<p>obtained from the offloading characterization efforts during pressurized suited testing at 
</p>
<p>ARGOS and document the ongoing process to define an appropriate pivot point location 
</p>
<p>through iterative quantitative and qualitative assessments. Human-spacesuit CG locations for 
</p>
<p>the ARGOS lunar simulation were estimated using a 3D body scan and density model 
</p>
<p>combined with spacesuit hardware CAD and specifications. Early pilot testing of the gimbal 
</p>
<p>revealed that setting the pivot point coincident with the modeled CG location was not always 
</p>
<p>possible due to the current gimbal design, and small pivot point shifts had noticeable effects 
</p>
<p>on subject stability. Fourteen subjects performed a series of CG-related tasks in the 
</p>
<p>Exploration Extravehicular Mobility Unit (xEMU) to assess simulation characteristics. 
</p>
<p>Through iterative testing, this task list evolved to streamline the process needed to efficiently 
</p>
<p>identify a suitable pivot point for a given subject. The developed methodology will be critical 
</p>
<p>for pivot point selection during astronaut training in the ARGOS environment. 
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>RESSURIZED spacesuit testing in a lunar analog environment has been gaining increased interest in recent 
</p>
<p>years as teams prepare for a return to the Moon. Numerous analog environments have been developed by NASA1-
</p>
<p>3, and all come with different advantages and limitations. The Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory (NBL) offers unthetered 
</p>
<p>freedom of movement and a large work volume with multiple suited operation capability, however water drag impacts 
</p>
<p>dynamic motions. Parabolic flight provides realistic reduced gravity offload, but the cabin volume is limited and task 
</p>
<p>durations can’t exceed 30 s1. The Active Response Gravity Offload System (ARGOS) at NASA Johnson Space Center 
</p>
<p>(JSC) is an analog environment which provides a reduced gravity simulation at any chosen gravity level between 0-
</p>
<p>1g4,5. ARGOS expanded the limitations of previous offload devices such as the partial gravity simulator (POGO) by 
</p>
<p>additng translational capability, improving gimbal mechanisms, and enhancing simulation quality through robotic 
</p>
<p>control1,4,6. The system actively tracks and follows the subject’s translational motions while providing a constant force 
</p>
<p>offload (e.g. 1/6g) through a feedback-controlled overhead robotic crane4.  
</p>
<p>The primary ARGOS gimbal mechanism that has been used for pressurized testing is the Spreader Bar Exploration 
</p>
<p>Gimbal paired with an exploration PLSS (Portable Life Support System) mockup, or MockPLSS. The MockPLSS is 
</p>
<p>a weighted rigid structure that attaches to the Exploration Extravehicular Mobility Unit (xEMU) hatch to mimic the 
</p>
<p>mass of an exploration PLSS, or xPLSS, and simultaneously provides an attachment point for the gimbal. Rotational 
</p>
<p>movements occur through a passive gimballing mechanism that attaches to the MockPLSS at a common pivot point 
</p>
<p>for pitch and roll, and a common axis of rotation overhead for yaw. Thus, the lifting/offloading of the human-spacsuit 
</p>
<p>occurs through one location on the gimbal called the gimbal 
</p>
<p>pivot point. Adjustment of this gimballing mechanism is a 
</p>
<p>non-trivial process as the overall system center of gravity (CG) 
</p>
<p>changes across subjects and postures. Given that there are 
</p>
<p>hundreds of possible gimbal configurations, a standardized 
</p>
<p>and repeatable approach was developed to determine a 
</p>
<p>subject’s gimbal setting. This required calculation of the CG 
</p>
<p>of the overall system and computer modeling of how this CG 
</p>
<p>location correlates to the actual gimbal hardware pivot point 
</p>
<p>positioning. This paper highlights the methods used to 
</p>
<p>calculate and model the system CG as well as the results 
</p>
<p>gained through various measures across multiple xEMU 
</p>
<p>subjects participating in engineering tests conducted at 
</p>
<p>ARGOS. 
</p>

</p>

<p>1. Subject-Specific CG 
</p>
<p>For the subject-specific CG, 
</p>
<p>subjects were scanned in a 3D full 
</p>
<p>body surface scanner (Vitronic 
</p>
<p>Machine Vision, LTD, Atlanta, GA) 
</p>
<p>in a neutral standing posture with 
</p>
<p>arms slightly away from the sides 
</p>
<p>(Figure 2). All scans were taken in 
</p>
<p>minimal scanwear clothing. 
</p>
<p>Additional comfort and cooling 
</p>
<p>garments worn during suited 
</p>
<p>activities were assumed to not 
</p>
<p>significantly impact the location of the calculated subject CG. The scans were parameterized with a homogenous body 
</p>
<p>shape template, allowing for uniform body segment definitions. The scans were then broken down into segment-wise 
</p>
<p>CG calculations which considered each segment to have homogeneous density and a center of mass coincident with 
</p>
<p>the geometric center. The whole-body CG was calculated in the Blender environment (Blender Foundation, the 
</p>
<p>Netherlands) from the combined segment-wise CGs and output in the model as a representative sphere that could be 
</p>
<p>overlayed with the suit and ARGOS hardware. This is a similar CG modeling approach as was taken during NBL Z2 
</p>
<p>spacesuit testing.7  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>2.  Suit-Specific CG 
</p>
<p>Similar to the approach used for 
</p>
<p>determining the subject whole-body CG, 
</p>
<p>the suit CG was determined by breaking 
</p>
<p>down the hardware into individual segment 
</p>
<p>point masses. The 3D model of the xEMU 
</p>
<p>was positioned into a neutral standing 
</p>
<p>posture matched to the subject’s body scan 
</p>
<p>and was resized to the particular subject of 
</p>
<p>interest. This resizing included adjustments 
</p>
<p>made to softgoods lengths as well waist 
</p>
<p>sizing ring changes. The calculation was 
</p>
<p>again done within Blender and produced a 
</p>
<p>representative CG sphere for overlaying 
</p>
<p>with the other models (Figure 3). For the 
</p>
<p>combined system CG calculation, the suit 
</p>
<p>was superimposed over the body model 
</p>
<p>using body landmarks from the scan to standardize positioning across subjects within the suited model. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>specifications (Figure 4a). The MockPLSS has the capablility of adding weight to it to better mimic the CG of a variety 
</p>
<p>of PLSS designs or to alter the entire system CG. Working with the designers of the MockPLSS and xPLSS teams, it 
</p>
<p>was determined that an additional 10lbs in the center pin position on the top weight rail, and two 20lb weights on the

<p>B. Correlating the System CG to the Gimbal Hardware  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>1. Gimbal Mechanism Mapping 
</p>
<p> The ARGOS gimbal mechanism consists of three layers of adjustment capability. The first is a slider along the 
</p>
<p>side of the MockPLSS that offers three different positions spaced 1.0 in (2.5 cm) apart. The second and third levels of 
</p>
<p>adjustability are provided by a sidearm piece with 11 linear bolt positions that can also be rotated around a mounting 
</p>
<p>plate in 16 different clocking orientations (Figure 4b). This adjustablility provides for 528 different gimbal positions 
</p>
<p>across a wide area. Numbering and color schemes were applied to the mapping to quickly translate pivot point dots 
</p>
<p>on the map to actual hardware settings during testing (Figure 5). This mapping scheme and translation method between 
</p>
<p>the computer models and the actual hardware had not previously been put into practice at ARGOS and made for much 
</p>
<p>more effective real-time identification of new pivot point settings when needed during testing. 
</p>
<p>2. Pivot Point Selection 
</p>
<p>For each new subject-suit-gimbal environment, the system CG is calculated and aligned with the ARGOS gimbal 
</p>
<p>hardware model. In early testing, pivot points were selected systematically to understand the characteristics of the 
</p>
<p>system. Currently, an initial target pivot point is identified based on previous test experience, anthropometric similarity 
</p>
<p>to previous test subjects, and the objectives of the given test day. The test conductors then determine a pivot point as

</p>
<p>dead zones where the pivot point cannot physically be positioned to with the current gimbal design (Figure 5). This 
</p>
<p>presented several limitations. It was not possible for every subject to set the pivot point coincident with their subject-
</p>
<p>specific system CG, nor was it possible to provide an identical offset to each subject. While there is often a desire to 
</p>
<p>align the pivot point with the CG of the system, these CG-pivot point coincident settings are incredibly challenging 
</p>
<p>for the subject to manage in a way that has seemed unrealistic and has had considerable impacts on the ability to 
</p>
<p>accomplish most test objectives. Additionally, during initial scale offload where the subject is completely suspended 
</p>
<p>by ARGOS, the subject may rotate upsidedown about the pivot point with this type of setting.  
</p>
<p>An added potential factor is that the system CG is calculated based on a static neutral posture. When the subject 
</p>
<p>bends over, climbs stairs, or goes into a prone position for example, the location of the system CG has changed and 
</p>
<p>responds dynamically throughout these motions. The current gimbal design is not capable of adjusting its relative 
</p>
<p>attachment point to the subject dynamically throughout these motions, nor is it practical for the test team to make 
</p>
<p>gimbal adjustments in real time while a subject is in a different posture. Adding to these challenges are the effects of 
</p>
<p>gravity that are still at play despite the subject being offloaded by the ARGOS system. For example, the legs and arms 
</p>
<p>are not individually offloaded by the gimbal, so any movement against gravity is felt at 1g by the subject. This is 
</p>
<p>particularly the case in prone positions where the entire lower torso is outside of the region of the offloading cable

<p>Thus, a methodology was needed to identify a pivot point placement (defined as a “functional gimbal configuration 
</p>
<p>zone”) that could enable subjects to accomplish the test objectives while experiencing a consistent weighout 
</p>

</p>
<p>enough buffer to account for subject and posture variability as well as gimbal design limitations.

<p>C. Gimbal Evaluation Tasks 
</p>
<p>The tasks used to evaluate the suitability of a given gimbal setting for a particular subject have evolved throughout 
</p>
<p>testing as more experience and knowledge was gained on what tasks were most effective at identifying sub-optimal 
</p>
<p>settings. The tasks generally focused on analyzing whole-body sway and reaches commonly used in external load 
</p>
<p>carriage CG assessments8,9 as well as functional tasks such as object pickup and prone-to-recover. An example of the 
</p>
<p>gimbal evaluation tasks used is shown in Table 1. While not comprehensive of all of the expected tasks of a planetary 
</p>
<p>EVA, the list shown represents tasks that were found to be challenging and characteristic of unstable or overly stable 
</p>
<p>planetary EVA performance. This downsized list allowed time for multiple pivot point setting assessments with each 
</p>
<p>subject to assess an appropriate setting for the rest 
</p>
<p>of the test objectives of the day. For example, if 
</p>
<p>lunar geology tasks were the main focus of an 
</p>
<p>ARGOS simulation, then more time was given to 
</p>
<p>find a setting to best accomplish those tasks, as 
</p>
<p>opposed to tests where walking was the primary 
</p>
<p>objective which is easier to manage in less optimal 
</p>
<p>settings. By putting brand new subjects through 
</p>
<p>these challenging postures and balance limits at the 
</p>
<p>start of the test, we were also able to minimize the 
</p>
<p>impacts of general learning effect in the ARGOS 
</p>
<p>suited environment on subsequent objectives for the 
</p>
<p>run. Testing of these  tasks was deemed as non-
</p>
<p>human research since it was hardware evaluation-
</p>
<p>focused, thus not subject to the NASA Institutional 
</p>
<p>Review Board (IRB), however the protocol was 
</p>
<p>reviewed and approved through the NASA Test 
</p>
<p>Readiness Review (TRR) process. 
</p>

</p>

<p>III. Analysis 
</p>
<p>A. Identifying the Pivot Point 
</p>
<p>“Functional Zone” 
</p>
<p>Through these tasks and 
</p>
<p>subjective feedback, generalized 
</p>
<p>findings were identified in regards to what can be expected for settings that fall within the “functional zone” which 
</p>
<p>lies above and aft of the system CG. Settings that are too far forward of the system CG resuled in extreme hunching 
</p>
<p>(Figure 6), while settings that are too low tend to have a very unstable knife-edge tippiness at the waist. Both types of 
</p>
<p>settings result in high cognitive effort to manage the resulting moments. Settings that are high and aft are less 
</p>
<p>challenging to manage, however if the pivot point is too high it can feel like the gimbal is artificially pulling up and 
</p>
<p>restricts leaning postures, while if it’s too aft there are unrealistically comfortable upright and sitting postures (Figure 
</p>
<p>7) that are possible, and in both cases it becomes difficult to assume and maintain ground-based postures.  
</p>
<p>A dedicated pivot point assessment test allowed for 10 different pivot point settings to be tested on one experienced 
</p>
<p>xEMU subject on the same day (Figure 8a). In this dedicated test there were several pivot point settings tested in both 
</p>
<p>the fore/aft direction, as well as up/down, system CG-coincident, and forward of the system CG. Up until this point 
</p>
<p>there were limited opportunities to test more than two settings per subject, and those changes typically focused on the 
</p>
<p>fore-aft direction. The feedback gained through testing these settings in rapid succession, repeating the same tasks 
</p>
<p>over and over, provided great insight into the bounds of the functional zone. Comparing metrics across the ten settings, 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>The functional zone was further refined with subsequent subjects who were allotted 2-4 different pivot point 
</p>
<p>evaluations per test for a total of 68 different pivot point configurations tested. Their functional settings generally lie 
</p>
<p>within a box that is approximately 1.0-2.5 in (2.5-6.4 cm) above and 0.5-1.0 in (1.3-2.5 cm) aft of the system CG 
</p>
<p>(Figure 9). This zone is what we currently use as our target functional zone for any new subject participating in an 
</p>
<p>xEMU run at ARGOS. Although the subject pool here was limited to subjects who were able to fit in the available 
</p>
<p>size of the xEMU HUT and therefore do not represent the full astronaut population, they do represent a wide range of 
</p>
<p>anthropometry (175.1 ± 8.4 cm). The subjective feedback across subjects found that fore/aft shifts are much more 
</p>
<p>sensitive than up/down shifts. Even as small as a 0.2 in (0.6 cm) fore/aft shift was noticeable by subjects, whereas 
</p>
<p>up/down shifts of as much as 1.0 in (2.5 cm) showed minimal performance differences. Variability within the 
</p>
<p>functional zone still exists across subjects in how well they can accomplish a variety of tasks, in part due to differences 
</p>
<p>in anthropometry and flexibility. However, placing the pivot point in this region has been shown to provide consistent 
</p>
<p>reduced gravity effects and has been effective for completing planetary EVA-type tasks.

<p>B. Lessons Learned and Forward Work 
</p>
<p>1. Learning Effect 
</p>
<p>Although our evaluations were not aimed at assessing the existence or duration of a learning effect at ARGOS, it 
</p>
<p>was apparent that it does exist for every new subject. Experienced subjects with a pre-defined gimbal setting are ready 
</p>
<p>to go within minutes of leaving the donning stand, but new subjects need a half to a full day of experience before they 
</p>
<p>are familiarized enough to move through tasks comfortably. There are multiple techniques used to complete various 
</p>
<p>tasks, and subjects differ in what they prefer. There are also postures like kneeling to pick up an object that require 
</p>
<p>proper foot placement that can be difficult for less experienced subjects to target initially. Allowing them time to try 
</p>
<p>different methods to find what works best for them at the beginning of the day makes the rest of the test day more 
</p>
<p>efficient. It has been observed that the learning effect will impact test objectives, thus it is recommended to account 
</p>
<p>for necessary time for subjects to become familiar with the test environment.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>2. Dedicated CG Runs 
</p>
<p>Dedicated CG evaluation assessments where a subject can continuously evaluate multiple gimbal settings greatly 
</p>
<p>improves our understanding of the suit-gimbal interactions. By testing multiple settings at the fringes and extremes of 
</p>
<p>the zone we were already working within we were able to better define the “functional zone” as well as form a better 
</p>
<p>picture of what “bad” settings look like in each direction of pivot point movement. This greatly supported our ability 
</p>
<p>to quickly evaluate a subject’s posture and listen to their feedback to efficiently adjust the pivot point to improve any 
</p>
<p>gimbal-related challenges they were experiencing. A dedicated CG run is highly recommended for any new suit in the 
</p>
<p>ARGOS environment, keeping in mind that the subject must also have sufficient experience before evaluating settings 
</p>
<p>for comparative data.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>

</p>
<p>The current bar gimbal was designed to maximize the options for pivot point placement relative to the hardware 
</p>
<p>while maintaining critical factors of safety. No input was available at the time on where the pivot point should be or 
</p>
<p>how often it might need to be adjusted. This study highlighted the importance of human factors in the design of 
</p>
<p>hardware that interfaces directly with subjects and engineers operating ARGOS in real time. Every pivot point

<p>IV. Conclusion 
</p>
<p>Suited operations at ARGOS have significantly increased in recent years as it is expected to become an important 
</p>
<p>training facility for lunar exploration missions. An important concern about any analog environment is how 
</p>
<p>realistically it represents the environment it’s strying to mimic. While we recognize the importance of achieving a 
</p>
<p>“realistic” lunar simulation at ARGOS, we do not currently have comparable data to be able to say what postures and 
</p>
<p>methods of task completion are realistic with our current pivot point selections. This will require data from parabolic 
</p>
<p>flight or actual lunar analysis in a modern planetary spacesuit to make these comparisons. In lieu of such data, we 
</p>
<p>carefully choose pivot point settings that minimize any obvious gimbal influence to the subject. A dynamic gimbal 
</p>
<p>which can sense and adjust the subject’s overall system CG throughout a variety of tasks would help to mitigate the 
</p>
<p>limitations of the static model predictions, however this technology is not yet available. Through continuous testing, 
</p>
<p>the functional zone definitions we have developed for the xEMU has consistently been shown to be an effective and 
</p>
<p>acceptable means to accomplish test team objectives and is currently being applied toward other suit architectures 
</p>
<p>being utilized at ARGOS.
