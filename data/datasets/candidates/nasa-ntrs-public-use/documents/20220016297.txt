ABSTRACT 

Nuclear Electric Propulsion (NEP) is an in-space propulsion technology capable of 
enabling opposition and conjunction class crewed Mars missions. NEP subsystems include the 
reactor for heat generation, a power conversion system (PCS), power management and distribution 
(PMAD), electric propulsion subsystem (EPS), and a primary heat rejection system. Specific mass, 
or αe (kg / kWe), is a key performance parameter (KPP) of the propulsion system which is directly 
scalable with the performance and mass estimates for individual components. To inform technology 
maturation planning, full system and component level parametric modeling is ongoing to explore 
the design trade space and illustrate the effect of subsystem design choices on the system KPPs. 

In this study, scaling of high-assay, low-enriched uranium (HALEU) reactor designs is 
assessed through coupled reactor physics and thermal hydraulics analyses. Scaling analyses 
evaluate the impact of system performance parameters (power level, interface temperatures) on 
mass for direct gas cooled, pumped liquid metal, and passively cooled heat pipe reactor concepts. 
Each concept requires specific geometries and working fluids to reach the performance goals of 
PCS interface conditions (temperature, pressure, flow rate) and system mass. The reactor 
assembly includes the active core (fuel, moderator, cladding, working fluid), axial and radial neutron 
reflectors, control drums, structural support / pressure vessel, and external radiation shielding. 
Each of these components are parametrically sized based on performance parameters for a 
megawatt-class power cycle.  

Results of this scaling analysis increase NEP propulsion system modeling fidelity and 
ultimately aim to support technology down-selection along with related technology development 
planning. The reactor and shield αe are a function of several PCS design choices, and reactor 
scaling with these parameters must be considered to enable an informed decision on reactor 
geometry and working fluid combination.  

INTRODUCTION 

 National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s (NASA) Space Nuclear Propulsion 
(SNP) project is investigating nuclear thermal propulsion (NTP) and nuclear electric propulsion 
(NEP) technologies capable of enabling crewed opposition class missions to Mars in the late 2030s 
to early 2040s timeframe [1]. Fission reactors have been examined since the 1950s as a viable 
option to meet trip time, engine thrust / specific impulse (Isp), and mass goals for interplanetary 
missions [2, 3]. NEP is one of the primary nuclear systems capable of satisfying these requirements 
with a primary heat source of a low power, continuously operating reactor. NEP is a complex 
spacecraft propulsion system consisting of five main critical technology elements (CTEs):  

1. Reactor and Coolant Subsystem (RXS) 

2. Power Conversion Subsystem (PCS) 

3. Power Management and Distribution (PMAD) 

4. Electric Propulsion System (EPS) 

5. Primary Heat Rejection System (PHRS) 



2  
 

This work focuses on parametric sizing of the reactor and external shield based on 
satisfying required system performance metrics, including electric power and mass. Analyzing the 
ratio between these two metrics, commonly referred to as the specific mass (αe, units of kg / kWe), 
informs trajectory modeling for cislunar and interplanetary missions. Calculating αe in terms of 
electric power is difficult when investigating the reactor performance separate from the PCS. Thus, 
α in this work will refer to the ratio of the mass and thermal power, in units of kg / kW th. To assess 
the “true” αe, the α in this work can be divided by the PCS efficiency.  

The RXS CTE yields one of the largest contributions to the overall α, thus significant 
analyses must be performed to determine which reactor choice will allow for optimization of this 
term at the desired operating power level [2, 4, 5]. Through analysis and project subject matter 
expert (SME) input, a list of key performance parameters (KPPs) of the reactor CTE is provided in 
Table 1. These KPPs will be used for RXS technology maturation planning by the SNP project.

Each reactor configuration has a unique interface with the PCS to convert heat to electric power, 
either through direct ducting interfaces or via heat exchanger (HX). The reactor must be capable 
of increasing the PCS working fluid temperature to the desired PCIT while minimizing mass 
consequences to the entire system. Meeting these KPPs require advanced design considerations 
that have limited parallels to terrestrial applications due to the mass and volume constraints, high 
operating temperature, and heat transfer method. Choosing one of these design options is 
dependent on the thermal hydraulics (T/H), neutronics, and mass impacts on the entire system. 
The NEP reactor design types identified at the onset of NEP technology maturation planning are 
described below: 

1. High-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR): Gas-cooled thermal hydraulic loop 
connected directly to the PCS using a 72% He – 28% Xe mixture (40 g/mol) 

a) Benefits of gaseous working fluids include a simpler reactor design, chemical 
compatibility with in-core materials, and the removal of a HX. 

b) Disadvantages include increased number of single-point failures due to possible 
ducting failures, high CTE-1 pressure losses (in ducting, pressure vessel (PV) 
cooling, in-core, etc.), and elevated PV mass at high temperature / pressure [7].  

2. Heat pipe reactor (HPR): Passive lithium or sodium heat pipes (HPs) connected to the PCS 
via cross flow tube bank HX (see Figure 2) 

a) Passive HPs remove the need for a pump, are applicable to fission surface power 
(FSP) and advanced terrestrial reactor concepts, and operate at a high technology 
readiness level (TRL) from industry / government research and development [8]. 

b) Sodium HPs are limited in performance above 1300 K for the HP geometry used 
in this work, making it less extensible to higher NEP operating temperatures. 
Lithium heat pipes yield high heat throughputs, but they require extreme 
temperatures which may be unrealistic for multi-year reactor operation. 



3  
 

c) Disadvantages include the TRL of advanced lithium HPs, two-phase operational 
limits, mass scalability to higher power levels, cascading HP failure, and radiation 
streaming [4]. 

3. Liquid metal reactor (LMR): A pumped liquid metal T/H loop connected to the PCS via shell 
and tube HX (see Figure 3) 

a) Lithium provides benefits for in-core thermal hydraulic performance due to low 
pressure losses and continuous single-phase flow during steady state operation.  

b) Disadvantages include pump power and technology readiness, Li-7 enrichment to 
avoid neutron parasitic absorption, and pump blade / HX degradation due to grain 
boundary embrittlement and alloy formation [9].

ANALYSIS METHODOLOGY 

Analyzing the trade space of a reactor system requires intimate knowledge of the 
underlying physics which is typically dependent on the geometry, material, and temperature / 
pressure. To properly investigate these phenomena, performance mapping of the thermal power 
and mass and reactor scalability to reach goal KPPs of the three primary reactor technologies is 
assessed using a coupled multiphysics methodology. Figure 4 overviews the methodology used in 
this work. Temperature and power profiles are generated based on the selected reactor option, and 
the PCIT interface is used as a boundary condition for convergence. Following T/H convergence, 
the suite generates a case-specific neutronics input file in Serpent or OpenMC and an alpha based 
on all subcomponents in the RXS loop. Parametric sensitivities using this methodology will be 
presented in the Results and Discussion section to follow based on the inputs and outputs shown 
in Table 2.

THERMAL HYDRAULICS 

A majority of the reactor core sizing is based on its T/H performance which is required to 
satisfy the PCIT interface with the PCS subsystem. There exists some design limits, including 
pressure drop and sonic limits, which may force the reactor designer to increase the reactor size 
to decrease these fluid mechanical penalties. An example of a design decision to mitigate these 
effects with a penalty of mass growth is to increase the system pressure or reduce the amount of 
mass flow rate per channel in the core. The T/H models used to assess each reactor option with 
various working fluids and geometry inputs are described in this section.  

The HTGR and LMR options use a first-order, physics-based solver that converges on the 
reactor exit temperature by varying the flow rate per channel and power density. This approach 
uses a normalized axial power profile with the power density serving as the scaling factor. Due to 
the constant unit cell geometry used in this work, off-optimal reactor configurations can be observed 
as one moves extremely far away from the thermal power of the reference design. However, these

resulting off-optimal reactor geometries will yield conservative estimates of the system mass. The 
analytical procedure for obtaining T/H convergence relies on a forward-difference solver that 
balances pressure loss and enthalpy gain along the axial heated length of the flow channel [10]. 
Eq. 1 shows that the in-core pressure drop is directly related to temperature, pressure, and mass 
flux (G) in different degrees of magnitude. This equation can be used to estimate the total pressure 
loss across the channel or across individual, axially-discretized bins. Similarly, Eq. 2 introduces the 
enthalpy gain due to heating and pressure drop. Code convergence is obtained by varying the flow 
rate and power density until a desired reactor temperature, referred to as PCIT in this work, is 
reached for an average channel case. 

 

Gaseous working fluids can have extreme pressure losses depending on the system 
pressure, mass flow rate, and temperature change [11]. Liquid metal working fluids do not exhibit 
the same amount of pressure losses due to the significantly higher fluid density, allowing their 
properties to exhibit those of incompressible fluids. Pressure losses from this scalability 
assessment include the constituents of Table 3 for each test case and are included in Figure 7 
results. Analysis of these results show that the pressure loss external to the in-core flow channels 
are ~10% of the total losses expected in CTE-1.

Convective heat transfer is assessed using various empirical correlations from literature 
that are dependent on geometry, fluid properties, and other key physical phenomena [12]. 
Following convergence on the convective heat transfer, the axial average and maximum fuel 
temperature distribution are calculated using thermal conduction. Results of this approach are 
converted into interface files for the methodology described in the Reactor Physics section to 
improve the assumptions of in-core neutronics. Outputs of this model include the fluid state points 
at the reactor inlet and exit, fuel material temperature distributions, and pressure losses due to 
rapid area change in the reactor plenums, friction, and acceleration which occur in the PV, ducting, 
and in-core regions.  

HPR heat transfer is modeled using a limits-based approach and treating the heat pipe as 
an isothermal heat source with a surface temperature defined as a differential between the PCIT 
and a user-input ΔT [13, 14]. HPs are a passive heat transfer option since the internal fluid is in a 
saturation state and heat transfer primarily occurs during isothermal phase changes (such as 
boiling and condensation). The limits that govern heat pipe operation include the viscous, capillary, 
sonic, entrainment, and boiling limits which are dependent on the chosen working fluid, HP 
geometry and wick dimensions, and operating temperature [8]. For the reference KPPs of this 
study, lithium and sodium are the only viable fluid options for in-core heat removal. As seen in 
Figure 5, sodium yields a larger maximum heat throughput until ~1300 K before lithium significantly 
dominates. These results are simulated using unit cell geometries provided by the Los Alamos 
National Laboratory (LANL) [13].

Increasing HP temperature will result in increased HP heat throughput until the boiling limit 
is reached. Maximizing throughput reduces required core volume since each pipe can handle more 
heat load and allows for higher reactor power densities. However, increased core temperatures 
and power densities reduces the design margin for fuel, moderator, and other reactor 
subcomponents for the extended mission duration. Since the reactors in this work will be connected 
to the PCS via cross flow tube bank HX (as seen in Figure 2), the analysis converges on the number 
of heat pipes required to provide the desired enthalpy gain across the HX. The total number of heat 
pipes in this analysis is equal to the sum of the HPs in the fuel (FHP) and moderator (MHP) with 
dimensions provided by the LANL reference design [13]. As mentioned previously, simulated 
results can begin to reach conservative, off-optimal results as one deviates significantly from the 
reference thermal power level. Thus, the results of this work are used to describe data trends for 
HPRs while allowing for parameter optimization, if desired.  

Enthalpy is essentially pressure-independent at high temperatures, thus the required 
thermal power transfer between the HPR and the PCS working fluid is calculated using Eq. 3. A 
heat throughput factor (fHP) between zero and unity is applied. While operating at a heat throughput 
factor of 100% would be optimal from a reactor mass perspective, it is preferred to operate the HPs 
below their operational limits to avoid cascading failure during local power spikes and other off-
nominal conditions.  

REACTOR PHYSICS 

While the heat transfer from the reactor to the PCS is crucial for system operation, the 
reactor must be capable of reaching a critical configuration to generate the desired thermal power. 
Varying the reactor geometry to meet the requirements of the T/H simulation will also change the 
amount of nuclear fuel, neutron moderation, and neutron loss due to leakage from the system. The 
neutron multiplication factor (keff) is a useful metric to determine if a reactor geometry and 
heterogeneous material inventory will allow for a sustainable chain reaction. To determine if the 
NEP reactor geometries are capable of reaching a critical mass, reactor physics solvers, such as 
Serpent and OpenMC, are viable options to assess this parameter and to create an axial and radial 
core power distribution. A keff of unity is chosen as the threshold value in this coupled work to allow 
for a wide array of possible reactor geometries. Following T/H convergence, the suite is capable of 
creating a case-specific neutronics script based on satisfied reactor dimensions. This input file 
would include the active core with the converged number of unit cells, axial and radial reflectors, 
control drums, and reactor structural support (e.g., PV). If desired, the sequence can analyze the 
axial and radial power distribution from the Monte Carlo simulations to inform subsequent iterations 
of the T/H analysis. The sampling of LMR operating conditions presented in this work use a 99.9% 
enriched Li-7 working fluid. Natural lithium contains roughly 7.6% Li-6 which has an extreme

MASS ESTIMATION 

Following reactor core sizing and optimization from the coupled T/H and reactor physics 

analysis, a mass model predicts overall RXS mass based on reactor core geometries. Room-

temperature densities are used to analyze the launch mass of the reactor core components based 

on the converged dimensions and quantity of in-core unit cells. The number of unit cells and their 

geometries are used to determine the minimum active core size which is bounded with a cylinder 

or hexagonal parallelepiped. The reflector and PV mass are calculated after the active core 

dimensions are determined. For the HTGR and LMR options, PV thickness is determined via a 

hoop stress calculation to determine minimum wall thickness for the required reactor working fluid 

operating pressure. Eq. 4 and Eq. 5 show the calculations for PV thickness (tPV) and mass (mPV) 

based on the ultimate strength (UPV), inlet pressure (Pin), weld efficiency (εweld), and converged 

reactor dimensions (RPV, LPV). Weld efficiency is held constant at unity for this effort, but future 

efforts may investigate this value for different PV materials. Ultimate strength is dependent upon 

pressure vessel material selection and predicted vessel operating temperature.  

 

Due to a low operating pressure of the HPR, the vessel mass requirement is based on 

thermomechanical limits associated with the higher operating temperature since there is no active 

cooling. Current efforts analyze a tungsten-based vessel to minimize thermal creep over the reactor 

lifetime. Additional subcomponent masses, such as vessel plenums, piping, and instrumentation / 

control, are calculated using scaling correlations from the SP-100 project [15]. 

The RXS includes an external shield to mitigate neutron and gamma-ray exposure and 

damage to the non-nuclear subsystems, habitat, and cargo. The external shield is composed of 

attenuating materials which reduce gamma-ray and neutron intensity. The largest contributor to the 

external shield mass is the gamma shield due to the dense, high atomic weight materials 

necessary. Light elements tend to be excellent neutron attenuators and are commonly found in low 

density materials, such as lithium hydride or boron carbide, leading to a smaller relative impact on 

the external shield alpha [7, 15]. The shield configuration used in this analysis is a four-layer shield, 

consisting of a beryllium reflector, two neutron shield layers, and a gamma-ray shield (Figure 6). 
While the external shield thicknesses are user defined, the radii vary with the converged 

dimensions of the reactor, and the desired shadow shield angle (Θ) which will change the resulting 

material volumes and resulting subcomponent mass. Results presented in this work focus on radial 

sizing as the reactor geometry scales with constant thicknesses. Table 4 defines the material 

options for the neutron and gamma-ray shields.

channels and cylindrical fuel pins. A performant hydride-based moderator is also included to

For the HTGR, the PV mass is the primary contributor to RXS α variation within the trade 

space. By varying the sampled parameter range in Table 5 for ~six million test cases, a statistical 
distribution for the PV thickness emerges which can be observed in Figure 8. While the mean value 
of these sampled cases is approximately 1 cm, the right-skewed distribution shows that the 
thickness to accommodate internal stresses is case-specific and could dominate the overall system 
mass for high-temperature reactor options. Sufficient margin to handle additional multiphysics 
phenomena, such as launch load stress, radiation damage, and thermal creep, should be evaluated 
in future analyses to avoid PV failure during steady state and off-nominal operation. Performant 
and higher density materials, such as refractory alloys, may be required to reduce the impacts of 
these phenomena over the lifetime of the power system. Use of these materials may limit the 
applicability of HTGRs for the goal KPP criteria.

shown in Figure 9 and Figure 10. The values for reactor α include the scaled PV mass which leads

to the larger than expected α for higher inlet gas temperature cases. These trends are varied across

mass and thermal power. The reactor mass is predominately dominated by the mass flow rate and 
the thermal power is affected by both terms. However, the thermal power change is significantly 
more sensitive to these parameters than the reactor mass since each reactor has additional 
subcomponent masses, such as the reflector and PV, that will not drastically change in magnitude

Further assessment of these results show that low He-Xe mass flow rates with small ΔTs 
(top left of each heat map of Figure 9) will result in the largest α values since these reactors will 
have the smallest power level and largest PV thickness requirement. These systems may be 
applicable for science missions, but the operating power level is not high enough to enable crewed 
missions. Bounding the thermal power to 5 – 8 MW, as described in Table 1, will highlight a range 
of these input parameters that should be investigated. Increasing the PCIT to the goal of 1400 K 
will improve the T/H performance of the reactor and PCS, but there will not be a significant benefit 
from an α comparison due to the increased PV thickness, assuming that these systems will be 
operated at higher inlet temperatures.

HEAT PIPE REACTOR OPTION 

The passive HPR unit cells chosen in this analysis are operated with both sodium and 
lithium to show their benefits and consequences. Lower temperature reactors could benefit from a 
potassium or eutectic sodium-potassium alloy fluid, but these options are outside the operating 
temperature range of interest, as defined by the KPPs. The performance of these working fluids 
can be observed in Figure 5. The general configuration follows the same form as the HTGR reactor 
option: graphite matrix with interspersed cylindrical fuel pins and heat pipes. This option also 
includes smaller geometry moderator HPs that transfer approximately 10% of the heat throughput 
of the larger fuel HPs. Reactor α results for these designs include the in-core components, 
reflectors, reactor structural support mechanisms, and additional subcomponents. HX masses are 
not included in this work, but the results in this reactor scalability assessment are used to 
parametrically size the HX to observe mass trends as a result of desired pressure loss and enthalpy 
rise [14]. Investigating the physics-based response to each of these parameters has been 
performed for each working fluid at the operating conditions outlined in Table 6.

 Pressure is held constant for this analysis since it will have a minimal impact on all desired 
outputs since there is no PV thickness variation and the thermal power due to enthalpy rise is 
essentially pressure-independent at elevated temperatures. Pressure drop across the cross flow 
tube bank HX is assumed to be a constant fraction of the inlet pressure for all test cases. As 
expected, trends in reactor and external shield α for variations in mass flow rate and HX ΔT are 
comparable to those outlined in the Gas-Cooled Reactor Option section since the same He-Xe 
working fluid is chosen. However, the magnitudes are varied based on the chosen HP working fluid 
and isothermal operating temperature of the HP itself. Thus, the main sensitivity for this section, as 
shown in Figure 11, involves the 1200 K PCIT results for sodium and lithium by varying the two 
HP-specific characteristics: the HP throughput factor and HP to PCIT ΔT.  

 Since neither the HP factor nor HP to PCIT ΔT terms will have an impact on the thermal 
power requirement, the α trends are solely based on the impact on the reactor mass. However, 
operating at a larger ΔT will increase the reactor operating temperature which can introduce thermal 
stress limits over its lifetime. When comparing sodium and lithium HP reactors at the same PCS 
operating conditions, the impact of HP performance can cause drastic changes based on the heat 
throughput of each HP at different temperatures. Moving left to right in the images follow a logical 
trend that increasing the amount of heat each HP can transfer will decrease the number of required 
HPs and the reactor mass. Performance of sodium will decrease as the ΔT is increased since its 
throughput limit occurs at 1200 K; consequently, the number of HPs will have to increase to 
accommodate the reduced heat transfer. A contrasting effect is seen when increasing the ΔT 
between the isothermal HPs as the desired PCIT increases the performance for lithium at all 
temperatures due to its peak throughput occurring around 1700 K. Investigating the results of 
Figure 11 show that the magnitude and trends of α between the two working fluids are contrasting 
due to the heat throughput performance at the lower temperature ranges. As discussed in the 
Analysis Methodology section, sodium outperforms lithium until roughly 1300 K for the HP operating 
temperature which would equate to a HP ΔT of 100 K in these results. Thus, one can obtain an α

above it.

change across the reactor which is undesirable for multi-year reactor operation and could lead to 
fuel and moderator failure as a result of swelling, creep, and additional thermal stresses. A 
transparent scatter plot of the reactor α is provided in Figure 12. This plot includes sodium HPR 
cases for PCITs of 1150 and 1200 K and lithium results for 1150, 1200, and 1400 K. As shown in 
Figure 5, sodium becomes impractical at temperatures above 1400 K due to the boiling limit of the 
fluid. Key observations from these results include that the α is below 2 kg/kWth for all cases within 
the 5 – 8 MW power requirement set forth in the KPPs (Table 1). In addition, the high PCIT lithium 
cases provide the lowest α due to the increased amount of heat transfer per HP, leading to a lower 
α. The 1150 K lithium case yields the highest α  in the desired range because the heat throughput 
below 1300 K for this fluid choice is extremely small, leading to large reactor geometries.

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS 

A comparison of expected RXS α for each reactor concepts is shown in Figure 15. This 
figure provides an α trends through violin plots of each technology option for both threshold and 
goal PCITs (1200 and 1400 K, respectively) over the KPP range of power levels (5 – 8 MWth). This 
type of plot is chosen to include the statistical variation of the data (mean, median, range, etc.) 
without losing the distribution spread. The width variation across each violin distribution provides 
insight into the quantity of cases that can be expected at each α value. 

The 1200 K HPR cases have a larger mean and median reactor mass for the 5 – 8 MWth 
thermal power range, leading to an elevated α. This trend is observed due to the large range of 
heat throughput factors that are applied and the use of a thick tungsten core barrel. While tungsten 
is included in the reference reactor design, other high-strength materials, such as molybdenum or 
superalloys, may provide enough structural support with a lower mass penalty. The 1200 K HTGR 
and LMR sampled cases provide a skewed distribution with a majority of the results between 0.8 
and 1.5 kg / kWth. This showcases that the desired CTE-1 α KPP of 4.5 – 6 kg / kWe can be reached 
with an efficient PCS (25 – 33%). Based on these results, HTGR reactors would likely be optimal 
for the 1200 K PCIT conditions. These systems, in comparison to higher PCITs, would exhibit low 
pressure losses, reduced temperatures on the PV, and heritage research and development through 
the JIMO / Prometheus program [7, 16]. 

 

Figure 15. Violin Plots for the Reactor α of the HTGR, LMR, and HPR Technology Options 
at PCITs of 1200 K and 1400 K 

 

The 1400 K results show that designers will be able to reach or exceed the KPP metrics 
for any of the design options. Use of a lithium HPR will provide a less massive system due to the 
enhanced heat throughput along with passive operation. Approximately 50% of the reactor mass 
can be removed while producing a similar thermal power level. The HTGR cases observe a reduced 
mass even with a thicker PV requirement due to enhanced in-core T/H performance attributed to 
an increase in thermal power density. The mean, median, and maximum expected α all decrease 
within a sufficient range to reach goal KPPs. HTGRs and LMRs do not vary significantly due to the 
small change in pressure drop and in-core sizing due to PCIT variation. Both of these options will 
likely result in decreased mission lifetime due to the higher thermal power level and reduced fissile 
material inventory.  



15  
 

Overall, all three of these reactor options will require additional investigation into limits due 
to thermal stresses, fuel swelling, and fuel / moderator temperature. Technology maturation for 
these in-core components will further inform the down-selection on which option is preferred for 
each reactor test case. However, the results of this assessment are conservative in nature and will 
require optimization of the in-core fuel loading, control drum rotation angle, reactor geometry (i.e., 
length, reflector thickness, etc.), and other considerations to approach an ideal point-design. These 
considerations may be taken in future iterations of this work depending on project SME guidance.
