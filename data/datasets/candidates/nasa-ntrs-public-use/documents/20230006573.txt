<p>SLS Test Program Philosophy</p>
<p>SLS Testing Program set up to answer critical questions, feed design cycles, verify structural models, for qualification, and for integrated vehicle verification</p>
<p>Tests are used to answer questions that can’t easily be answered by analytical models, empirical or historical data, and when it is cheaper than other options</p>
<p>Development testing</p>
<p>Largely driven by Design Cycle needs, or risk reduction</p>
<p>Aerodynamics, AeroThermal (base heating &amp; high Mach aeroheating), venting, Lift-off acoustics</p>
<p>Test philosophy since the beginning is to include best known Block 1B configurations when reasonable</p>
<p>Structural Tests</p>
<p>Philosophy is to test to known configurations of Block 1B loads</p>
<p>Select structures tested to best estimate of Block 2 loads (e.g. intertank – thrust carry through)</p>
<p>Block 1 specific structures tested to FoS 1.4 or greater (e.g. ICPS)</p>
<p>When appropriate, Structural tests to failure (e.g. LH2, intertank, CS engine section, LO2)</p>
<p>Qualification Testing</p>
<p>Vibration test qualification scaled to max q trajectory enclosure of all B1 and B1B vehicles </p>
<p>Block 2 trajectory currently limited to vibroacoustics design trajectory for B1</p>
<p>Select components and structures are known to need requal for Block 1B</p>
<p>IAS testing – covered in IAS portion of SE&amp;I discussion</p>

<div class="slide-notes"><p>From wind tunnel to flight hardware, testing is a big part of making SLS safe for astronauts. </p>
<p>Before the vehicle can fly, its components will have been structurally tested to ensure they can survive the extreme pressures and loads experienced in flight. </p>
<p>SLS structural qualification testing at Marshall complete, largest test campaign at MSFC since Space Shuttle.  Five structural test articles underwent 199 separate test cases, and more than 421 gigabytes of data were collected to add to computer models used to design the rocket and optimize it in the future.</p>
<p>Software is tested in a flight-like test environment, and then again on the flight hardware to ensure everything functions as needed to send crew to space. </p>
<p>RS-25 engines adaptation hotfire series complete: 2015 – 2019 at Stennis to adapt the RS-25 to SLS performance and environment requirements and develop a modern engine controller and software. Tests also included new engine flight controller development and flight controller qualification as well as development parts for restart engine program.  Total of 32 tests totaling nearly 15,000 seconds of hot-fire time. </p>
<p>Eight-test Green Run series at Stennis ending with a simulated launch hot fire test will prove Core Stage 1 is ready for Artemis I. </p>
<p>The 5-segment boosters for SLS underwent both hot and cold firings early in the program. A Flight Support Booster will be test fired in September to qualify a new propellant vendor for future flights.</p>

<div class="slide-notes"><p>Green Run Video at Stennis Space Center – 30 seconds, no sound</p>
<p></p>
<p>A series of 8 tests in 2020 and early 2021 were conducted to fully test the Artemis I core stage in Stennis Space Center in Mississippi. All eight major Green Run tests have been completed, with a 67-second hot fire test occurring on Jan. 16, 2021, and a full-duration eight-minute (499.6 seconds) hot fire on Thursday, Mar. 18. All four engines ignited successfully and produced 1.6 million pounds of thrust.</p>
<p></p>
<p>Green Run included several first-time events for the world’s largest rocket stage: </p>
<p>•	First propellant fill-up for the LOX and LH2 tanks</p>
<p>•	First end-to-end flow test of propellants and other fluids through the complete stage</p>
<p>•	First operational test of FLIGHT avionics</p>
<p>•	First firing of all four RS-25 engines together</p>
<p></p>
<p>In addition to the hot fire, other tests as part of Green Run included vibration testing, leak and functional checks, hydraulics and thrust vector control, safing, simulated countdown and wet dress rehearsal during which propellants were flowed through the stage but not ignited. </p>
<p></p>
<p>When Green Run was complete and the core stage was verified, the core stage was sent to Kennedy. There, all the disparate elements of Artemis I were put together, culminating in a launch!</p>

<div class="slide-notes"><p>The WDR test was the last major test before the Artemis I launch. In a typical WDR, SLS will be fully fueled with over 730,000 gallons of cryogenic propellant in the core stage and ICPS, followed by a countdown, recycle, and de-tanking of the cryogenic propellant. </p>
<p>WDR allowed NASA to fuel and fully test, short of igniting the engines and boosters, the integrated SLS, KSC launch facilities, and Eastern Range through countdown. WDR also provided NASA with the opportunity to measure structural stress on the rocket during tanking and exposure to wind.</p>
<p>WDR also gave the teams an opportunity to practice operational procedures prior to launch, including loading propellant into the rocket’s tanks, conducting a full launch countdown, demonstrating the ability to recycle the countdown clock, and draining the tanks to practice the timelines and procedures the team will use for launch.</p>

<div class="slide-notes"><p>This is the Artemis I mission map.  </p>
<p></p>
<p>After launching from the Kennedy Space Center, Orion orbited Earth to complete system check-outs.</p>
<p></p>
<p>Followed by a 18-minute trans-lunar injection burn to set us on the path to the Moon.  </p>
<p></p>
<p>Along the way, Orion separated from the upper stage and deployed several secondary payloads to study the Moon and radiation environments and conduct technology demonstrations. </p>
<p></p>
<p>Once at the Moon, we came in close for a lunar fly-by, around 80.6 nautical miles off the surface, before heading out to distant retrograde orbit, 38,000 nautical miles beyond the far side of the Moon...farther than any human-rated spacecraft has ever traveled.</p>
<p></p>
<p>Over the course of the mission: Orion traveled over 268,000 miles from Earth, stayed in space longer than any human spacecraft has without docking to a space station, and returned home faster and hotter than ever before.</p>
<p></p>
<p>Orion left the distant retrograde orbit and performed return trajectory correction burns as necessary to aim for Earth, before entering the atmosphere and splashing down in the Pacific Ocean, where it was recovered by a U.S. interagency team, made up of Navy, Air Force, and NASA engineers and technicians.</p>

<div class="slide-notes"><p>The primary goal of the Artemis I mission was to test Orion’s heatshield and ensure it is safe for astronauts returning from the Moon.  But there were other objectives.</p>
<p>We needed to:</p>
<p>Learn how Orion flies in space, especially in the deep space and lunar-vicinity environments</p>

<p>And, most relevant to us – we needed to test the Space Launch System.</p>

<p>It was our job - SLS’s job - to give it that energy.</p>

<div class="slide-content"><p>ARTEMIS I FLIGHT</p>
<p>Nov. 16, 2022</p>
<p>The solid rocket boosters provided just over nominal performance to separation.</p>
<p>The core stage inserted ICPS and Orion into an initial Earth orbit at a velocity of 25,579.86 ft./sec – 6.58 ft./sec off nominal.</p>
<p>Orbital insertion velocity was 2.9 miles shy of the nominal target.</p>
<p>Nominal separation of Orion occurred following the TLI burn.</p>
<p>Nominal performance of the boosters, core stage, ICPS, and flight software.</p>
<p>POST-FLIGHT DATA INDICATES THAT SLS IS READY TO SUPPORT A CREWED  FLIGHT ON ARTEMIS II.</p>
<div class="embedded" id="slide14_rId3"></div>
<p>Bottom Line: This machine did the job we designed it to do.</p>
</div>
<div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-notes"><p>We are still assessing flight data, but preliminary post-flight data indicate that all SLS systems performed exceptionally and that the SLS rocket is ready to support crewed flight on Artemis II.</p>
<p></p>
<p>Booster Performance Statistics</p>
<p>Burned out within 0.4 seconds of each other (action time)</p>
<p>Hit peak thrust within 0.1 seconds of each other</p>
<p>Performed within one-quarter of a percent of each other during ascent</p>
<p>The 50-psi separation que was within .04 seconds for all pressure gauges (3 each) for both boosters</p>
<p></p>
<p>The core stage was the big new development in the SLS Program, and it performed exceptionally well.</p>
<p>The SLS core stage’s 999 sensors and 45 miles of cable executed all functions</p>
<p></p>
<p>The RS-25s performed magnificently.  Here are some statistics from launch:</p>
<p>- RS-25 engines’ thrust and mixture ratio control valves were within 0.5% of pre-flight predicted values</p>
<p>- Internal pressures and temperatures were within 2% of pre-flight predicted values</p>
<p></p>
<p>The ICPS, using its RL10 engine, gave Orion the final shove it needed to go out to the Moon.</p>
<p>Extremely precise injection accuracy</p>
<p>Three burns first raised Orion and ICPS’s perigee, then performed trans-lunar injection, and the final burn was the heliocentric disposal burn</p>
<p>During the burn, the RL10 set a record for the longest duration burn of that engine at approximately 18 minutes.</p>
<p></p>
<p>The flight software of the rocket is really the unsung hero of the entire system.  It doesn’t produce smoke and fire, but without it, nothing happens.  Some statistics from the flight software:</p>
<p>Overall performance was basically down the middle of predictions</p>
<p>Transition from GLS to ALS was smooth, and all ALS functions were performed without issue</p>
<p>Mission execution was nominal</p>
<p>No avionic hardware issues during flight</p>
<p>Excellent core stage LH2 and LOX closed loop ullage control</p>
<p>No trigger level “close calls” in the abort monitor system</p>
