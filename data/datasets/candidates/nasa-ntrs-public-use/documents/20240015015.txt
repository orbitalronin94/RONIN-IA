<p>DECISION AID FOR CONJUNCTION RISK MITIGATION BY
DIFFERENTIAL DRAG
</p>
<p>Dr. James R. Clark* and Dr. Dolan E. Highsmith†
</p>
<p>The NASA Conjunction Assessment Risk Analysis (CARA) team has conducted a
review of the historical record of high-risk conjunctions involving CARA-protected
missions and evaluated whether (and how much) differential drag can provide ef-
fective mitigation. From this analysis, CARA has identified a rule of thumb: mis-
sions with perigees below 560 km capable of tripling their drag area for 72 hours
(or a larger scale factor for a lesser amount of time) can reliably use differential
drag to remediate high-risk events.
</p>
<p>INTRODUCTION
</p>
<p>In the previous five years, the number of conjunctions that the NASA Conjunction Assessment
Risk Analysis (CARA) team processed and analyzed has more than tripled.1 New missions in the
early development phases are now required to plan for mitigating conjunctions under the present
space environment, and also projecting forward into a future likely having even further increased
utilization of the space environment, thereby causing a higher number of events. Some missions are
investigating the possibility of using differential drag to remediate conjunctions because propulsive
capabilities are not required. The NASA CARA team studied the historical record of conjunctions
to evaluate the circumstances under which differential drag could have been successfully applied to
mitigate those conjunctions and has developed a series of tables to use as a decision aid for missions
considering whether differential drag would be an acceptable option for their operations concept.
</p>
<p>Currently, if a CARA-protected mission with maneuvering capabilities is predicted to have a
conjunction with a probability of collision (Pc) ≥ 7×10−5 (the maneuver planning threshold) within
three days of the time of closest approach (TCA), CARA will analyze and recommend options for
the timing and magnitude of a risk mitigation maneuver (RMM) based on the mission capabilities. If
the Pc is greater than 1×10−4 (the maneuver threshold) at the maneuver commit point, then CARA
recommends performing an RMM per the threshold specified in NASA Procedural Requirements
(NPR) 8079.1.2
</p>
<p>The magnitude of the maneuver is typically a few centimeters per second (cm/s), and CARA
estimates how many will be required over the mission’s nominal lifetime – typically a few per year
– during the pre-launch planning process, to inform the mission’s ∆V requirement. For traditional
satellites, this ∆V requirement is usually smaller than other requirements for orbit insertion, main-
tenance, and disposal, but for CubeSats or other small satellite missions, a propulsion system may
not provide much more than a few cm/s of ∆V, or may not fit at all within the available budget
</p>
<p>*Project Engineer, The Aerospace Corporation
†Senior Project Leader, The Aerospace Corporation; Chief Engineer, NASA CARA
</p>
<p>1</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>of funding, time, size, weight, and/or power. Conversely, CubeSats often have deployable solar
panels. Such a mission can instead use “differential drag” to remediate a conjunction – in other
words, change the spacecraft drag area (usually increasing) by adjusting the spacecraft attitude to
put the larger solar array area in the velocity direction, thereby increasing drag and changing the
decay rate of the spacecraft. This in turn changes the timing of the close approach. With enough
advance notice, this method can apply small amounts of differential drag (larger than the nominal)
to deviate from the predicted collision course. Differential drag is used by Planet’s Dove space-
craft to maintain their formations and remediate conjunction risks since they do not have on-board
propulsion.3, 4 CARA has a Maneuver Trade Space (MTS) tool that considers how variations in
mitigation maneuver timing and magnitude affect the resulting Pc, and differential drag is one of
the options that can be used within the tool.
</p>
<p>METHODS
</p>
<p>CARA has archived records of all conjunctions experienced by their protected payloads since
inception in January 2005. From this archive, approximately 7,300 events had a Pc greater than
1 in 10,000, the nominal threshold for executing an RMM, at three days prior to TCA. Of these,
approximately 4,300 had fully-defined covariance matrices for both the primary (i.e., CARA) and
secondary objects; this set of conjunctions is the basis of the analysis in this work to determine the
efficacy of differential drag applied at three days prior to TCA.
</p>
<p>In differential drag mode, MTS models a change in the ballistic coefficient (Bc) of the primary
satellite beginning at some time prior to TCA to evaluate the change in the conjunction at TCA
due to the integrated effect of the change in atmospheric drag resulting from the change in atti-
tude/effective drag area. In this study, MTS was used to propagate the primary satellite forward
beginning at TCA − 3 days using various increases in Bc. Because these conjunctions came from
multiple missions, the increase in Bc was governed by a non-dimensional parameter ∆-Bc defined
in Equation 1:
</p>
<p>∆-Bc =
Bcnew
Bcold
</p>
<p>− 1 (1)
</p>
<p>Expressed this way, a ∆-Bc of 0 represents no change, a negative value represents a reduction in
drag (but cannot be less than −1), and a positive value represents an increase in drag. For example,
a ∆-Bc of 3 is an increase in Bc by a factor of four.
</p>
<p>After propagation with the new Bc value, the Pc was recalculated to evaluate whether it was
reduced below the “mitigation” threshold of 3 × 10−6 set forth in NPR 8079.1 for events having
a maneuver threshold of Pc ≥ 1 × 10−4. These results were then binned and sorted along several
axes, including altitude, amount of ∆-Bc, and (pre-maneuver) energy dissipation rate (EDR) – the
specific rate of energy loss (W/kg) as estimated from measured orbital decay – to identify underlying
patterns in what percentage of conjunctions were remediated for a given ∆-Bc.
</p>

</p>
<p>Unsurprisingly, the efficacy of differential drag is strongly dependent on the altitude of perigee.
The heat map shown in Figure 1 displays the percent of conjunctions remediated for a given increase

altitude bin, indicating sufficient samples at operational altitudes. Clearly, the lower the altitude and

<p>A strong correlation is also observed with EDR, shown in Figure 2 with the heat map showing

EDR &gt; 0, which clearly show the highest remediation efficacy with high initial EDR values and
large Bc changes (upper right corner of heat map), with a distinct boundary at 1E-4 W/kg.
</p>
<p>Unlike binning by altitude and EDR, binning conjunctions by the initial ballistic coefficient of the
primary object presents a weak correlation to remediation efficacy. The Figure 3 heat map shows
the increasing ∆-Bc factors relative to initial Bc in 0.005 m2/kg bins, with the histogram showing
a relatively even distribution of samples across those bins. This is a reasonable result given that
specific Bc values can be found across all altitudes, washing out the efficacy when binning by initial
Bc value.
</p>
<p>Figure 4 shows correlation to remediation efficacy relative to solar activity by using smoothed
values of the F10.7 index in bins of 20 solar flux units (sfu). The histogram shows that most of the
samples come from periods of low to medium solar activity, with very few samples in the highest
bin of 160 – 180 sfu where one would expect the largest sensitivity to changes in drag. The heat map
shows a spike in the top right corner, but that is likely due to a combination of increased sensitivity
to drag and small sample size. This result does not provide conclusive evidence of more than a
weak correlation to solar activity. Future work would expand this analysis to include more recent
conjunctions during the current solar maximum to fill out the upper end of F10.7 range.

<p>Because differential drag efficacy is strongly correlated to altitude, and altitude is one of the
earliest decisions made in the mission design, altitude will be used as the primary variable for the
rest of this analysis. While EDR also shows strong correlation, it is more difficult to compute
accurately for missions in development and is less intuitive than altitude. Nevertheless, if EDR
could be calculated with some confidence, it could also be used to assess differential drag efficacy.
</p>
<p>For the altitude analysis, when the efficacy results are binned in 100-km intervals, there is a
clear break between 500 – 600 km and 600 – 700 km. To identify a reasonable rule of thumb,
we sought the 50% break point by examining the ∆-Bc = 2 column from Figure 1 with finer bins,
shown in Figure 5. Relative to 25 km perigee altitude bins between 500 – 700 km, the left axis of
Figure 5 indicates the percent of conjunctions remediated, while the right axis indicates the number
of conjunctions in each bin. The break point where efficacy falls well below 50% is 560 km – that
is, satellites with perigees at 560 km or below and capable of adopting an average ∆-Bc of 2 or
greater (i.e. multiplying their ballistic coefficient by a factor of three or more) for three days can
use differential drag to effectively remediate high-risk conjunctions.
</p>
<p>However, this is a difficult threshold for a mission to clear; very few spacecraft are capable
of adopting a high-drag configuration for 72 hours continuously unless specifically designed to
continue to accomplish their mission while in the extreme drag attitudes. Planet’s Dove spacecraft
use differential drag to remediate conjunctions,4 and they have a maximum ∆-Bc factor of 9, but
in practice with mission and sun-pointing constraints, they achieve a time-averaged ∆-Bc that is
closer to 2.3
</p>

<p>To explore the efficacy of differential drag under more realistic operational scenarios, we ran
similar analyses for three other scenarios: spacecraft that only adopt the high-drag attitude for a
30% duty cycle over three days (Figure 6), as for example, slewing to high drag during eclipse
periods only; spacecraft that adopt the high-drag attitude for two days prior to TCA (Figure 7); and
spacecraft that adopt the high-drag attitude for one day prior to TCA (Figure 8). These figures use
the same altitude bins as Figure 1, as indicated by the identical histograms. It is not surprising to
see that efficacy is reduced under these circumstances, but it is interesting to compare the 30% duty
cycle and one day cases (Figures 6 and 8). For higher altitudes (perigee above 500 km), it is more
effective to begin lower-intensity remediation earlier, while at lower altitudes, it is more effective to
wait until later and then hold a high-drag attitude continuously.
</p>

<p>Validation
</p>
<p>To validate this analysis, we consulted the record of a NASA mission which uses differential
drag to maintain its orbit (at an altitude of approximately 520 km) and to remediate conjunction
risk, with RMMs typically starting around 48 hours prior to TCA. CARA’s empirical record of the
mission’s orbit history suggests it achieves a ∆-Bc of 2.2. This can most easily be seen at the end of
the mission, plotted in Figure 9 comparing its final months of operations when it maintained tight
attitude control for minimum drag to the following months when it began to tumble.
</p>
<p>Figure 9. Daily (red) and smoothed (blue) ballistic coefficient of a NASA mission.
</p>
<p>Of the 21 RMM slew plans that were submitted by this mission, 17 were matched with 16 con-
junctions in the historical archive (one of the plans was revised and re-submitted). Of those 16
conjunctions, 11 were successfully remediated (final computed Pc &lt; 3× 10−6), and five were not.
This is a rate of 69%, which is higher than the expected efficacy shown in the MTS simulation,
although not to a degree which is statistically significant. Taking into account the small number of
data points, the mission’s empirical results would be consistent with an efficacy between 41% and
89% at the 95% confidence level, while MTS predicted an efficacy of 44% at this mission’s perigee
altitude (520 km), ∆-Bc (2.2), and maneuver duration (48 hr), as shown in Figure 10.
</p>
<p>All the submitted maneuvers were then simulated with MTS, to compare its predictions against
the reality that ensued. An example of this simulation is shown in Figure 11 with the actual Pc
history starting seven days prior to TCA and the MTS-predicted post-maneuver Pc shown as a
single red “x”. MTS simulated the proposed maneuver, starting from the last received state prior
to the maneuver plan’s submission. This would have been the latest information available to the
mission at the time. In this case, MTS predicted that the maneuver would successfully remediate
the conjunction to a Pc of approximately 10−8, and following the timeline of observed Pc values
forward, the actual Pc rapidly dropped below 10−20 after the maneuver was included in the predicted

of the conjunction were independently changing with time, but the maneuver was undoubtedly a

<p>Positive or negative ∆-Bc
</p>
<p>The analysis herein has evaluated the effectiveness of differential drag assuming the effect is
accomplished with an increase in ballistic coefficient. For most conjunctions, “more is more” as far
as ∆-Bc goes, as illustrated in Figure 12, which shows the Pc of a historical conjunction predicted
with MTS decreasing with increasing ∆-Bc starting at a value of -1 (unlike the heat map plots above
which start at +1). For reference, the actual Pc is also shown at ∆-Bc = 0.
</p>
<p>However, there are some conjunctions for which “less is more”, as depicted in Figure 13 (Pc
decreases with decreasing ∆-Bc), or even where a small amount of positive ∆-Bc is worse than
either a large amount or doing nothing, as in Figure 14 (local peak in Pc with mild increase in ∆-
Bc). Comparing the Pc scale in these two figures to Figure 12 shows much less sensitivity to Bc
change, but the trends are still illuminating.

<p>These effects are most strongly influenced by the direction of the relative separation of the pri-
mary and secondary, as shown in Figure 15. The figure depicts the more effective change in Bc
(increase or decrease) based on predicted relative radial, in-track, and cross-track position at TCA.
Given the choice of having a ∆-Bc of positive or negative 0.9, if the secondary is passing ahead of
the primary (positive in-track separation), then it is better to reduce drag, maintain a higher altitude
and longer orbital period in comparison to the no-maneuver case, and allow the secondary to pull
further ahead. If the secondary is passing behind (negative in-track separation), it is better for the
primary to increase drag, reduce its orbital altitude and period, and increase its lead.
</p>
<p>Figure 15. 3-D scatter plot of maneuvers for which positive or negative ∆-Bc is more effective.
</p>
<p>Note that “more effective” is relative – neither positive nor negative 0.9 is a particularly effective
∆-Bc value. In general, it is better for a spacecraft to stay in a low-drag configuration and transition
to high-drag for a maneuver. By definition, ∆-Bc could only be as low as −1 if a spacecraft could
present zero drag area, while positive ∆-Bc is theoretically unbounded. However, if a mission with
limited ∆-Bc capability had to use differential drag anyway – perhaps one that was designed to use
propulsion, but has suffered a propulsion system failure and is on course for a high-interest event –
this kind of analysis might be needed to make the most of its capability.
</p>
<p>Example application
</p>
<p>During the course of this work, a NASA mission in development reached out to CARA to evaluate
the utility of differential drag to remediate potential conjunctions. The spacecraft orbit has yet to
be finalized, but will likely be a circular orbit between 500 and 700 km, and it will not have a
propulsion system. It will have large deployable solar panels and is capable of a ∆-Bc of greater
than 10, but only during eclipse periods. If the spacecraft were otherwise remaining in the low-drag
attitude during the rest of each orbit period, then the mission could realize the full factor of 10 ∆-Bc
during eclipse. Assuming eclipse periods result in a 30% duty cycle, a factor of 10 ∆-Bc at that duty
</p>
<p>14</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>cycle can be approximated to first order as a 100% duty cycle at a ∆-Bc of 3. Referring to Figure 1
for a three-day maneuver duration, the corresponding efficacy at ∆-Bc=3 for 500-600 km is 56%,
but drops to only 16% for the 600-700 km bin. Thus, differential drag would be effective (under
these operational assumptions) for remediating conjunctions in the lower half of the target altitude
range for this mission assuming the slewing activity starts three days prior to TCA. Examination of
Figures 7 and 8 indicate that shorter maneuver durations would not surpass 50% efficacy for that
level of Bc change.
</p>
<p>This mission, however, does not plan to maintain the low-drag attitude during the entire orbit
period. In fact, due to charging and imaging activities during every orbit, the spacecraft must fre-
quently slew to different, higher-drag attitudes. Based on that concept of operations, the actual
maximum ∆-Bc that the spacecraft can achieve over the nominal orbit-averaged Bc is just 0.68,
which is not enough to meaningfully remediate conjunctions using differential drag (referring to
Figure 1, the efficacy would be worse than the ∆-Bc=1 column). Thus, CARA did not recommend
implementing differential drag as a means to remediate conjunctions for this mission considering
the given operational constraints.
</p>
<p>CONCLUSION
</p>
<p>CARA has developed tables that can be used as decision aids to advise missions in development
about the best way to utilize their differential drag capabilities. For missions below 560 km with the
operational flexibility to devote multiple days to holding a high-drag configuration (or a sufficiently
high drag ratio to compensate for limitations on that time), differential drag can be used successfully
to remediate many high-risk conjunctions. Conversely, missions that do not meet these exacting
criteria – most missions – should instead be advised to investigate other options for conjunction
mitigation, such as adding an onboard propulsion system or simply slewing to a minimum area
configuration during TCA.
</p>
<p>Further analysis is required in several areas to refine the guidance on differential drag efficacy for
conjunction remediation. As mentioned above, additional test cases during solar maximum would
increase the resolution of the efficacy to higher levels of solar activity, providing insight into the sen-
sitivity of this method of conjunction remediation to the solar cycle. In addition, this study considers
a conjunction remediated if the Pc falls to 3 × 10−6 or below, but increased efficacy would clearly
be achieved if the remediation threshold were not as stringent. Future analyses could investigate
the sensitivity of the percentage of conjunctions remediated to higher thresholds (e.g., 1 × 10−5)
or to a target reduction from the starting Pc value (e.g., one order of magnitude reduction). Lastly,
consideration must be given to the acceptable level of efficacy for differential drag remediation to
be recommended for a mission. The analysis in this paper targets a relatively low 50% efficacy, as-
suming that anything less than that would not be worthwhile for a mission to implement. However,
from both a mission protection and a space environment protection standpoint, a higher threshold
for efficacy may be warranted given the ever increasing number of conjunctions in low earth orbit.
</p>
