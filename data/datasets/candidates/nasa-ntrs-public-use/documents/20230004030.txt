<p>  PASSIVE CONTROLS OF TIP CLEARANCE FLOW IN A TRANSONIC COMPRESSOR 
FOR STALL MARGIN IMPROVEMENT: COMMON FLOW PHYSICS  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Chunill Hah 
NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>ABSTRACT 
Two passive tip clearance control devices for stall margin 
</p>
<p>improvement in a transonic compressor are compared in detail. 
</p>
<p>The primary objective of the current study is to examine the 
</p>
<p>overall performance in terms of stall margin increase and any 
</p>
<p>associated performance penalties near design operation with 
</p>
<p>axial casing grooves (ACGs) and circumferential casing grooves 
</p>
<p>(CCGs). The underlying flow physics of these passive flow 
</p>
<p>control devices are examined and compared to identify any 
</p>
<p>relative advantages in the development of optimized casing 
</p>
<p>treatment designs.    
</p>
<p>To understand the underlying flow mechanisms of ACGs and 
</p>
<p>CCGs, available measured data and both steady and unsteady 
</p>
<p>flow simulations from Large Eddy Simulations are used. The 
</p>
<p>measured data illustrate changes in the overall time-averaged 
</p>
<p>performance and provide very useful snap shots of the flow fields 
</p>
<p>at specific times. The simulated steady (CCGs) and unsteady 
</p>
<p>(ACGs) flow fields from LES show changes in the entire flow 
</p>
<p>structure, which reveal the underlying flow physics.  The current 
</p>
<p>LES captures measured changes in the flow field due to the 
</p>
<p>applied casing treatments well. Detailed comparisons of the 
</p>
<p>calculated flow fields with and without CCGs or ACGs show that  
</p>
<p>flow blockage near the casing is reduced by both treatments for 
</p>
<p>near-stall operation. This reduction of blockage generation 
</p>
<p>extends the compressor stall margin. Therefore, the root fluid 
</p>
<p>mechanism of stall margin increase by these casing treatments 
</p>
<p>must be the underlying method of reduction of blockage 
</p>
<p>generation. 
</p>
<p> Tip leakage flow occurs due to higher pressure on the 
</p>
<p>pressure side of the blade than on the suction side at the blade 
</p>
<p>tip. The tip leakage flow moves counter to and collides with the 
</p>
<p>incoming main flow, creating a tip leakage vortex (TLV). The 
</p>
<p>formation of the TLV due to this collision is the main source of 
</p>
<p>flow blockage generation. The unsteady flow fields from LES 
</p>
<p>near the casing with ACGs show that flow blockage in the tip 
</p>
<p>region is periodically reduced when the blade passes under the 
</p>
<p>ACGs. Detailed examination of the flows near the casing 
</p>
<p>indicates that effective tip leakage flow below the casing is 
</p>
<p>reduced by about 26% as some of the tip leakage flow moves into 
</p>
<p>the ACGs. Most of the flow into the current ACGs is reinjected 
</p>
<p>into the main passage upstream of the leading edge. The reduced 
</p>
<p>effective tip leakage flow colliding with the incoming main flow 
</p>
<p>produces a much smaller TLV with ACGs. This is the main 
</p>
<p>mechanism for the blockage reduction with ACGs.  
</p>
<p>With CCGs, part of the tip leakage flow moves into the rear 
</p>
<p>portion of the grooves and is reinjected into the main flow near 
</p>
<p>the front side of the grooves. The radially reinjected flow from 
</p>
<p>CCGs blocks upstream movement of tip leakage flow below the 
</p>
<p>casing, resulting in roughly 16% less effective tip clearance flow 
</p>
<p>below the casing. Near stall operation, a much smaller TLV is 
</p>
<p>formed with ACGs as compared to CCGs due to a larger 
</p>
<p>reduction of effective tip leakage flow. With the smaller blockage, 
</p>
<p>the overall incoming axial velocity is smaller for the given flow 
</p>
<p>rate and larger pressure rise is obtained with increased inlet flow 
</p>
<p>angle.   
</p>
<p> The increase in stall margin by ACGs and CCGs is due to 
</p>
<p>the reduction of blockage generation. The reduction of the 
</p>
<p>blockage generation is due to the decrease of the effective tip 
</p>
<p>leakage flow under the casing by ACGs and CCGs. 
</p>
<p>It has been believed that casing treatments always incur 
</p>
<p>small additional losses near the design condition due to the 
</p>
<p>mixing of the flow exiting the grooves and the main flow, as well 
</p>
<p>as to an increased wetted area due to the grooves. Near design 
</p>
<p>operation, tip leakage flow typically initiates at about 25-30% 
</p>
<p>axial chord downstream of the leading edge. ACGs or CCGs 
</p>
<p>located near the leading edge barely affect tip leakage flow 
</p>
<p>development. Since the current ACGs are located near the 
</p>
<p>leading edge, the tip leakage flow development is not affected by 
</p>
<p>the ACGs. An additional efficiency penalty is incurred by mixing 
</p>
<p>losses near the ACGs. Measurements and LES indicate 1.1% and 
</p>
<p>1.4% efficiency penalties due to ACGs at design condition. 
</p>
<p>CCGs near the trailing edge pull the TLV toward the blade 
</p>
<p>suction side at the design condition, eliminating double leakage 
</p>
<p>flow in the current transonic compressor and resulting in higher 
</p>
<p>efficiency. The measured efficiency penalty due to the CCGs is 
</p>
<p>within the measurement uncertainty and LES shows the same 
</p>
<p>result. An integrated arrangement of ACGs and one or two rows 
</p>
<p>of CCGs toward the trailing edge could eliminate any efficiency 
</p>
<p>penalty near the design condition with a significant increase of 
</p>
<p>stall margin. </p>
<p></p>

<p> 
1. INTRODUCTION 
</p>
<p>Various compressor casing treatments have been 
</p>
<p>investigated over the past 50 years. The primary objective of 
</p>
<p>compressor casing treatments is to increase stall margin without 
</p>
<p>reducing efficiency near design operation. Compressor casing 
</p>
<p>treatments affect the flow near casing, primarily the tip leakage 
</p>
<p>flow. They also, however, affect the flow field over the entire 
</p>
<p>span by altering the flow blockage in the passage.  Some simple 
</p>
<p>forms of casing treatment, like circumferential casing grooves, 
</p>
<p>have been successfully installed in production engines. With the 
</p>
<p>trend of increasing blade loading and decreasing core 
</p>
<p>compressor size, more aggressive compressor casing treatments 
</p>
<p>are being pursued for real engine applications. Integrated designs 
</p>
<p>of compressor blades and casing treatments could be used to 
</p>
<p>achieve higher overall pressure ratios, increased stall margins 
</p>
<p>and greater efficiency over the entire operating range in small-
</p>
<p>core engine environments. 
</p>
<p>Circumferential casing grooves (CCGs) and axial casing 
</p>
<p>grooves (ACGs) have been applied to both subsonic and 
</p>
<p>transonic compressors. Most fan/compressor casing treatments 
</p>
<p>installed in real engines are based on these two types of grooves. 
</p>
<p>Although CCGs and ACGs can substantially extend stall margin 
</p>
<p>(up to 30-40%) when applied properly, the fundamental flow 
</p>
<p>mechanisms are not clearly understood. Consequently, the 
</p>
<p>design criteria are still based on loosely connected experimental 
</p>
<p>data. 
</p>
<p>Earlier studies attempted to understand the flow physics 
</p>
<p>behind stall margin increase with casing treatments through 
</p>
<p>experimental and analytical approaches (for example, Moore et 
</p>
<p>al. [1], Prince et al. [2], Takata and Fujita [3], Paulon and 
</p>
<p>Dehoudt [4], Smith and Cumpsty [5], Lee and Greitzer [6], 
</p>
<p>Crook et al. [7], Muller et al. [8, 9], Houghton and Day. [10], 
</p>
<p>Sakuma et al. [11], and Mileshin et al. [12]). Prince et al. [2] 
</p>
<p>reported performance characteristics with several configurations 
</p>
<p>of ACGs in a subsonic compressor. The experimental 
</p>
<p>investigation of ACGs and CCGs by Takata and Fujita [3] 
</p>
<p>implied that tangential momentum from the groove jets to the 
</p>
<p>mainstream plays an important role in delaying stall inception. 
</p>
<p>While these studies reported significant changes and 
</p>
<p>improvements in the entire compressor flow field with ACGs or 
</p>
<p>CCGs, details of the flow mechanism behind the changes in stall 
</p>
<p>margin and compressor efficiency have not been fully explained. 
</p>
<p>Detailed information about the steady/unsteady flow structure 
</p>
<p>near the casing has not been available.  Crook et al. [7] performed 
</p>
<p>a CFD study that concluded that the removal of blockage from 
</p>
<p>the main stream and the reduction of the core vortex blockage 
</p>
<p>are the main mechanisms of stall margin increase due to casing 
</p>
<p>grooves. The momentum balance analysis of Shabbir and 
</p>
<p>Adamczyk [13] concluded that radial transport of axial 
</p>
<p>momentum in and out of the grooves is the main mechanism. 
</p>
<p>Maher et al. [14] reported a very detailed investigation of 
</p>
<p>advanced ACGs in a modern single-stage high-pressure 
</p>
<p>compressor. The ACG geometry was optimized with unsteady 
</p>
<p>Reynolds-averaged Navier Stokes (URANS) simulation. Their 
</p>
<p>study indicates that their ACGs increase pressure ratio and stall 
</p>
<p>margin across all rotor speeds and operating conditions. Even a 
</p>
<p>small increase in efficiency at the design operation was reported 
</p>
<p>with their ACGs.   
</p>
<p>Although many numerical studies have reported that the 
</p>
<p>current generation of Reynolds-averaged Navier Stokes (RANS) 
</p>
<p>CFD codes can correctly calculate the stall margin extension by 
</p>
<p>CCGs, some recent studies (for example Mileshin et al., [12]) 
</p>
<p>report that the effects of CCGs on compressor stall margin are 
</p>
<p>not correctly predicted by some RANS-based CFD codes. We 
</p>
<p>must understand the fundamental mechanisms of various casing 
</p>
<p>treatments to successfully apply any numerical tools to the 
</p>
<p>optimization of compressor casing treatments. 
</p>
<p>NASA initiated a research program with Johns Hopkins 
</p>
<p>University (JHU) to study compressor tip clearance flow and 
</p>
<p>casing treatments in JHU’s optically index matched compressor 
</p>
<p>test facility. Chen et al. [15] reported the detailed flow structure 
</p>
<p>near the casing and inside ACGs using stereo particle image 
</p>
<p>velocimetry (SPIV). Hah [16], using detailed Large Eddy 
</p>
<p>Simulation (LES), concluded that the stall margin increase with 
</p>
<p>ACGs is due to the periodic reduction of flow blockage 
</p>
<p>generation when the rotor blade passes under an axial groove. He 
</p>
<p>observed that the jet flow exiting an ACG is not the main 
</p>
<p>mechanism for stall delay, although it affects overall 
</p>
<p>performance by modifying flow incidence at the rotor inlet. All 
</p>
<p>previous experimental and analytical studies indicate that 
</p>
<p>effective application of compressor casing treatments reduces 
</p>
<p>blockage generation near stall, which results in delayed stall 
</p>
<p>inception and increase of stall margin.  
</p>
<p>One of the lessons from the joint NASA/JHU program is 
</p>
<p>that both advanced flow measurement and CFD are 
</p>
<p>complementary and should be applied together to investigate 
</p>
<p>complex flow phenomena in modern compressors. Advanced 
</p>
<p>measurement techniques like SPIV can provide an accurate state 
</p>
<p>of the flow in a certain area at a given moment. However, CFD 
</p>
<p>can give a picture of the entire unsteady flow field if executed 
</p>
<p>properly. 
</p>
<p>Previous experimental and numerical studies on various 
</p>
<p>casing treatments have shown that decreased flow blockage near 
</p>
<p>the casing delays stall inception in modern tip-loaded 
</p>
<p>compressors. However, the flow mechanisms behind the 
</p>
<p>decrease of the flow blockage for different casing treatments 
</p>
<p>have not been explained. The present work aims to uncover the 
</p>
<p>flow mechanisms behind flow blockage reduction with 
</p>
<p>successful ACGs and CCGs. A clear understanding of the 
</p>
<p>fundamental flow mechanism could guide any design 
</p>
<p>optimization of advanced casing treatments.    
</p>

<p>3. NUMERICAL PROCEDURE 
Large eddy simulation was used in the present study to 
</p>
<p>explain the flow mechanism associated with CCGs and ACGs. 
</p>
<p>Flow fields with CCGs and ACGs were calculated in a single 
</p>
<p>stage configuration. The actual stage has 16 rotor blades and 29 
</p>
<p>stator blades. For LES, the number of stator blades was scaled to 
</p>
<p>32 to save computational resources. Upstream influence of the 
</p>
<p>stator on the rotor performance in the current compressor is 
</p>
<p>believed to be minor since a relatively large space exists between 
</p>
<p>the rotor and the stator. LES was applied to capture unsteady 
</p>
<p>flow mixing of the tip leakage flow, flow from CCGs or ACGs, 
</p>
<p>and the incoming main flow as accurately as possible. 
</p>
<p>Unsteady flow mixing and blockage generation from 
</p>
<p>URANS calculations depend on turbulence closure. With 
</p>
<p>spatially filtered Navier-Stokes equations, the subgrid-scale 
</p>
<p>stress tensor term must be modeled for closure of the governing 
</p>
<p>equations. A Smagorinsky-type eddy-viscosity model was used 
</p>
<p>for the subgrid stress tensor, with the standard dynamic model 
</p>
<p>by Germano et al. [17]. Wall resolutions in the streamwise, 
</p>
<p>pitchwise, and spanwise directions are within the ranges Dx+ &lt; 
</p>
<p>20, Dy+ &lt;5.0, and Dz+ &lt; 20, respectively. Details of the 
</p>
<p>numerical procedures are given by Hah [16]. 
</p>
<p>The computational grid for a single blade passage consists 
</p>
<p>of 394 nodes in the blade-to-blade direction, 280 in the spanwise 
</p>
<p>direction, and 440 in the streamwise direction. For the CCG 
</p>
<p>simulation, each circumferential groove is gridded with 60 and 
</p>
<p>30 nodes in the radial and axial directions. For the ACG </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 4  
</p>
<p>simulation, each axial groove was represented with 200 and 30 
</p>
<p>nodes in axial and radial directions. A sliding mesh arrangement 
</p>
<p>was used to transfer flow properties between the rotor and the 
</p>
<p>stationary ACGs. A buffer zone was created between the rotor 
</p>
<p>grid and the ACGs to transfer flow properties between the two 
</p>
<p>different grid topologies. The inflow boundary is located 6 
</p>
<p>average blade spans upstream of the rotor’s leading edge and the 
</p>
<p>outflow boundary three blade spans downstream of the trailing 
</p>
<p>edge.  Since the present investigation focuses flow fields before 
</p>
<p>stall, one rotor passage and two stator passages with periodic 
</p>
<p>boundary condition were calculated. All the computations were 
</p>
<p>performed with NASA’s supercomputing system. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>4. OVERALL FLOW STRUCTURE 
Calculated rotor performance characteristics with CCGs and 
</p>
<p>ACGs are compared in Fig. 4 along with measured data. 
</p>
<p>Uncertainties of the measurements are 0.2% for mass flow 
</p>
<p>coefficient, 0.6% for total pressure ratio, and 1.5% for isentropic 
</p>
<p>efficiency. For the smooth casing, a slightly higher rise in 
</p>
<p>pressure is calculated than the measured value. The measured 
</p>
<p>stall point with the smooth casing agrees well with the value 
</p>
<p>calculated by LES. In the simulation, the stall mass flow rate was 
</p>
<p>determined by increasing the exit reference pressure by small 
</p>
<p>increments until a stable converged solution could not be 
</p>
<p>obtained. The measurements show that CCGs increase the 
</p>
<p>compressor stall margin by almost 7.3%. At the stall point with 
</p>
<p>the smooth casing (marked as “A” in Fig. 4), the pressure rise 
</p>
<p>with CCGs is about 0.8% higher than without CCGs. When 
</p>
<p>ACGs are installed, the measurements show an increase in stall 
</p>
<p>margin of over 23% and a 4% higher pressure rise at the smooth 
</p>
<p>wall stall condition A. The simulation calculates a slightly higher 
</p>
<p>pressure rise than the measurements at the smooth-casing stall 
</p>
<p>point for both CCGs and ACGs. Changes in measured adiabatic 
</p>
<p>efficiency with the smooth-wall casing are fairly well calculated 
</p>
<p>with LES. 
</p>
<p>The measurements show a slight decrease in the adiabatic 
</p>
<p>compressor efficiency with CCGs near the design condition. 
</p>
<p>However, the difference is within the measurement uncertainty. 
</p>
<p>The LES calculates almost the same adiabatic efficiency at the 
</p>
<p>design condition with the smooth casing and with CCGs. The 
</p>
<p>measurements show the maximum efficiency drops by 1.1% 
</p>
<p>when ACGs are installed. A slightly higher efficiency penalty 
</p>
<p>(-1.4%) was calculated by LES. 
</p>
<p>Hah [18] reported a detailed investigation of the flow field 
</p>
<p>with CCGs in the current compressor. Detailed flow mechanisms 
</p>
<p>for the stall margin increase and change in aerodynamic 
</p>
<p>efficiency at the design condition were presented in the report. 
</p>
<p>In the current paper, flow mechanisms behind the large increase 
</p>
<p>in the stall margin and the pressure rise with the ACGs are first 
</p>
<p>identified with the LES simulations. Then relative differences in 
</p>
<p>flow mechanisms between ACGs and CCGs are compared to 
</p>
<p>identify any relative advantages for future design applications. 
</p>
<p>Calculated flow fields with the smooth casing, with CCGs and 
</p>
<p>ACGs near the stall point with the smooth wall (Condition “A” 
</p>
<p>in Fig. 4) are first examined in detail to identify the flow physics 
</p>
<p>detailing how the stall margin is affected by the two passive flow 
</p>
<p>control devices. Then, calculated flow fields at the design 
</p>
<p>condition (Condition “B” in Fig. 4) are examined in detail to 
</p>
<p>explain how any efficiency penalty near the design condition is 
</p>
<p>generated with CGGs and ACGs.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>5. EFFECTS OF CCGS AND ACGS ON FLOW 
BLOCKAGE AND STALL MARGIN 
Figure 5 compares calculated instantaneous velocity vectors 
</p>
<p>at the rotor tip near stall with the smooth casing, CCGs, and 
</p>
<p>ACGs. Various previous studies on compressor casing 
</p>
<p>treatments have shown that flow blockage near the casing is 
</p>
<p>reduced when a successful casing treatment is applied. The 
</p>
<p>results in Fig. 5 show that the passage is almost blocked near the 
</p>
<p>pressure side of the blade with the smooth casing. The flow is 
</p>
<p>about to spill over into the next blade passage. With CCGs and 
</p>
<p>ACGs, the velocity vectors show that flow blockage does not 
</p>
<p>reach to the pressure side of the blade and significant space is 
</p>
<p>available for extra flow. With the smooth wall, the tip leakage 
</p>
<p>vortex stands almost on the leading-edge plane. With CCGs and 
</p>
<p>ACGs, the tip leakage vortex stays well inside the blade passage 
</p>
<p>and the mass flow rate can be further reduced before stall 
</p>
<p>inception.  
</p>
<p>Mueller et al. [9] reported a detailed flow measurement on 
</p>
<p>the current compressor with smooth casing and CCGs using 
</p>
<p>stereo particle image velocimetry. Figure 6 compares measured 
</p>
<p>relative Mach number distributions from that study for the 
</p>
<p>smooth casing at 95% span and for CCGs at 92.5% span. For the 
</p>
<p>smooth casing at 95% span, two-dimensional particle image 
</p>
<p>velocimetry (PIV) was used. For the CCGs at 92.5% span, stereo 
</p>
<p>PIV was used to measure all three velocity components. In the 
</p>
<p>latter case, the laser sheet was restricted to lie at 92.5% span due 
</p>
<p>to the casing geometry. Details of the system setup, the particles 
</p>
<p>and measurement uncertainties are given by Voges et al. [19]. 
</p>
<p>Flow blockage at these locations is indicated by isolines of Mach 
</p>
<p>number (Ma) = 0.8. Figure 6 clearly shows that flow blockage is 
</p>
<p>significantly reduced when CCGs are installed on the casing.  
</p>
<p>Calculated relative Mach number distributions for the same 
</p>
<p>cases as in Fig. 6, as well as with ACGs at 92.5% span, are 
</p>
<p>compared in Fig. 7. The measured flow blockages for the smooth 
</p>
<p>casing and with CCGs are well reproduced by LES (again, 
</p>
<p>indicated by isolines of Ma = 0.8) in Fig. 7. The LES calculates 
</p>
<p>an even greater reduction in flow blockage with ACGs than with 
</p>
<p>CCGs. Previous studies on compressor casing treatments (for 
</p>
<p>example, Shabbir and Adamczyk [13], Hah [16]) have shown 
</p>
<p>that flow blockage generation near the rotor tip is reduced when 
</p>
<p>a successful casing treatment is applied. Therefore, the driving 
</p>
<p>flow mechanism for compressor casing treatment effectiveness 
</p>
<p>is a flow mechanism that changes the generation of flow 
</p>
<p>blockage near the rotor tip.</p>

6. CHANGES IN FLOW BLOCKAGE GENERATION 
</p>
<p>NEAR THE CASING WITH CCGS AND ACGS 
In the following, the simulated flow fields will be inspected 
</p>
<p>at selected locations. Two of these locations are illustrated in Fig. 
</p>
<p>8, a radially inward view of the compressor blade row. The lines 
</p>
<p>denoted 1 and 2 in Fig. 8 represent meridional planes extending 
</p>
<p>in the streamwise and radial directions. Plane 1 is located at 33% 
</p>
<p>axial chord downstream from the leading edge where the tip 
</p>
<p>leakage vortex has already developed near stall (condition A in 
</p>
<p>Fig. 4). As shown in Fig. 8, the blade tip and front side of the 
</p>
<p>second CCG intersect at this location. Also, the overlap of the 
</p>
<p>ACG and the blade ends at this location.  Plane 2 is located at the 
</p>
<p>mid-chord of the blade tip. As shown in Fig. 8, the blade tip and 
</p>
<p>the third CCG intersect near this location. At the design 
</p>
<p>condition, the tip leakage vortex start to develop between planes 
</p>
<p>1 and 2 and the first and the second CCGs have no interaction 
</p>
<p>with the tip leakage vortex. Plane 2 is located just downstream 
</p>
<p>of the ACGs. 
</p>
<p>Figure 9 shows time-averaged velocity vectors and vorticity 
</p>
<p>on Plane 1 near stall for the smooth casing. Only every third 
</p>
<p>vector is shown for clarity. Tip leakage flow arises from the 
</p>
<p>pressure difference across the blade tip. This tip leakage flow, 
</p>
<p>which travels against the incoming main flow, collides with the 
</p>
<p>incoming main flow creating a TLV and subsequent flow 
</p>
<p>blockage generation. The size of the flow blockage generation 
</p>
<p>from this collision between the incoming main flow and the tip 
</p>
<p>leakage flow depends directly on the size of the tip leakage flow 
</p>
<p>and its axial momentum before it collides with the incoming 
</p>
<p>main flow. The center of the tip leakage vortex is located away 
</p>
<p>from the blade suction side and radially below the rotor tip 
</p>
<p>section. Instantaneous flow structures from PIV measurements 
</p>
<p>and LES simulations (Hah [20]) have shown that the tip leakage 
</p>
<p>vortex is not fixed in space and oscillates constantly, resulting in 
</p>
<p>further blockage generation. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

</p>

<p>When CCGs are employed, the flow structure in the tip 
</p>
<p>region changes drastically. Figure 10 shows time-averaged 
</p>
<p>velocity vectors and vorticity on Plane 1 with CCGs. Above the 
</p>
<p>rotor blade tip section, part of the tip leakage flow moves up into 
</p>
<p>the first groove and is later injected back into the main flow. This 
</p>
<p>reduces the total tip leakage flow below the casing before it 
</p>
<p>collides with the incoming main flow and deflects the tip leakage 
</p>
<p>flow towards the hub. Consequently, a much smaller TLV with 
</p>
<p>less flow blockage forms closer to the blade suction side and 
</p>
<p>closer to the casing compared to the smooth casing case. The 
</p>
<p>radially inward jet emerging from the first groove blocks tip 
</p>
<p>leakage flow below the casing from going further upstream. This 
</p>
<p>interaction reduces the overall tip leakage flow below the casing. 
</p>
<p>This smaller tip leakage flow interacts with the incoming main 
</p>
<p>flow, creating a smaller area of low momentum fluid and less 
</p>
<p>blockage generation.

7. CHANGE IN MASS FLOW THROUGH TIP GAP DUE 
</p>
<p>TO CCGS AND ACGS 
Axial distributions of calculated time-averaged tip leakage 
</p>
<p>mass flow rates below the casing near stall (condition “A” in Fig. 
</p>
<p>4) are compared in Fig. 16. The axial locations of the CCGs and 
</p>
<p>ACGs are also indicated in Fig. 16. The total tip leakage mass 
</p>
<p>flow below the casing is reduced by 16% with CCGs  and by 
</p>
<p>26% with ACGs before colliding with the incoming main flow. 
</p>
<p>With CCGs, the reduction of the tip gap mass flow rate occurs in 
</p>
<p>the area directly below the CCGs. Figure 16 shows that reduction 
</p>
<p>of the tip gap mass flow rate occurs below each of the six CCGs. 
</p>
<p>For the ACGs, a large reduction in tip gap mass flow occurs near 
</p>
<p>the leading edge where the ACG overlaps with the blade (33% 
</p>
<p>of axial chord). With ACGs, the overall level of tip leakage flow 
</p>
<p>is lower across the blade passage except near the trailing edge.  
</p>
<p>Copenhaver et al. [21] showed that in a swept transonic axial 
</p>
<p>compressor the stall margin can increase by about 10% when the 
</p>
<p>tip gap is decreased by 0.27% of the tip axial chord. Reduction 
</p>
<p>of tip gap mass flow rate by CCGs or ACGs influences the 
</p>
<p>compressor stall margin in a way similar to reducing tip gap size 
</p>
<p>with a smooth casing. 
</p>
<p> 
FIGURE 16: COMPARISON OF TIP LEAKAGE MASS 
</p>
<p>FLOW RATE BELOW CASING, NEAR STALL 
 
</p>
<p>Velocity vectors in Fig. 10 indicate a strong radial velocity 
</p>
<p>component in the flow reinjected from the CCGs into the main 
</p>
<p>passage, blocking the tip gap flow from moving further 
</p>
<p>upstream. This effect contributes to the reduction of tip gap mass 
</p>
<p>flow rate near the CCGs. On the other hand, with ACGs, a large 
</p>
<p>portion of the tip leakage flow enters the rear section of the 
</p>
<p>ACGs and is carried to the front of the ACGs before being 
</p>
<p>reinjected into the main-stream well ahead the blade passage, as 
</p>
<p>shown in Fig. 12. With ACGs, low momentum tip leakage flow 
</p>
<p>moves out of the blade passage, which results in a larger 
</p>
<p>reduction of flow blockage compared to CCGs. 
</p>
<p>The flow field near the compressor casing with CCGs or 
</p>
<p>ACGs becomes very complex as reinjected flow interacts with 
</p>
<p>the flow below the casing, and various flow mechanisms are 
</p>
<p>involved. However, the primary driver of the decreased blockage 
</p>
<p>generation in the rotor tip region with CCGs or ACGs is the 
</p>
<p>reduction of total tip leakage mass flow rate under the casing 
</p>
<p>before it collides with the incoming main flow. The primary flow 
</p>
<p>mechanism for the stall margin increase is the same for these two 
</p>
<p>passive flow control devices.  
</p>

<p>8. EFFECTS OF CCGS ON EFFICIENCY AT DESIGN 
CONDITION 
The primary objective of compressor casing treatments is  to 
</p>
<p>extend stall margin without incurring any efficiency penalty at 
</p>
<p>high flow rates near the design condition. It has been thought that 
</p>
<p>all compressor casing treatments incur additional aerodynamic 
</p>
<p>losses near design operation. However, some recent 
</p>
<p>measurements in high-speed axial compressors show that certain 
</p>
<p>CCG designs can even increase compressor efficiency by 1% to 
</p>
<p>2% at the design flow condition (for example, Mueller et al [8]), 
</p>
<p>Mileshin et al [12]). 
</p>
<p>Both the measurements and LES simulations reported in this 
</p>
<p>paper indicate a small adiabatic efficiency penalty due to CCGs 
</p>
<p>for the current compressor at the design condition (-0.5% 
</p>
<p>and -0.3%). Mueller et al. [9] reported a 1.1% efficiency drop </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 11  
</p>
<p>with the current ACGs at the design condition. LES simulation 
</p>
<p>indicates a similar efficiency penalty for the current ACGs 
</p>
<p>(1.4%). The measured efficiency penalty with CCGs is relatively 
</p>
<p>small and appears to be within the measurement uncertainty. 
</p>
<p>However, the measured efficiency penalty with the ACGs is well 
</p>
<p>outside of measurement uncertainty.  
</p>
<p>Figure 17 compares measured and simulated time-averaged 
</p>
<p>static pressure distributions on the casing for the smooth casing 
</p>
<p>case. LES calculates the detailed flow structure very well at the 
</p>
<p>design condition. The TLV starts to develop near 25% chord, and 
</p>
<p>the static pressure in the blade passage starts to increase near 
</p>
<p>40% of the axial chord. Therefore, the first two CCGs do not 
</p>
<p>directly affect the TLV development at the design condition. The 
</p>
<p>overlap of the current ACGs with the blade passage ends at 33% 
</p>
<p>of the axial blade chord at the tip. Consequently, the tip leakage 
</p>
<p>vortex at the design condition   does not interact at all with the 
</p>
<p>ACGs.  
</p>
<p>Figure 18 compares computed time-averaged contours of 
</p>
<p>radial velocity at design condition with CCGs and ACGs at the 
</p>
<p>entrance to the grooves. For the CCGs, areas with large radial 
</p>
<p>velocity are located near the third, and fourth grooves. Similar to 
</p>
<p>the near stall condition in Fig. 11, significant radial movement 
</p>
<p>of tip leakage flow occurs only near the blade surfaces with 
</p>
<p>CCGs. The large radial velocity at the front part of these grooves 
</p>
<p>means that the reinjected flow from these grooves blocks 
</p>
<p>upstream movement of tip leakage flow below the casing, like at 
</p>
<p>the near stall condition. Also like at the near stall condition, the 
</p>
<p>flow blockage generation is decreased and the tip leakage vortex 
</p>
<p>is pulled toward to the blade suction side by the CCGs. For the 
</p>
<p>ACGs, very little radial flow is seen in Fig. 18, which indicates 
</p>
<p>ACGs add additional friction losses by the small amount of flow 
</p>
<p>going through the ACGs. 
</p>
<p>Calculated time-averaged tip leakage mass flow rates below 
</p>
<p>the casing (before colliding with the incoming flow) at the design 
</p>
<p>condition are compared in Fig. 19. The total tip leakage mass 
</p>
<p>flow rate is about the same with the smooth wall and ACGs. The 
</p>
<p>total tip leakage mass flow rate is reduced by about 10% with 
</p>
<p>CCGs. The tip leakage mass flow rate decreases near CCG 
</p>
<p>grooves located toward the blade trailing edge. The reduction of 
</p>
<p>the tip leakage flow with the CCGs results in less blockage 
</p>
<p>generation and pulling of the tip leakage vortex toward the blade 
</p>
<p>suction side.    
</p>
<p>Figure 20 compares computed entropy contours at the rotor 
</p>
<p>tip section at the design condition with smooth casing, CCGs, 
</p>
<p>and ACGs. Additional entropy generation due to double leakage 
</p>
<p>tip clearance flow with the smooth casing is seen in Fig. 20. With 
</p>
<p>CCGs, the TLV is pulled toward the suction side of the blade, 
</p>
<p>eliminating the double leakage tip clearance flow and the 
</p>
<p>associated additional loss. Flow mixing between reinjected flow 
</p>
<p>from the CCGs and the main passage flow creates additional 
</p>
<p>loss. Depending on the blade loading at the design condition, the 
</p>
<p>removal of double leakage tip clearance flow can compensate for 
</p>
<p>any additional mixing losses between the main flow and flow 
</p>
<p>from the CCGs, possibly resulting in an efficiency gain at the 
</p>
<p>design condition due to the use of CCGs. With the ACGs, the 
</p>
<p>development of entropy is very similar to that with CCGs up to 
</p>
<p>about 60% of axial chord. However, the tip leakage vortex spills 
</p>
<p>over into the adjacent blade passage resulting in additional loss 
</p>
<p>with a double leakage tip vortex.

<p>9. CONCLUSION 
The flow mechanisms underlying the stall margin increase 
</p>
<p>caused by two passive control devices (CCGs and ACGs) were 
</p>
<p>studied in detail with measured data and LES simulations. The 
</p>
<p>following are major findings: 
</p>
<p>1. Flow mechanism for stall margin increase with CCGs: 
</p>
<p>In the vicinity of the blade tip surfaces, part of the tip 
</p>
<p>leakage flow moves into the grooves and is later 
</p>
<p>reinjected into the main passage. This reinjection 
</p>
<p>occurs mostly near the front part of the grooves. The 
</p>
<p>reinjected flow blocks upstream movement of tip 
</p>
<p>leakage flow below the casing, reducing the overall tip 
</p>
<p>leakage mass flow rate and total axial momentum 
</p>
<p>below the casing before it collides with the incoming 
</p>
<p>main flow. For the current compressor, the overall tip 
</p>
<p>leakage mass flow rate below the casing is reduced by 
</p>
<p>16% compared to the smooth casing case. This 
</p>
<p>reduction of tip gap mass flow has an effect on the 
</p>
<p>stall margin similar to that from a reduction in tip gap 
</p>
<p>size. The reduction of overall tip gap mass flow below 
</p>
<p>the casing is the primary mechanism by which CCGs 
</p>
<p>decrease blockage generation and increase compressor 
</p>
<p>stall margin. 
</p>
<p>2. Flow mechanism for stall margin increase with ACGs: 
</p>
<p>Near the rear part of ACGs, where the ACGs overlap 
</p>
<p>with the blade passage, part of the tip leakage flow 
</p>
<p>moves into grooves and is later reinjected back into 
</p>
<p>the passage. The reinjection mostly occurs near the 
</p>
<p>front of the grooves which is located well ahead of 
</p>
<p>blade passage. This recirculation of tip leakage flow 
</p>
<p>occurs in all ACGs due to the pressure increase along 
</p>
<p>the blade passage. Therefore, a large portion of the tip 
</p>
<p>leakage flow is removed from the blade passage and 
</p>
<p>reinjected upstream. Like with CCGs, the overall tip 
</p>
<p>leakage flow below the casing is reduced by 26% in 
</p>
<p>the current compressor. This is the main mechanism 
</p>
<p>for the reduction in blockage generation and the stall 
</p>
<p>margin increase. The reduction of flow blockage 
</p>
<p>generation with ACGs is much greater than that with 
</p>
<p>CCGs. With the large reduction of flow blockage, the 
</p>
<p>effective blade passage area increases, resulting in 
</p>
<p>lower axial velocity at the inlet across the entire blade 
</p>
<p>span. This is why total pressure rise is higher with 
</p>
<p>ACGs than with the smooth wall or with CCGs.  
</p>
<p>• The flow mechanisms behind the stall margin increase 
</p>
<p>are the same for CCGs and ACGs. Flow blockage near 
</p>
<p>the compressor casing is mostly generated when the 
</p>
<p>tip leakage flow collides with the incoming main flow 
</p>
<p>near the casing. Both CCGs and ACGs reduce the tip 
</p>
<p>leakage flow below the casing before it collides with 
</p>
<p>the main flow, which is the main flow mechanism of 
</p>
<p>the stall margin increase. 
</p>
<p>• High blade loading at the rotor tip is common in 
</p>
<p>modern compressor designs, and double tip leakage 
</p>
<p>flow is commonly observed. The tip leakage vortex 
</p>
<p>initiates downstream of the blade leading edge (at 
</p>
<p>25-30% axial chord) at the design condition for the 
</p>
<p>current compressor. CCGs located further downstream 
</p>
<p>pull the TLV toward the suction side of the blade. This 
</p>
<p>eliminates double leakage tip clearance flow and the 
</p>
<p>associated efficiency loss. For some compressors this 
</p>
<p>removal of loss generation by the double leakage tip 
</p>
<p>clearance flow can be larger than the inherent 
</p>
<p>additional mixing loss by CCGs. The current ACGs 
</p>
<p>have very limited influence on the development of the 
</p>
<p>tip leakage vortex at design condition since they 
</p>
<p>overhang only the front part of the blade passage. The 
</p>
<p>current ACGs incur an efficiency penalty of around 
</p>
<p>1% at the near-design condition for the current 
</p>
<p>compressor. 
</p>
