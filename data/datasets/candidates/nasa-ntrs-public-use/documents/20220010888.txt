Comparison of the 3 µm Feature from Remote Sensing, MORB Glass, and Synthetic 

Spectra 

To create spectral mixtures with a known total water abundance, we required sample data 

for which reflectance spectra in the near infrared and mid infrared were available and for which 

the total water abundance was determined in a method independent of the 3 µm band. The Mid-

Ocean Ridge Basalt (MORB) glass samples have been used to generate a 3 µm band depth vs. 

total water abundance relationship, and they are a close analog to lunar glasses where significant 

water abundance may reside. Below are comparisons of MORB glass spectra to remote sensing 

datasets to highlight the similar band shape and position. Figure S1 shows comparisons of 

ground-based InfraRed Telescope Facility (IRTF) data from Honniball et al. (Honniball et al., 

2020) with scaled MORB spectra overlain. Figure S2 shows orbital spectroscopic data from the 

M3 spectrometer taken from McCord et al. (McCord et al., 2011) and the EPOXI spectrometer 

(Sunshine et al., 2009) with one of our simulated spectral mixtures overlain. In both cases the 

band minima match up well. This example figure also demonstrates our spectral mixtures 

approximate real remote sensing spectra well. 

 

Figure S1. Comparison of remote sensing data from the Infrared Telescope Facility (IRTF) and 

hydrated MORB spectra. IRTF spectra are from Honniball et al., 2020. 

 



 

 
Figure S2. Comparison of lunar remote sensing data from M3 and EPOXI to synthetic spectrum 

from our simulations. 

Removal of Terrestrial Organic and Water Features from Endmember Apollo Spectra 

 The Apollo soil spectra from the RELAB database all exhibited various absorption 

features between 2.6 and 4 µm including a characteristic 3 µm water absorption feature and 

organic doublets near 3.4 µm, indicating the presence of unquantified hydration, likely from a 

terrestrial source (Figure S3, left). Our goal was to introduce only a known hydration signature to 

compare with the retrieved value, which meant we must first remove this unknown water 

signature from the endmember spectra. We performed a linear fit of the reflectance data for the 

five Apollo samples using anchor points at 2.6 and 4.0 µm, then substituted the linear fit into the 

component spectrum to remove both the water and organic absorption features. Finally, the 

component spectra were interpolated to a uniform 5-nm spectral sampling from 1 to 4 µm 

(Figure S3, right). For reference, the processed mare spectra shown in Figure S3 are comparable 

to spectra of Apollo 17 mare soils heated to 500 °C under high vacuum by Ichimura and co-

authors (Ichimura et al., 2012).  

 

Figure S3. Raw Apollo soil spectra from the RELAB database (left) exhibiting unquantified 

terrestrial water and organic contamination. Processed spectra (right) with water and organic 

spectral features removed and interpolated to uniform spectral sampling. Note these spectra are 



 

still for the laboratory viewing geometry of 30/0/30 (incidence angle/emission angle/phase 

angle). 

Normalization and Interpolation Methods for Mid-Ocean Ridge Basalt (MORB) Glass 

Spectra 

Reflectance data from the step-wise heating experiments of MORB D38A were obtained 

in units of instrument count and required normalization between spectra to isolate the 3 µm band. 

We normalized the four experimental MORB spectra at a wavelength of 2.6 µm, and appended 

the low-wavelength region of sample D38A measured separately to cover the entire lidar 

wavelength range. This process is shown in Figures S4 and S5. 

 

Figure S4. (left) Raw MORB spectra of sample D38A. (right) MORB spectra after normalization 

at 2.6 µm and appending low-wavelength data. 



 

 

Figure S5. Low-wavelength spectrum of MORB glass sample D38A 

We then converted the laboratory reflectance spectra to single scattering albedo using the Hapke 

radiative transfer model described in the main text. The SSA spectra are shown in Figure S6.  

  

Figure S6. Derived single scattering albedo (SSA) for MORB glass spectra using the Hapke 

radiative transfer model. 

We interpolated the data from the four experimental SSA spectra to create MORB spectra 

at 1 ppm total water intervals. For each wavelength, we calculated the ESPAT, defined as (1-⍵)/⍵ 
as a function of total water content. We then performed a linear fit to the experimental data. An 

example for a wavelength of 2.8 µm is shown in Figure S7. By using the linear fits at each 

wavelength, synthetic spectra were generated over the entire wavelength range and total water 

abundance range. Figure 2 in the main text shows a selection of the interpolated MORB spectra. 

We note the linear fit is not perfect, and the slight difference in the experimental data point and 

linear fit at 1522 ppm was the primary source of the mean error in total water retrievals. 



 

   

Figure S7. Linear interpolation method for hydrated MORB spectra. The blue dots are the 

ESPAT values calculated from the step-wise heating SSA spectra. The red line denotes a linear 

fit. 

Synthetic Lunar Glass Spectra 

The synthetic lunar glass spectra (orange and yellow glasses, (Wetzel et al., 

2015)) were retrieved from the RELAB database. Two different datasets were needed to 

cover the 1 to 4 µm spectral range. We stitched the two datasets together by normalizing 

the reflectance values of the low-wavelength data to that of the high-wavelength data at 

1.6 µm via division. Figure S8 shows the combined orange and yellow synthetic lunar 

glass spectra in laboratory reflectance geometry. The difference in band shape can be 

seen here. The orange glass has a band minimum at a slightly longer wavelength than the 

yellow glass and the MORB glass, which is relevant for the retrieval error as discussed in 

the main text.  We then used the Hapke radiative transfer modeling used for the rest of 

the endmember spectra to convert between the RELAB geometry and SSA. The SSAs 

were used in spectral mixtures in the place of the MORB glass and converted into zero-

phase reflectance measurements. 



 

 

Figure S8. Reflectance spectra from the RELAB database of orange (OGV-5) and yellow (YG-5) 

synthetic glasses. 

Band Depth Effects due to Pyroxene 

The effects of pyroxene on the 3 µm band depth are discussed at length in the main text. 

Here we show one example of the effect from the simulations used to create Figure 8. For these 

simulations only, the interpolated MORB spectrum and MORB abundance were fixed at 1000 

ppm and 0.3 respectively. Thus, each mixture here has 300 ppm total water, with only the 

amounts of pyroxene and regolith endmembers being varied. Here the spectra are shown with no 

noise added to the reflectance to clarify the band depth differences. We fit a linear continuum to 

the spectra using anchor points at 2.65 µm and 3.5 µm. The spectra and continua are shown in 

the left-hand side of Figure S9 for a mare mixtures with high pyroxene (31%) and a mare 

mixture with low pyroxene (12%). On the right it can be seen that the high-pyroxene mixture 

shows a deeper band than the low-pyroxene mixture. This highlights the care necessary in 

interpreting spectral band depths in the presence of pyroxene that may affect the continuum and 

affect the overall albedo. 



 

 

Figure S9. Example spectra, both with 300 ppm total water abundance, highlighting the effects 

of pyroxene on the observed band depth. These spectra were 2 of the 2000 spectra used to create 

Figure 8 in the main text, although for Figure 8 random noise was added to simulate the 

measurement SNR of 250. 

Converting Total Water Abundance Error to Band Depth Error 

The maximum standard deviation of total water error for our simulations was 52 ppm for 

the mare dataset. We converted this to a band depth precision to determine how well our 

multispectral lidar might constrain the 3 µm band depth. In the same manner as the pyroxene 

tests, we fixed the total water abundance at 52 ppm and generated 1000 noise-free mare spectra 

to determine the band depth for this abundance. Here we define band depth for the lidar as the 

difference between a linear continuum with anchor points at 2.65 and 3.5 µm and the reflectance 

spectrum at 2.8 µm. This ensures a band depth of zero when no hydration is present. The relative 

band depth is the continuum-removed band depth divided by the reflectance value at 2.65 µm. 

Two example spectra with 52 ppm and the continua for each are shown on the upper left in 

Figure S10. The continuum-removed 3 µm region is shown on the upper left of this figure. A 

histogram of the band depths from the 1000 spectra is shown on the bottom of Figure S10. The 

range in band depths comes from the different pyroxene and regolith abundances in the mixtures. 

The mean band depth value was 0.62%. 



 

 

Figure S10. (Upper left) Two example spectra from the 52 ppm experiments. Black lines denote 

the linear continua used to calculate the band depth. (Upper right) Continuum-removed and 

normalized spectra. (bottom) Histogram of relative band depths (in percent) for the 52 ppm mare 

simulations (1000 spectra total). Each bin is 0.01% wide. The range of band depths comes from 

the difference in albedo of the continuum, which affects the relative band depth.

lunar orbital lidar the background signal contains contributions from solar reflected light (for 

sunlit terrain) as well as thermal photons emitted from the surface. At wavelengths near 3 µm it 

is particularly difficult to discriminate between thermal emitted photons and reflected solar 

photons as their contributions are similar in magnitude for the sunlit moon at low latitudes. As 

the lidar measurement is made using laser photons in a nominally nadir-pointing geometry, with 

the solar reflected and thermally emitted photons acting as noise, this technique has the distinct 

advantage of not requiring precise correction for the surface temperature or phase angle effects. 

In practice, the background photon contribution is removed in post-processing by averaging the 

detected signal between laser pulses. The number of dark counts per second (ndark) is often 

determined experimentally for the detector of interest. The instrument parameters used to
