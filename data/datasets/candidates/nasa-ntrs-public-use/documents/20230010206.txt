<p>BALLISTIC LEVERAGE FOR CONJUNCTION AVOIDANCE 
DURING THE LUNAR TRANSIT TRAJECTORY OF NASA’S CO-
</p>
<p>MANIFESTED VEHICLE 
</p>
<p>Scott N. Karn,* Steven L. McCarty,* Joseph P. Scalora,† Jonathan W. Bro-
gan,‡ Christine L. Schmid,§ Melissa L. McGuire** 
</p>
<p>Analysis is completed to investigate the sensitivity of the Lunar Transit Trajectory 
of NASA’s Co-Manifested Vehicle (CMV) to the insertion of intentional coasting 
into an otherwise nearly continuous low thrust maneuver. A reference trajectory 
is developed which explicitly models low altitude thruster shutdowns in a level of 
detail not included in previous Design Reference Missions. This reference trajec-
tory is used to investigate the sensitivity of the Lunar Transit to additional inserted 
coasting, presented in the form of separation of the perturbed trajectory from the 
reference as a function of mission elapsed time and duration and location of the 
coast segment. These results are shown to inform potential conjunction avoidance 
strategies during the CMV’s long low-thrust spiral from a highly elliptic Earth 
parking orbit to the target Gateway Near Rectilinear Halo Orbit (NRHO). 
</p>
<p>INTRODUCTION 
NASA’s Gateway is envisioned to serve as a crew-tended outpost in cislunar space. The first 
</p>
<p>two elements of the Gateway to be delivered to cislunar space will be the Power and Propulsion 
Element (PPE) and the Habitation and Logistics Outpost (HALO). Prior to launch, these two ele-
ments will be integrated into a single spacecraft known as the Co-Manifested Vehicle (CMV). The 
CMV will be injected into a highly elliptic Medium Earth Orbit (MEO) by a commercial launch 
vehicle, after which a high-power solar electric propulsion (SEP) system will be used to transit the 
vehicle to cislunar space. Previous work has described the development of a low-thrust Lunar 
Transit trajectory by which the CMV will be delivered to a target Near-Rectilinear Halo Orbit 
(NRHO)1.  
</p>
<p>The Lunar Transit trajectory begins in a MEO parking orbit with a perigee altitude of 200 km. 
Due to the low-thrust nature of the mission, the initial acceleration of the CMV remains small, and

</p>
<p>A collision between the CMV and a sizeable foreign object could lead to loss of the Gateway 
mission as well as the generation of a significant debris field in low altitude bands. To protect 
against this risk, the PPE Mission Design (MD) Team, in coordination with the Johnson Space 
Center (JSC) Flight Operations Directorate (FOD) flight dynamics team, has developed a prelimi-
nary collision avoidance strategy for the CMV mission. This strategy is tailored to the unique be-
havior of the Lunar Transit and the capabilities of the CMV. 
</p>
<p>BACKGROUND 
</p>
<p>Orbital Object Environment 
Earth orbit, and particularly Low Earth Orbit (LEO), represents a highly active environment in 
</p>
<p>terms of catalogued orbiting objects. Since the flight of the first artificial satellite, Sputnik in 1957, 
the number of individual tracked objects in Earth orbit has grown to more than 25,000. The rate at 
which new objects find their way into orbit is also increasing, with LEO becoming more and more 
densely inhabited over time. This behavior is clearly seen in Volume 20, Issue 1 of NASA’s Orbital 
Debris Program Office Quarterly News (March 2023)2.  In recent years, the number of operational 
spacecraft in orbit has grown substantially thanks to decreases in launch costs and the rise of small, 
readily available spacecraft such as cubesats. While these factors have resulted in unprecedented 
access to LEO, the current number of objects inhabiting low altitude orbits has also resulted in 
unprecedented risk to spacecraft. 
</p>
<p>Any spacecraft that finds itself orbiting in, or transiting through, low Earth altitudes is faced 
with an object-rich environment. Several thousand objects inhabit orbital altitudes between 200 and 
2,000 km including a mix of operational and non-functional spacecraft, fragmentation debris, and 
rocket bodies. A detailed reporting of object density as a function of altitude can be seen in Figure 
2.1-6 of the 16th edition of the History of on Orbit Satellite Fragmentations (2022)3. The 500 to 
550 km altitude band has grown especially dense over the last three years as the Starlink 
constellation has grown in size. Starlink, currently operating with more than 3,000 individual 
spacecraft, has been approved by the FCC to launch no more than 7,500 satellites to altitudes 
between 525 and 535 km4. This object-rich environment represents a significant risk to transiting 
spacecraft. A single collision with a sizeable object could cause catastrophic loss of the CMV, and 
therefore the Gateway, mission. In addition, NASA would lose the backbone of the Artemis 
program and a central component of the Agency’s Moon to Mars architecture5. 
</p>
<p> 
Lunar Transit Trajectory 
</p>
<p>At the time of writing, the expected CMV launch mass exceeds the direct-to-NRHO delivery 
capability of any currently operational commercial launch vehicle. Instead of a direct delivery, the 
highly efficient SEP system onboard PPE will be used to transit the CMV to cislunar space. The 
baseline low-thrust Lunar Transit spiral trajectory (Figure 1) begins in a 33,700 x 200 km altitude 
parking orbit with an inclination of 28.5°. The trajectory itself is broken into four primary phases 
(Spiral, Alignment, Ballistic, and Insertion), each one unique in its design, optimization, or 
operational strategy. </p>

<p>With current design and performance assumptions, the Lunar Transit trajectory requires a time 
of flight in excess of one year to complete from launch vehicle separation to arrival in the NRHO. 
More than two thirds of this time is spent under thrust, with the SEP system accounting for more 
than 3 km/s of Dv (a more complete overview of the Lunar Transit has been published previously 
by the PPE MD Team1). 
</p>
<p>Due to a high launch mass and the low thrust output of the SEP system, the initial acceleration 
of the CMV is small. This low initial acceleration means that the CMV will maintain a perigee 
altitude below 2,000 km for roughly the first 50 days of the Lunar Transit (Figure 2). Every perigee 
pass below 2,000 km will encounter an object-rich environment, as discussed above. The CMV 
will experience more than 100 of these low perigee passes, with each revolution posing the risk of 
one, or multiple, foreign object collisions. It is thus critical that a robust and repeatable conjunction 
avoidance strategy be developed for the unique CMV mission.   
</p>
<p>The Lunar Transit represents a departure from the typical paradigm of spacecraft operation. 
Traditional missions often involve a spacecraft primarily under coast operating in a stationary 
baseline orbit. Maneuvers, such as orbit maintenance maneuvers (OMMs), are typically short in 
duration (when compared to the period of a single revolution) and often separated from other 
maneuvers by several hours or days. This paradigm also applies to many low-thrust missions (such 
as geocommunications satellites or the Gateway mission), where an electric propulsion system 
completes OMMs in an otherwise coast dominant concept of operations (CONOPS). Even a several 
hour long electric thruster firing represents a small share of a single orbit revolution at destinations 
such as geostationary orbit (24 hour period) or the Gateway baseline NRHO (~7 day period). </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>

</p>
<p>During the CMV transit to the NRHO, the mission will instead maintain a thrust-dominant 
CONOPS, where the vehicle will be under nearly continuous thrust for more than 300 days. While 
this continuous use of the SEP system is effective at limiting the overall duration of the Lunar 
Transit, it also presents unique challenges for conjunction avoidance.  
</p>
<p> 
Figure 2: Lunar Transit apogee and perigee evolution as a function of mission elapsed time. 
</p>
<p>In a coast-dominant CONOPS conjunction avoidance is typically achieved through the 
utilization of a “bang-bang” maneuver sequence. This involves completing a propulsive maneuver 
to perturb a spacecraft from its baseline trajectory (and thereby avoid a potential collision), then 
completing a second maneuver to correct back to the desired flight path. For the majority of the 
Lunar Transit, particularly early in the transit when perigee is below 2,000 km, the CMV will be 
executing a nearly continuous low-thrust maneuver. In order to perturb CMV from the planned 
flight path, this low-thrust maneuver must be modified in some way.  
</p>
<p>Two primary perturbing methods form the basis of the developed preliminary CMV conjunction 
avoidance strategy; the use of the PPE reaction control system (RCS) and the interruption of a 
planned thrust arc with an inserted coast. Use of the chemical RCS thrusters represents a more 
traditional approach to conjunction avoidance maneuvers, where a propulsion system is used to 
perturb the spacecraft trajectory. The insertion of additional coasting into a planned thrust arc, 
however, represents a departure from traditional conjunction avoidance methodologies. The ability 
to generate separation from a reference trajectory through the use of coasting is referred to in this 
paper as ballistic leverage.  
</p>
<p>METHODOLOGY 
</p>
<p>Preliminary Conjunction Screening 
</p>
<p>To develop a reliable conjunction avoidance methodology for the Lunar Transit, a representative 
conjunction screening was completed utilizing Design Reference Mission (DRM) 5 as a reference. 
This preliminary screening aimed to accomplish two objectives. The first was to identify the most 
probable periods of the Lunar Transit during which conjunctions may occur. This analysis also </p>

expected conjunctions across the trajectory and the type of objects that would be encountered (op-
erational satellite vs debris, rocket body, etc). 
</p>
<p>This case study utilized the resident space object catalogue maintained by the 18th Space De-
fense Squadron (18 SDS) of the United States Space Force. The 18 SDS maintains the United States 
Space Command Space Situational Awareness sharing program, which shares tracking data of more 
than 45,000 catalogued space objects with government, commercial, and academic partners6. The 
tracking data available in the space object catalogue is valid for a period of roughly 5 days, after 
which the uncertainty in propagating the initial state forward in time becomes too large to be of use 
in a conjunction assessment. DRM-5, however, is more than one year in duration with a launch 
date planned for late 2024. To produce a valid screening the DRM-5 ephemeris was modified, with 
the Lunar Transit being broken into 5-day segments. Each 5-day interval was screened against the 
same reference epoch, March 17, 2023, at a time of 00:00:0000 GMT. Any object entering a +/- 
10x40x40 km volume in the radial, in-track, and cross-track directions surrounding the CMV was 
flagged as a conjunction. A summary of the screening assumptions is located in Table 1.

<p>In addition to the assumptions presented above, only the first 65 days of the trajectory were 
screened for conjunctions. This was done to limit the resource demand for this analysis at 18 SDS, 
as the same resources are used to complete real-time object clearing for the International Space 
Station (ISS). The results of this representative screening (Figure 3) show that the most critical 
period of the Lunar Transit is the first ~60 days of the mission. At mission elapsed time (MET) +60 
days, the CMV has encountered all expected conjunction events within the bounds of the analysis 
and according to the +/- 10x40x40 km screened volume, with the 90th percentile reached near MET 
+40 days. The results show that beyond MET +35 days, the rate at which conjunctions are encoun-
tered begins to plateau, supporting the termination of the screening at MET +65 days. Additionally, 
all expected conjunctions occurred within +/- 40º true anomaly of perigee. These results are con-
sistent with the expected object environment that the CMV will encounter early in the Lunar 
Transit. The bulk of the expected conjunction events occur as the CMV’s perigee altitude is raised 
from 200 km through an object-dense series of altitudes between 500 and 1,000 km. As the CMV 
continues its low thrust spiral trajectory, the number of expected conjunctions begins to level off 
as the spacecraft’s perigee continues to rise. </p>

<p>The representative conjunction screening results presented are notional. These results are meant 
to further inform the development of a conjunction avoidance strategy by providing context 
surrounding the number and type of conjunction events that could be encountered during the Lunar 
Transit and their location in terms of both MET and true anomaly. During flight, a fixed control 
volume will not be used to determine the probability of collision with a foreign object. A 
probabilistic risk assessment will be carried out in real-time to determine whether or not a collsion 
avoidance action must be taken. Object conjunctions may very well occur beyond MET +60 days 
during the flight of the CMV. Additionally as the launch date of the CMV is, at the time of writing, 
no earlier than November of 2024 it is reasonable to expect that a higher number of objects will be 
encountered during the mission than is reflected in these preliminary results. 
</p>
<p>Design Reference Mission Trajectories 
The DRM trajectories developed to date1 by the PPE MD team represent simplified engineering 
</p>
<p>models of the Lunar Transit. During the flight of the CMV, there will be a number of events and 
periods during which the SEP system cannot be operated. Several of these events are known 
quantities and can be directly accounted for in the modeling of the Lunar Transit. These include 
eclipses, during which the SEP system will not be operated (as the PPE solar arrays will be 
producing no power), and in-orbit testing (IOT) of various spacecraft systems which require the 
CMV to be under coast. As of DRM-5, Earth eclipses and major spacecraft IOT events are explicitly 
modeled as coasting periods in the DRM. There are, however, known events which are not currently 
explicitly modeled in the DRMs, including certain vehicle operational constraints and performance 
limitations. 
</p>
<p>To account for known as well as unknown coast periods that are not explicitly modeled, the 
DRMs utilize a reduced electric thruster duty cycle. The duty cycle effectively incorporates a time 
margin that can be used to account for unmodeled planned coast events, statistical uncertainties, 
execution errors, and various off nominal events. Explicit modeling of every known vehicle 
constraint and thruster shut down period would results in a large, complex, and cumbersome model 
of the Lunar Transit (Figure 4). This type of high fidelity model would prove prohibitive to the 
wide array of trade studies and analyses that are often required by the CMV project engineering 
teams. The use of a thruster duty cycle enables a simplified model of the Lunar Transit trajectory 
while still maintaining confidence in mission feasibility.

<p>One operational constraint included in the thruster duty cycle (and thus not explicitly modeled 
in DRM-5) is the shutdown of the electric propulsion system during low-altitude perigee passes 
(shown in Figure 4 in green). These low-altitude passes represent a very small portion of the total 
trajectory duration (less than 0.5% of DRM-5), and so modeling low perigee passes as a component 
of the duty cycle is sufficient for the level of fidelity of the DRMs. This does not, however, produce 
a reference trajectory sufficient for use in a conjunction avoidance analysis.  
</p>
<p>The CMV will encounter nearly all expected low-altitude perigee passes within the first 45 days 
of the Lunar Transit, a period of time that coincides with more than 90% of expected conjunction 
events. The duration of these low-altitude cutouts is a small portion of each individual revolution, 
with a maximum duration of roughly 20 minutes, compared to a ten hour orbital period (Figure 5). 
Although short in duration, these low-altitude passes have the ability to meaningly affect vehicle 
position at perigee and thus impact the criticality of a potential conjunction. To provide a more 
“flight-like” reference with which to complete a conjunction avoidance analysis, a detailed model 
of the Lunar Transit Spiral Phase was developed which explicitly includes these low-altitude 
perigee passes. </p>

<p>Conjunction Avoidance Analysis Reference Trajectory Development  
</p>
<p>DRM-5 employs a relatively simple model of the Spiral Phase. During this early phase of the 
trajectory all vehicle thrusting is restricted to be along the instantaneous Earth velocity vector with 
the objective of raising the energy of the CMV’s orbit as quickly as possible. Thrusting begins at 
MET +7 days at the end of an initial 7-day period of system checkouts and testing. Thrusting is 
then modeled as a continuous reduced duty cycle maneuver, interrupted only by eclipses during 
which the SEP system will not be operated. An additional 7-day IOT period, where the CMV will 
be under coast for the duration of testing, is notionally placed when the vehicle reaches an orbital 
period of 24 hours. Thrusting then resumes, uninterruped save for eclipses, until the completion of 
the Spiral Phase (currently defined as a semi-major axis of 130,000 km).  
</p>

the remainder of the Spiral Phase is modeled consistent with heritage DRMs. Modeling of heritage 
DRMs as well as the described detailed Spiral Phase reference is achieved with Copernicus7, a

<p>Analysis Methodology 
</p>
<p>The described reference trajectory forms the basis for a representative conjunction avoidance 
analysis completed utilizing Copernicus. Beginning at a given apogee state within the reference 
trajectory, a conjunction avoidance sequence is constructed and then propagated downstream for 
multiple revolutions. The initial epoch and state of a sequence takes the form of a control point 
defined using the reference trajectory ephemeris. Beginning at apogee, the sequence then contains 
nominal thrusting along the velocity vector with the vehicle following the reference trajectory. A 
conjunction avoidance action is then inserted into the trajectory in order to perturb the vehicle away 
from the reference and thereby avoid a potential collision. This perturbed trajectory is then 
propagated for multiple revolutions downstream. This sequence of events is visualized in Figure 8. 
The ability of a given action to generate separation from the reference is calculated as the magnitude 
of separation range between the perturbed trajectory and the reference as a function of elapsed time. 
For preliminary analysis, only the magnitude of range is presented, results are not decomposed into 
in-track, cross-track, or radial components. 
</p>
<p>The primary avoidance mechanism investigated in this analysis is ballistic leverage. Ballistic 
leverage involves breaking up a planned thrust maneuver with an additional inserted coast period. 
By this method, a lack of propulsion is used to generate separation from the reference trajectory, 
contrasting with more traditional conjunction avoidance maneuvers where separation is gained 
propulsively. 
</p>
<p>The amount of separation that can be gained through ballistic leverage is presented as a function 
of three independent variables; the duration of the inserted coast, the location of the coast within a 
given revolution, and the mission elapsed time of the apogee control point. The duration of a coast 
period directly impacts the amount of thrusting that a perturbed trajectory misses with respect to 
the reference and thus how far the perturbed trajectory will lag behind the reference. The location 
of a coast within a single revolution is used to determine the sensitivity of ballistic leverage to true 
anomaly and will inform the placement of these avoidance actions during mission operations. 
Finally, the initial epoch of the apogee control point is used to assess the sensivity of ballistic 
leverage to location within the Lunar Transit itself and thus identify critical periods of the transit 
where conjunction avoidance may be the most difficult to achieve. 
</p>
<p>To simplify the analysis and operational implementation of ballistic leverage, three potential 
locations were chosen for coast segment placement. The base hypothesis of ballistic leverage 
theorizes that coasting where the reference trajectory assumes thrusting will produce separation 
from the reference in the same manner as thrusting where the reference assumes a vehicle under 
coast. Following this theory, coasting during a period where there is already a planned coast in the 
reference will generate no separation. Thus, the low-altitude perigee passes which are explicitly 
modeled as coasts in the Spiral Phase reference trajectory represent periods where no ballistic 
leverage can be generated.  
</p>
<p>Placing an avoidance coast near perigee thus necessitates locating the segment immediately 
prior to or following the planned low-altitude cutout (during revolutions where the low-altitude 
perigee passes exist). Both of these placements effectively extend the low-altitude passes into a 
longer duration coast arc, as seen in Figure 8 (a) and (b). The final chosen location is for the 
additional coast segment is to bracket the initial apogee control point, shown in Figure 8 (c). These 
coasts are placed around apogee such that half of the coast occurs before apogee, the other half 
after. Together, these three locations bound a given revolution and provide insight into ballistic 
leverage sensitivity to coasts placed at apogee and near perigee. </p>

from the reference through propulsive means. RCS burns are restricted to occuring at apogee, the 
burns are modeled as impulsive maneuvers, and the burn vector is assumed to align with the 
velocity vector of the vehicle (Figure 9). Burn magnitudes of 0.5 m/s are presented. 
</p>

<p>Scope Limitations 
The most critical period of the Lunar Transit in terms of number of expected conjunctions is the 
</p>
<p>first 42 days of the mission (where 90% of expected encounters occur). The first 7 days, however, 
is allocated for system checkouts and SEP system characterization. This first week of flight is 
planned to be spent under coast, meaning that propulsive maneuvers will be the only available 
means of collision avoidance. In order to focus on the efficacy of ballistic leverage, the initial 
checkout period has been neglected in this analysis. Instead, the scope has been limited to the first 
35 days of thrusting, beginning at MET +7 days and terminating at MET +42 days.  </p>

<p>There are a number of details that are not captured in this analysis and must be investigated in 
future work. The presented results do not account for the necessity of avoiding multiple 
conjunctions within a single revolution, nor do they account for perturbing a trajectory out of the 
path of one conjunction and into the path of a second, previously unencountered object. While the 
separation gained from the inclusion of additional coasting is assessed, the larger impacts of 
additional coasting on the Lunar Transit are not investigated. These effects include perturbing the 
location and duration of downstream eclipses as well as the ability to target back to the reference 
trajectory, if necessary.  
</p>

<p>Coast Placed Prior to Low-Altitude Perigee Pass 
</p>
<p>For the case of coasts placed prior to the low-altitude perigee passes, no separation is generated

and follows the reference exactly. Separation from the reference is not generated until the beginning 
of the added coast arc, placed prior to the low-altitude cutout near perigee. This separation contin-
ues to grow as a function of time through the following apogee, until maximum separation (within 
the identified time horizon) is reached at the second perigee downstream of the initial control point 
(Figure 11).  
</p>
<p>From Figure 11 it can be seen that ballistic leverage is effective at generating significant sepa-
rations from a reference even with short duration coasts. A 1-hour added coast (short when com-
pared to the 10-hour long orbital period of a single revolution) generates separations on the order 
of 100 km within 15 hours of the initial apogee control point. Additionally, separation distances 
roughly scale according to the duration of the added coast. While a 1-hour coast produces separa-
tions on the order of 100 km, a 30-minute coast produces separations of roughly 50 km and a 15-
minute coast produces separations of roughly 25 km.

between the two cases, with the notable exception that separations for post-perigee coasts begin 
later in time relative to pre-perigee coasts (as the perturbing action occurs later in the trajectory).

<p>These results indicate that ballistic leverage is not particularly sensitive to the exact true anom-

Knowledge of this behavior will be valuable to mission controllers, who will need to consider a 
wide array of parameters and outcomes when deciding where to place a collision avoidance action.

<p>Propulsive Maneuvers 
</p>
<p>The final presented analysis case involves utilizing the PPE RCS to complete a propulsive ma-
neuver at apogee in the direction of the velocity vector. A critical difference between this propulsive 
leverage case and previously presented ballistic leverage cases is that while additional coasting 
means that a resulting perturbed trajectory lags behind the reference in terms of orbital energy, a 
propulsive maneuver boosts the perturbed trajectory ahead of the reference. 
</p>
<p>Propulsive maneuvers occurring at apogee display very similar behavior to apogee coasts, both 
in magnitude of separations and growth of dispersions over time (Figure 15). Significant separa-
tions are generated over a short horizon, with a small impulse of 0.5 m/s creating more than 30 km 
of separation from the reference within half of one revolution. This suggests symmetric behavior 
in terms of ∆v added versus ∆v lost, with similar results being generated for both. The similarity of 
the results in terms of separation magnitudes further demonstrates the efficacy of ballistic leverage 
as a means by which to affect the Lunar Transit. Even small propulsive maneuvers, such as the 0.5 
m/s burns presented here, require propellant to be expended. Any RCS propellant used during the 
Lunar Transit cannot be used later for attitude control and orbit maintenance in the NRHO during 
the Gateway mission. 
</p>

<p>DISCUSSION 
The results presented in this paper show that ballistic leverage is not only a viable method by 
</p>
<p>which to generate separation from a reference trajectory, it also exhibits similar performance to 
propulsive maneuvers conducted by a chemical RCS. Through ballistic leverage the CMV will 
have the ability to perform debris avoidance coasting (DACs) during the Lunar Transit, in place of 
more traditional debris avoidance maneuvers (DAMs). 
</p>
<p>The Lunar Transit is a departure from the form of mission that the mission controllers of FOD 
have operated in the past. The CMV (and Gateway) will be the first human rated NASA mission to 
rely primarily on electric propulsion and the first low-thrust mission to be flown from the Mission 
Control Center in Houston. Where Orion flew for roughly 25 days during Artemis 1, the CMV will 
be transiting to the Moon for more than 12 months. And while FOD has flown the International 
Space Station for more than two decades, the ISS remains in a relatively stationary orbit (save for 
high-thrust orbit boosting maneuvers) while the CMV will be under nearly thrust continuous for 
more than 300 days. 
</p>
<p>While ballistic leverage represents yet another departure from heritage operations, its imple-
mentation will likely follow established procedures and methodologies. During mission operations, 
it is often beneficial to wait as long as possible before performing a DAM. By delaying action, the 
probability of a collision can be more accurately assessed as additional orbit determination data of 
both the spacecraft and the conjuncting object can be analyzed. Following this “wait and see” meth-
odology ensures that DAMs are only executed when necessary and thus saves wasteful use of pro-
pellant. 
</p>
<p>In this paper, coasts placed near perigee are shown to produce significant separations from the 
reference one full revolution after the perturbing action, at the next perigee downstream. While 
these separations are on the order of tens to hundreds of kilometers, maximum values are not </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>   
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 18 
</p>
<p>realized for more than 14 hours after the initial apogee control point. Coasts placed near apogee, 
however, generate separations within half a revolution and are thus more aligned with the “wait 
and see” approach to collision avoidance. 
</p>
<p>With comparable performance, ballistic leverage is a natural choice over propulsive DAMs. 
Ballistic leverage utilizes no propellant while any propulsive maneuver conducted by the RCS rep-
resents ∆v that cannot be utilized to maintain the Gateway mission in the NRHO. Ballistic leverage, 
however, does effectively represent missed thrust for the Lunar Transit. An hour of coasting can be 
thought of as an hour of lost ∆v that must be made up later in the mission. The details of making 
up the lost ∆v will be analyzed in future work. 
</p>
<p>While propulsive maneuvers are less-preferable due to the consumption of valuable propellant, 
there are times during the Lunar Transit where the vehicle will not be under thrust. For most of 
these cases, the more efficient EP system could be used to complete a propulsive maneuver. How-
ever, during periods where the EP system is not available (during initial checkout and characteri-
zation of the thrusters, for example), the RCS system will be the only means of conjunction avoid-
ance. 
</p>
<p>A number of alternate implementations of ballistic leverage exist but were not covered in this 
paper. For example, while completely shutting down the EP system represents the maximum pos-
sible ballistic separation that can be gained in a given amount of time, the operator could simply 
choose to throttle thrusters back instead of turning them off completely. This would prevent an 
additional on/off cycling of the EP system and would produce results similar, although smaller in 
magnitude, to those presented in this paper. 
</p>
<p>CONCLUSION 
</p>
<p>The CMV, the first two co-manifested elements of NASA’s Gateway, will likely experience a 
number of conjunction events with orbital objects and debris during the low-thrust Lunar Transit 
to the NRHO. A method of conjunction avoidance by which a nearly continuous low-thrust 
maneuver is interrupted with the insertion of additional coast arcs has been developed. This 
methodology, referred to as ballistic leverage, is shown to be a viable method by which to generate 
separations from a reference trajectory on the order of hundreds of kilometers. Separation 
magnitudes are a function of the duration of an inserted coast and that coast’s location within a 
revolution. Coast arcs placed near perigee are shown to create significant separations from the 
reference within two revolutions while coasts placed near apogee are shown to generate leverage 
within one half of a revolution. Ballistic leverage does not require the use of propellant and 
therefore is a highly repeatable method of conjunction avoidance. 
</p>
