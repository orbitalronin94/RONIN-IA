<p>Test Results for Autonomous Assembly of Modular Space
Structures
</p>
<p>John R. Cooper∗, Jacob R. Martin†, and Olive R. Stohlman‡
</p>
<p>NASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, VA, 23693
</p>
<p>Collin J. Cresta§, Thea V. Avila¶, Radhika Rajaram‖, and Andrew K. McQuarry∗∗
</p>
<p>Analytical Mechanics Associates, Hampton, VA, 23666
</p>
<p>This paper presents test results for autonomous assembly of a modular space structure. A
Universal Robots UR10e manipulator with a custom end-effector, known as the Grapple Tool,
was used to join TriTruss structural modules. The TriTrusses and their assembly configuration
were developed for an in-space assembled telescope conceptual design. The tests presented here
were performed with a 1:2.8 dimensionally scaled version of the Tri-Trusses. Robot trajectories
were generated autonomously using a sample-based motion planner and an a-priori worksite
model. The test results verify the assembly concept of operations as well as the software
requirements for the Grapple Tool. While the Grapple Tool used in the tests was designed for
the 1:2.8 scale Tri-Trusses, it has the same actuators, software, and computation architecture
as the version designed for the full scale structure. The test operations consist of picking up
two TriTrusses from their stowed locations and joining them to a third TriTruss mounted
on a test stand and joining them to each other. This constitutes the first three modules of
the in-space assembled telescope conceptual design. The paper presents the robot behavioral
architecture, the generated trajectories commanded, the actual trajectories of the robot, and
actuator performance of the Grapple Tool.
</p>
<p>I. Introduction
</p>
<p>The contribution of this paper is a concept of operations for autonomous assembly of a modular space structure
validated through hardware tests. In-space servicing, assembly, and manufacturing technologies have the potential
</p>
<p>to revolutionize the space ecosystem. NASA’s On-orbit Servicing, Assembly, and Manufacturing 1 mission will
demonstrate servicing of an existing spacecraft via teleoperated robotics [1]. The introduction of modular structural
design and autonomous systems can greatly enhance repairability as well as enable new types of spacecraft. This paper
represents a proof of concept for such an architecture. In the hardware tests presented herein, a Universal Robots UR10e
manipulator was used to position structural modules to be joined. A custom robotic end-effector, known as the Grapple
Tool, was used to both grasp modules at their stowed locations and attach them to other modules in the existing structure.
Once attached, the robot applied a test load to verify the connection was successfully made. Results are presented for an
assembly of three modules. Figure 1 shows the configuration in the Subscale In-Space Assembly Laboratory at NASA
Langley Research Center, and figure 2 shows the final state after three modules have been joined.
</p>
<p>The testing was performed as part of NASA’s Precision Assembled Space Structure (PASS) project. The design
reference mission of the project is construction of an in-space assembled telescope based on the concept proposed in the
NASA in-Space Assembled Telescope (iSAT) Study [2]. The study concludes that in-space assembly is likely the only
means to achieve a space telescope with aperture greater than 15 m. The design concept has a backbone truss structure
composed of structural modules, dubbed TriTrusses [3, 4]. A mirror segment would be attached to each TriTruss. Figure
3 shows the iSAT design concept. The iSAT mirror is 20 m in diameter. The PASS project will perform a full scale
ground demonstration of the assembly of the backbone truss structure for the mirror. The results presented in this paper
represent the first steps in the buildup to this demonstration.
</p>

<p>The test results presented here use a 1:2.8 scale version of the TriTruss also referred to as the 1 m TriTruss since 1 m
is the length of each side on the triangular face. A single 1 m TriTruss is shown in figure 4. The test results presented
herein validate the robot trajectory generation pipeline and the Grapple Tool software. The Grapple Tool uses the same
actuators, electronics, and software as the full scale version.
</p>
<p>The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: section II details the methods used for robot trajectory generation,
section III describes the Grapple Tool design, section IV presents the test procedure for assembling three TriTruss
modules, section V provides test results and data, and finally conclusions are drawn in section VI.
</p>
<p>II. Trajectory Generation
The Robot Operating System (ROS2) ecosystem is leveraged for trajectory generation and control of the manipulator
</p>
<p>[5]. The MoveIt motion planning software [6] is used to interface to the Open Motion Planning Library (OMPL) [7].
Trajectory generation is performed using a model of the robot’s workspace with mesh representations of obstacles.
</p>
<p>A six degree of freedom end-effector goal state is provided. This corresponds either to the grasping pose for picking
up a TriTruss or the insertion pose to connect the TriTruss to the rest of the structure. First, the robot inverse kinematics
are used to find a robot joint state that satisfies the end-effector goal state. If the resulting joint state is valid, i.e. no
constraints are violated (joint limits, collision constraints), a Cartesian motion planner finds a motion from the goal
state to an approach point. This Cartesian motion is executed in reverse to bring the end-effector from the approach
point to the goal state with the desired payload orientation. This step ensures that the goal state is reachable from the
approach point. Next, the Rapidly-Exploring Random Trees (RRT) algorithm [8] is used to plan the gross motion from
the starting state to the approach point. At each step of this pipeline, constraints are checked using a planning scene
which represents all objects in the robot workspace.
</p>
<p>III. Grapple Tool
The Grapple Tool is used to grasp and attach TriTrusses and was first presented in [9]. The version of the Grapple
</p>
<p>Tool used in the tests presented here is designed for the 1:2.8 scale TriTrusses but retains all actuators and electronics,

<p>and software of the full scale version. Therefore, these test results validate the Grapple Tool software requirements.
Figure 5 shows the Grapple Tool used for the tests. It has three prongs for grasping each corner of the triangular
</p>
<p>face of the TriTruss. Each prong has an identical set of actuators: three Dynamixel MX-106 rotary servos and one
MightyZAP 12Lf-78PT-53 linear actuator. Figure 6 shows the configuration of the actuators at each end of the Grapple
Tool. One rotary servo controls the Post Fingers which are used for grasping the TriTruss. Another rotary servo controls
the Capture Fingers which grasp a Capture Post attached to the TriTruss that is to be joined with on the existing structure.
This ensures fine alignment between the two TriTrusses. The final rotary servo is used to rotate the Lock Screwdriver
while the linear actuator pushes it forward. This mechanism drives a bolt which joins the two TriTrusses.
</p>
<p>IV. Test Procedure
The initial conditions for the test consist of one TriTruss attached to a test stand and the robot and two other
</p>
<p>TriTrusses in predefined starting poses as shown in figures 1. The test was carried out as follows. Note that in future
work, manual fine alignment steps will be replaced by a visual servoing algorithm discussed in [9].
</p>
<p>1) The robot plans a trajectory to pick up the first TriTruss using the trajectory generation algorithm of section II.
2) Fine alignment is performed manually using the UR10e teach pendant.
3) After fine alignment, the Post Fingers close on the TriTruss Grapple Post. At this point, the robot’s payload
</p>
<p>information is updated using a mass model of the TriTruss.
4) The trajectory generation algorithm is used to plan a trajectory to the insertion point for the first TriTruss.
5) Again, manual fine alignment is performed.
6) The robot switches into a compliance control mode and the Capture Fingers close on the Capture Post attached
</p>
<p>to the TriTruss that is being joined. Compliance control allows the Capture Fingers to pull the robot into a final
position such that the Lock Screwdriver can drive a bolt to join the two TriTrusses.
</p>
<p>7) The Lock Screwdriver tightens the bolt to 8.4 Nm using torque feedback from the rotary servo. Once the bolt is
tightened, the Lock Screwdriver backs out and the Capture Fingers open.
</p>
<p>8) The robot applies a 100 N test load in the axial direction of the bolt. This ensures the connection was successful.
9) The robot payload information is updated to remove the previously held TriTruss.
</p>
<p>10) Cartesian motion planner is used to drive the robot along a retreat path.
11) Steps 1-10 are repeated with the second TriTruss to complete a three-truss assembly.
</p>

<p>V. Results

<p>A. Robot Trajectories
The joint positions and end-effector state of the robot during the assembly operation are shown in the following
</p>
<p>figures. Figure 7 shows the joint positions for the first trial, figure 8 shows the end-effector states for the first trial, figure

</p>
<p>In both trials the sensed joint positions tracked the desired positions. Slight tracking errors occur when the payload
changes (TriTruss grabbed or let go) as the robot’s mechanical brakes are engaged during this step.
</p>

</p>

After joining the TriTrusses, the robot was used to apply a 100 N test load in the direction of the bolt axis. This
</p>
<p>ensures that the connection was successful. A force/torque sensor built in to the UR10e was used to verify this load.
Figure 17 shows the force data in this direction while the test load was applied during the three TriTruss attachment
trials.

<p>VI. Conclusion
The test results presented here verify the feasibility and repeatability of the autonomous assembly concept of
</p>
<p>operations. This basic concept can be used to enable new types of spacecraft that can be easily repaired, extended,
and evolved. The remaining gap is how to integrate visual servoing for fine alignment. Future work will explore this
concept. Additionally, future tests will further enhance the reliability of the autonomous systems by demonstrating
larger assemblies with more TriTrusses, and testing with the full scale, 2.8 m TriTrusses and Grapple Tool.
</p>
