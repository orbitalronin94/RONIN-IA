<p>INTROIUCTION 
</p>
<p>This paper presents a summary of results obtained in dynamic tests of free-flight 
</p>
<p>models of various configurations suitable for V/STOL transport aircraft at the NASA 
</p>
<p>Langley Research Center. The configurations covered will be the tilt-wing propeller­
</p>
<p>driven type as represented by the XC-142A tri-service transport, the fan-in-wing type as 
</p>
<p>represented by the XV-5A research airplane, and the jet-lift type as represented by the 
</p>
<p>Dornier 00-31 transport. The test techni~ues themselves are not described in this paper 
</p>
<p>since they have been described in detail previously in references 1 and 2. 
</p>
<p>TILT-WING CONFIGURATION 
</p>
<p>Free-flight tests have been made with a number of tilt-wing V/STOL configurations, 
</p>
<p>starting in 1955, The results of these investigations are reported in references 3 
</p>
<p>to 11. Recently tests have been made with the XC-142A configuration, but the results 
</p>
<p>have not yet been reported. All of these tests have shown certain stabtlity and control 
</p>
<p>characteristics which are common to all of the configurations and are therefore consid­
</p>
<p>ered characteristic of the tilt-wing configuration in general. All of these character­
</p>
<p>istics were evident to a certain degree in the XC-142A which is the most up-to-date con­
</p>
<p>figuration covered in dynamic model tests; so the results obtained with this configura­
</p>
<p>tion will be used for the purposes of illustration herein. A photograph of this model is 
</p>
<p>shown in figure 1. The model has a double slotted flap that is programed to deflect down 
</p>
<p>when the wing is at intermediate tilt angles between o0 and 90°. Control for hovering 
</p>
<p>flight is provided by the tail rotor for pitch control, ailerons built into the trailing 
</p>
<p>edge of the flap for yaw control, and differential pitch of the right and left propellers 
</p>
<p>for roll control. 
</p>

<p></p>

<p>Hovering 
</p>
<p>Out of ground effect.- For hovering flight out of ground effect, the basic stick­
</p>
<p>fixed motions of the model were characterized by unstable pitching and rolling oscilla­
</p>
<p>tions as indicated by the time histories of figure 2. These oscillations are quite 
</p>
<p>unstable in terms of the classical measure of cycles to double amplitude, but they can 
</p>
<p>be controlled quite easily by the pilot because of their relatively long period - 10 and 
</p>
<p>18 seconds, full scale in pitch and roll, respectively. The full-scale VZ-2 tilt-wing 
</p>
<p>research airplane had the same type of unstable os.cillations but the pilot found them 
</p>
<p>easy to control. In fact, he had flown the airplane many times before he realized that 
</p>
<p>there were unstable oscillations. He was aware of the. fact that the airplane tended to 
</p>
<p>diverge, but he did not let the aircraft diverge long enough for the periodic nature of 
</p>
<p>the motion to become evident. Only later during specific attempts to determine the char­
</p>
<p>acteristics of the uncontrolled stick-fixed motions was the periodic character of the 
</p>
<p>motion observed in the full-scale flight tests. The unstable oscillations had been 
</p>
<p>observed previously, however, in the free-flight tests of a 1/4-scale model of the VZ-2 
</p>
<p>reported in reference 6. 
</p>
<p>The total control power in pitch and roll required to deal with these unstable 
</p>
<p>oscillations and otherwise provide satisfactory controllability in hovering has been 
</p>
<p>found on the XC-142A model, and on all models flown previously, to be in agreement with 
</p>
<p>the control power requirements specified in various appropriate specifications such as 
</p>
<p>references 12 to 14 for helicopters and V/STOL aircraft. The fact that the pilot of a 
</p>
<p>small-scale remotely controlled model would want the s8.J!].e (scaled-down) total control 
</p>
<p>power as the pilot in a full-scale aircraft seems almost fortuitous. The tasks required 
</p>
<p>of the pilot in pitch and roll are about the same as those confronting the pilot of the 
</p>
<p>full-scale airplane, however. They are: hovering smoothly and precisely over a spot on 
</p>
<p>the ground, rapid maneuvering from one position to another, and recovering quickly from 
</p>
<p>inadvertent disturbances such as gust disturbances and from the unstable oscillations if 
</p>
<p>they are inadvertently allowed to buildup. These tasks require a proper balance between 
</p>
<p>the small control moments required for steady flight in still air and the large control 
</p>
<p>- 2 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>moments required for maneuvering and for coping quickly with disturbances. Because of 
</p>
<p>this similarity of tasks and considerations, therefore, perhaps it is not so surprising 
</p>
<p>that the control power requirements of the model scale properly to represent those of 
</p>
<p>the full-scale airplane. 
</p>
<p>For the yawing mode of motion, however, the flying model tests do not agree as well 
</p>
<p>with the requirements of the full-scale airplane. This difference is probably the result 
</p>
<p>of the model flight test technique. Neither the model nor the full-scale aircraft have 
</p>
<p>any stability (or instability) in yaw. They do not tend to either diverge from a given 
</p>
<p>heading or to return to it is disturbed. Consequently, the task of maintaining a heading 
</p>
<p>requires little control for hovering out of ground effect. This is the only task 
</p>
<p>assigned the yaw control pilot for hovering tests of the free-flight model where if the 
</p>
<p>model yaws through large angles the remotely located pilots lose their orientation. For 
</p>
<p>the full-scale aircraft, however, the pilots wish to be able to turn the aircraft rapidly 
</p>
<p>and this maneuver requires a higher level of control power than is required in the model 
</p>
<p>tests. 
</p>
<p>In ground effect.- There is a pronounced favorable ground effect on lift for tilt­
</p>
<p>wing aircraft which results primarily from the fact that positive pressures are induced 
</p>
<p>on the bottom of the fuselage because the recirculating propeller slipstream flows upward 
</p>
<p>along the plane of symmetry when the aircraft is hovering near the ground. This type of 
</p>
<p>flow and its effects have been discussed in many previous papers such as references 15 
</p>
<p>to 17. For large flat-bottomed fuselages, such as that of the XC-142A, this ground 
</p>
<p>effect can be very large as shown by the force test data of figure 3. There is also a 
</p>
<p>smaller contribution to ground effect which results from the fact that the propellers 
</p>
<p>themselves are influenced by their proximity to the ground. This is the well-known 
</p>
<p>effect of ground proximity experienced by helicopters. It is a relatively small part of 
</p>
<p>the whole picture in the tilt-wing aircraft, however, because the propellers are not very 
</p>
<p>close to the ground in terms of their own diameters. This contribution to the total 
</p>
<p>ground effect is shown in reference 16. 
</p>
<p>- 3 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>The groillld effect on lift gives a very pronoilllced height stability for hovering very 
</p>
<p>close to the ground and makes the maintenance of a constant height a very simple task for 
</p>
<p>the pilot since he hardly has to manipulate the throttle at all. 
</p>
<p>Another manifestation of this ground effect is its effect on stability in pitch and 
</p>
<p>roll. Groillld proximity has a pronounced static stabilizing effect in pitch and a lesser 
</p>
<p>one in roll as shown by the force test data of figure 4. The stabilizing effect in pitch 
</p>
<p>evidently results from the lift on the bottom of the fuselage resulting from the ground 
</p>
<p>proximity which would be expected to be greater on the end of the fuselage closest to the 
</p>
<p>ground and thereby produce the static stability in pitch shown in figure 4. The stabili­
</p>
<p>zing effect in roll evidently results from the fact that the proximity to the groillld 
</p>
<p>causes the thrust of the propellers closest to the ground to be the greatest and thereby 
</p>
<p>causes the static stability in roll. 
</p>
<p>The effects of the static stability in pitch on the dynamic stability of the model 
</p>
<p>show up very graphically from figure 5, This figure shows that the uncontrolled stick­
</p>
<p>fixed pitching motions of the model which were very unstable for hovering out of ground 
</p>
<p>effect were about neutrally stable for the case of hovering near the groillld, The time 
</p>
<p>history of figure 5 shows that the oscillation did not diverge when the model was very 
</p>
<p>near the ground and diverged slowly when the model rose slightly higher above the ground. 
</p>
<p>This ground effect on the pitching motions, which is quite dependent on the shape of the 
</p>
<p>bottom of the fuselage was found to occur to an even greater degree in tests of a model 
</p>
<p>of the X-18 airplane reported in reference 5. 
</p>
<p>The results of the static stability in roll on dynamic stability was not nearly as 
</p>
<p>apparent as the effect in pitch, but the pilots observed that the XC-142A model was some­
</p>
<p>what easier to control in roll when hovering in ground effect than when hovering well 
</p>
<p>above the ground, 
</p>
<p>The yawing motions of the XC-142A model were much more erratic and difficult to con­
</p>
<p>trol when hovering near the ground than when hovering out of ground effect. In groillld 
</p>
<p>effect the model appeared to be subjected to more frequent and larger yawing disturbances. 
</p>
<p>This type of behavior has been noted previously with other dynamic models and also with 
</p>
<p>- 4 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>the full-scale VZ-2 airplane in flight tests as indicated by references 18 and 19. The 
</p>
<p>problem had been studied in some detail in reference 17, and it seemed that the erratic 
</p>
<p>disturbances resulted from the unsteady nature of the upward flow of the slipstream along 
</p>
<p>the aircraft center line. This unsteady flow switches from side to side and causes the 
</p>
<p>inflow to the propellers to vary so that the propellers cause large random moments. 
</p>
<p>Also, it seems likely that this unsteady flow at the tail of the airplane might be a 
</p>
<p>source of erratic yawing disturbances. In any event, the model is subjected to erratic 
</p>
<p>yawing disturbances when hovering near the ground. 
</p>
<p>The proximity of the ground also causes the yaw control provided by the ailerons to 
</p>
<p>become weaker as the model nears the ground so that the control available to combat 
</p>
<p>yawing disturbances is lessened at the time the yawing disturbances are increased. This 
</p>
<p>reduction in yaw control is shown by the force test data of figure 6. The reason for 
</p>
<p>this reduction in yaw control effectiveness is explained in reference 20 which indicates 
</p>
<p>that it results from the fact that the slipstream velocity in which the ailerons are 
</p>
<p>operating is reduced in the region near the ground. 
</p>
<p>Transition 
</p>
<p>Level flight.- The longitudinal stability of the XC-142A model in the transition 
</p>
<p>range of flight is illustrated in figure 7. These data show that the unstable stick­
</p>
<p>fixed oscillation which had been noted in hovering became less unstable as the wing 
</p>
<p>incidence was reduced and that the model was stable at an incidence of 10°. The data 
</p>
<p>also show that the period became very long in the transition range so that it is doubtful 
</p>
<p>whether the pilot of the model or a full-scale airplane would ever be aware of the fact 
</p>
<p>that there was an oscillation unless he specifically looked for it. 
</p>
<p>The directional behavior of the XC-142A model in the transition range was character­
</p>
<p>ized by rather annoying small-amplitude yawing motions of a random character. This type 
</p>
<p>of behavior is characteristic of models which have low or neutral static directional sta­
</p>
<p>bility for small angles of sideslip but have adequate directional stability at higher 
</p>
<p>sideslip angles. Force tests of the model showed that it did have such static direc­
</p>
<p>tional stability characteristics and that the use of a larger vertical tail would 
</p>
<p>- 5 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>increase the directional stability over the entire sideslip range. Consequently a larger 
</p>
<p>vertical tail was tried in flight tests and was found to make the dynamic directional 
</p>
<p>behavior of the model satisfactory. Since it did not seem that the vertical tail of the 
</p>
<p>full-scale airplane would be enlarged, however, the tail extension was removed and the 
</p>
<p>remainder of the tests were made with the original tail. 
</p>
<p>The lateral stability of the model was much better in transition than in hovering 
</p>
<p>flight. In fact, the unstable rolling oscillations encountered in hovering flight, as 
</p>
<p>indicated in figure 2, had disappeared by the time the wing incidence was reduced to 65°. 
</p>
<p>At all lower angles of incidence the rolling oscillations of the model, which could prob­
</p>
<p>ably be called Dutch roll oscillations because of the forward speed and the probable 
</p>
<p>coupling with the yawing motions, were stable. The pilot could disturb the model in roll 
</p>
<p>and the ensuing oscillation would quickly damp out. No time histories of this motion 
</p>
<p>were obtained, however, because there were no cameras located in a position suitable to 
</p>
<p>record it. With the evident high degree of lateral stability the pilot found the model 
</p>
<p>to be quite easy to control laterally. 
</p>
<p>Descent conditions.- Descending flight conditions are simulated in free-flight tests 
</p>
<p>in the Langley full-scale tunnel by use of the technique indicated by figure 8. This 
</p>
<p>figure shows the balance of forces involved in actual descent at the left and in the 
</p>
<p>simulated descent at the right. For a descent condition, the aircraft must have a net 
</p>
<p>aerodynamic drag, and the lift, drag, and weight forces are in balance with the drag 
</p>
<p>being balanced by the forward component of the weight acting along the flight path. In 
</p>
<p>order to simulate the descent condition in the horizontal airstrea.m of the tunnel, the 
</p>
<p>model is flown with the same lift and drag (same angle of attack and power setting) and a 
</p>
<p>thrust force is added by means of a small compressed-air jet at the rear of the model to 
</p>
<p>balance the drag of the model. In this way the aerodynamic eff~cts of descent conditions, 
</p>
<p>which are very important for the tilt-wing V/STOL configuration, can be simulated in hori­
</p>
<p>zontal flight in the tunnel. 
</p>
<p>The descent tests were conducted to study the effect of wing stalling which has been 
</p>
<p>found in previous full-scale and model flight tests such as those of references 11, 18, 
</p>
<p>- 6 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>and l9 to cause the dynamic behavior of the aircraft to become so poor as to limit the 
</p>
<p>rate of descent that the pilot is willing to use in the airplane. The stalling results 
</p>
<p>from the reduced slipstream velocity over the wing due to the reduced power used in the 
</p>
<p>descent conditions. This effect is discussed in detail in reference 2l. 
</p>
<p>Figure 9 presents the results of the descent tests of the XC-l42A model in the form 
</p>
<p>of pilot ratings of the behavior of the model for a range of descent angle and wing­
</p>
<p>incidence conditions. The pilot rating system used in the model tests is similar to the 
</p>
<p>Cooper pilot rating system used in full-scale flight work and is shown in table I where 
</p>
<p>it is compared with the Cooper rating system. The use of this system does not imply that 
</p>
<p>the model techniQue can predict, Quantitatively with fine graduation, the full-scale 
</p>
<p>characteristics. Rather, the model ratings are alined with the Cooper rating system by 
</p>
<p>use of the~ and~ boundaries between satisfactory, unsatisfactory, and unacceptable 
2 2 
</p>
<p>characteristics. The intent of the model ratings is to consider, through past experi­
</p>
<p>ence, what type of behavior of the model would represent the behavior reQuired of an air­
</p>
<p>plane to meet the conditions listed under the Cooper system as to whether the mission 
</p>
<p>could be accomplished, the aircraft landed, whether acceptable for normal operating con­
</p>
<p>ditions or only for emergency conditions, and to present these .ratings with a system that 
</p>
<p>would be familiar to the most people. 
</p>

</p>
<p>and l5°. At each point, two ratings were obtained - (1) a rating of the behavior of the 
</p>
<p>model when reasonably smooth and steady flight was maintained, and (2) a rating for dis­
</p>
<p>turbed flight after the model had intentionally been given a large disturbance or had 
</p>

</p>
<p>angles the model was very stable and had to be intentionally disturbed with controls, 
</p>
<p>after which the disturbed motion damped Quickly, so for these conditions there was no

</p>
<p>cult at some wing incidences to establish steady flight conditions and two ratings are

<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>given. And, at the greatest descent angles, steady flight was not possible so that only

<p>a disturbed-flight rating was given. The ratings shown in figure 9 are overall ratings 
</p>
<p>obtained from individual ratings on lateral, directional, longitudinal, and power 
</p>
<p>characteristics. 
</p>
<p>For convenience in discussing the model pilots' interpretation of the results, the 
</p>
<p>ratings are summarized in figure 10 in the form of boundaries on a plot of flight-path 
</p>
<p>angle against wing-incidence angle. Figure 10 shows a 6° descent capability, above the 
</p>
<p>dotted area, where no difference from level flight was detected even when the model was 
</p>
<p>intentionally disturbed. As the descent angle was increased in the dotted area the model 
</p>
<p>required more and more pilot attention to the controls and flow disturbances could be 
</p>
<p>noticed occasionally from tufts. It was felt that, although the model characteristics 
</p>
<p>were not unacceptable in the dotted area, the flow disturbances noticed could mean that 
</p>
<p>buffeting would be a factor in this region of flight for the full-scale aircraft. At the 
</p>
<p>higher descent angles in the dotted area the model did not settle down quickly after a 
</p>
<p>disturbance and in the cross-hatched area, the model experienced abrupt wing dropping, 
</p>
<p>abrupt losses in height, and the generally sloppy, wallowing motions normally associated 
</p>
<p>with extensive wing stall. It was felt that the characteristics were completely unac­
</p>
<p>ceptable in this region. Figures 11 and 12 are graphic examples of how the results of 
</p>
<p>stalling show up in the model flights. Time histories of the model motions in controlled 
</p>
<p>flight for which the pilot was trying to fly smoothly are shown in figure 11 for the 
</p>
<p>level-flight and 13° descent flight conditions for a wing incidence of 30°. In each case 
</p>
<p>the angle of roll and angle of yaw is plotted against model time. In level flight the 
</p>
<p>model was very easy to fly and required only occasional corrective control. The erratic 
</p>
<p>wallowing motions at 13° descent angle, however, were extremely difficult to control; 
</p>
<p>and, in fact, control of the model was lost at times during some tests for this condition.· 
</p>
<p>Figure 12 shows time histories of the model motions at 7° and 10° descent angle after the 
</p>
<p>model had been intentionally disturbed from a smooth flying condition. At 7° descent 
</p>
<p>angle, two rapid control pulses were used by the pilot to set up the motion and he was 
</p>
<p>able to restore the model to smooth flight quickly since the basic lateral motions were 
</p>
<p>- 8 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>well damped. At 10° descent angle, the model motions were already erratic at the start 
</p>
<p>of the test and only one control pulse was needed to cause the wild, wallowing motions 
</p>
<p>shown in the figure which the pilot was barely able to control at all. 
</p>
<p>Other characteristics of the model motions in descent flight were observed that. can­
</p>
<p>not be expressed by simple ratings. For example, at times, in the tests reported above, 
</p>
<p>the model would drop in height abruptly without any appreciable effect on the lateral­
</p>
<p>directional characteristics being noted. This abrupt loss in height was a new type of 
</p>
<p>motion not previously experienced with the VZ-2 •. From watching the tufts on the wing, it 
</p>
<p>was evident that the abrupt dropping was caused by an abrupt symmetrical stall over a 
</p>
<p>large part of both the left and right wing panels. Another characteristic noticed was 
</p>
<p>that at high descent angles, somewhat different model motions were obtained at low wing 
</p>
<p>incidence than at high wing angles. For example, at 20° wing incidence, steady flight 
</p>
<p>could be achieved quite easily and with no apparent stalling to about 10° descent angle. 
</p>
<p>However, if a disturbance occurred at this point, the resulting abrupt wing dropping and 
</p>
<p>generally sloppy, wallowing motions were very difficult to control and a rating of seven 
</p>
<p>resulted. At 50° wing incidence, however, the tufts showed disturbed flow on the wing 
</p>
<p>long before the model motions were appreciably affected. This effect can probably be 
</p>
<p>explained by the fact that at the high incidence of the thrustline and high flap deflec­
</p>
<p>tion at 50° wing incidence, most of the weight was supported by power rather than by wing 
</p>
<p>lift so that wing stall affected only a very small part of the total lift. 
</p>
<p>Normally, small~scale tests would not be too suitable for representing the stall or 
</p>
<p>other flight conditions involving separated flows because of the discrepancy in Reynolds 
</p>
<p>number. Experience has shown, however, that the stall of a small-scale model usually 
</p>
<p>occurs at a lower angle of attack than that for the corresponding airplane, and also, 
</p>
<p>that when the stall does occur, the resulting motions are generally quite similar. In 
</p>
<p>the case of the descent tests, therefore, it would be expected that the effect of low 
</p>
<p>Reynolds number would tend to give conservative results and, that in any event, the free­
</p>
<p>flight model technique gives a good qualitative indication of the type of resultant 
</p>
<p>motion expected as the rate of descent is increased.

<p>FAN-IN-WING CONFIGURATION 
</p>
<p>Flight tests have been made with only one fan-in-wing configuration to date, and 
</p>
<p>these tests have not been completed; so that the discussion for this type of V/STOL air­
</p>
<p>craft is less detailed than that for the tilt-wing configuration, and it is less certain 
</p>
<p>whether the results obtained are characteristic of a class of aircraft or peculiar to the 
</p>
<p>one design tested. The configuration tested is that of the XV-5A airplane, and the 
</p>
<p>flying model is shown in figures 15(a) and 15(b). The airplane derives its lift for 
</p>
<p>hovering flight from two large tip-turbine driven fans in the wings and a smaller fan in 
</p>
<p>the nose. The transition is accomplished by deflecting the exhaust of the wing fans 
</p>
<p>rearward by means of louvers beneath the fans. For conventional forward flight, the tur­
</p>
<p>bojet engine exhaust is diverted from the fan turbines to conventional tailpipes beneath 
</p>
<p>the tail and the fans are covered over to form wing and fuselage nose contours. 
</p>
<p>The control system used on the model was not the same as that used on the full-scale 
</p>
<p>airplane. On the full-scale airplane pitch control for hovering is provided by the 
</p>
<p>- 11 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>scoops under the nose fan and height control, roll control, yaw control, and the forward 
</p>
<p>force required for transition are all provided by louvers under the wing fans. For the 
</p>
<p>model, pitch trim was provided by the scoops under the JOSe fan and the forward force 
</p>
<p>required for transition was provided by the wing-fan louvers, but all other control 
</p>
<p>(roll, yaw, and the additional pitch required for maneuvering) was provided by jet reac­
</p>
<p>tion controls at the wing tips and rear of the fuselage. The.object in using this jet 
</p>
<p>reaction type of control was to permit a determination of the basic stability and con­
</p>
<p>trollability of the fan-in-wing type of V/STOL airplane without the possibly confusing 
</p>
<p>effect of the particular novel type of control system of the XY-5A which wa·s quite diffi­
</p>
<p>cult to actuate mechanically on the small-scale model. 
</p>
<p>Hovering 
</p>
<p>Out of ground effect.- The model had unstable stick-fixed oscillations in both pitch 
</p>
<p>and roll as shown by the time histories of figure 16. These data show that the period of 
</p>
<p>the two oscillations and the degree of instability were fairly similar. The pilots 
</p>
<p>found, however, that the model was quite easy to control in pitch, but was very difficult 
</p>
<p>to control in roll - even with control power about each of the two axes set at the opti­
</p>
<p>mum value for that axis. It seemed that the reason for this difference was that the 
</p>
<p>model was much more sensitive to disturbances in roll than in pitch. This extreme sensi­
</p>
<p>tivity seemed to result from the fact that the model had a very high dihedral effect 
</p>
<p>(rolling moment due to sidewise velocity) and relatively low moment of inertia in roll. 
</p>
<p>The disturbances in this case were random fluctuations in the recirculating fan slip­
</p>
<p>stream in the large enclosure where the tests were made. No measurements have been made 
</p>
<p>with this, or any other model, to determine the degree of gustiness of the air in the 
</p>
<p>test area except by the qualitative observations of persons standing near the model. 
</p>
<p>From such observations, however, it seems that the velocity changes involved in the dis­
</p>
<p>turbances are probably small compared with those that would be encountered outdoors on a 
</p>
<p>gusty day, but they might have been more frequent than outdoor gust disturbances. 
</p>
<p>As part of the investigation of the rolling problem of this model, the moment of 
</p>
<p>inertia in roll was increased 30 percent in an attempt to reduce the response of the 
</p>
<p>- 12 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>model to disturbances. This change gave some improvement in the ease with which the 
</p>
<p>pilot could control the model in roll. There might be some question as to whether the 
</p>
<p>improvement in controllability of the model resulting from t~e increase in moment of 
</p>
<p>inertia resulted from a decreased sensitivity to disturbances or from an increase in the 
</p>
<p>period of the rolling oscillation. The period of the oscillation for the basic condition 
</p>
<p>was at least 4 seconds (model scale), however, which is not critically short, so the 
</p>
<p>increase in period would not seem to be the important factor. 
</p>
<p>As another part of the investigation of the problem in roll, chordwise fences were 
</p>
<p>installed on the upper surface of the wing just outboard of each wing fan. These fences, 
</p>
<p>which did not appreciably affect the static thrust, were 1~ inches high and extended over 
2 
</p>
<p>the middle 60 percent of the chord. The model pilot felt that the installation of these 
</p>
<p>fences resulted in the model being much easier to control in roll although the time his­
</p>
<p>tories of figure 17 show there was very little difference in the stability of the devel­
</p>
<p>oped stick-fixed motions with or without the fences. In fact, the period of the oscilla­
</p>
<p>tion was reduced somewhat with the fences in place. Apparently, then, the improved 
</p>
<p>flight behavior resulted from a reduction in the modeJ!'s sensitivity to a disturbance, 
</p>
<p>particularly the rolling moment due to sidewise velocity. The pilot also felt that a 
</p>
<p>contributing factor might have been that he required a high control sensitivity to con­
</p>
<p>tend with the erratic, large-amplitude motions of the basic model and this resulted in 
</p>
<p>some pilot-induced disturbances. The installation of the fences allowed a reduction in 
</p>
<p>the control sensitivity to a level where pilot overcontrolling was not a factor in the 
</p>
<p>model's flight behavior. 
</p>
<p>One further investigation was made of the problem in roll. The basic model was 
</p>
<p>equipped with artificial stabilization equipment to provide additional damping in roll 
</p>
<p>(rolling moment due to rolling velocity). It was found that by the addition of suffi­
</p>
<p>cient artificial damping in roll the rolling oscillation could be made completely stable 
</p>
<p>and the response to disturbances could be reduced to the point that the rolling motions 
</p>
<p>of the model became very easy to control. 
</p>
<p>- 13 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>No corresponding study of the pitching motions was made since it was felt that the 
</p>
<p>model was sufficiently easy to control in its basic condition without stability 
</p>
<p>augmentation. 
</p>
<p>The total control power available on the model to deal with these unstable motions 
</p>
<p>in both pitch and roll in hovering flight were those specified as being available on the 
</p>
<p>full-scale airplane which were also roughly the same as the control power specified in 
</p>
<p>the requirements of reference 12. The model control powers were found to be completely 
</p>
<p>adequate although a detaile~a,estigation of the minimum requirements was not made. 
</p>
<p>In ground effect.-✓Very little work has been done on the ground effects on the 
</p>
<p>X:V-5A model because of the difficulty of controlling the rolling motions which endangered 
</p>
<p>the model. A few take-off tests have been made, however. In these tests no appreciable 
</p>
<p>suck-down or lift-augmentation effect due to ground proximity was noticed. A ground 
</p>
<p>effect on pitching moment was very evident, however. When the model was trimmed for hov­
</p>
<p>ering flight out of ground effect, a very pronounced nose-up pitching moment was evident 
</p>
<p>when it was in ground effect. This pitching moment was evident as a marked tendency for 
</p>
<p>the model to pitch up and move backward as it broke ground on take-off. This nose-up 
</p>
<p>motion could be prevented by the pilot, however, by the use of nose-down pitch control 
</p>
<p>just as the model left the ground. 
</p>
<p>The nose-up pitching moment in ground effect is believed to result from the use of 
</p>
<p>the nose fan in the X:V-5A configuration and might not be typical of fan-in-wing config­
</p>
<p>urations in general. It seems that the use of the nose fan in conjunction with the wing 
</p>
<p>fans would cause a strong positive pressure on the underside of the fuselage forward of 
</p>
<p>the center of gravity because of the upward flow of the fan slipstreams at points between 
</p>
<p>the fans. 
</p>
<p>Transition 
</p>
<p>The only flights in the transition range made to date with the X:V-5A model have been 
</p>
<p>in the level-flight condition, and no descent conditions have been tested. 
</p>
<p>- 14 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>The longitudinal stability of the model in the transition range of flight is illus­
</p>
<p>trated in figure 18. This figure shows that the stick-fixed pitching oscillation which 
</p>
<p>had been noted in hovering became less unstable as the airspeed was increased and that 
</p>
<p>the model appeared to be stable at the highest speed reached in the tests. This speed is 
</p>
<p>approximately the speed at which the conversion to conventional wing-borne forward flight 
</p>
<p>could be made. The conversion was not actuallY made in the model tests, however, because 
</p>
<p>of the difficulty of making the conversion without changes in airspeed and height which 
</p>
<p>would be too abrupt to be accommodated in the limited confines and with the slow speed 
</p>
<p>control of the tunnel. 
</p>
<p>The rolling _motions of the model, which had been found to be so troublesome in hov­
</p>
<p>ering flight, became progressively easier to control as the airspeed was increased in the 
</p>
<p>transition range. Most of the flights were made with the roll stability augmentation 
</p>
<p>system operating and the improvement was evidenced mainlY in the need for a lesser degree 
</p>
<p>of artificial damping in roll as the speed increased. At the speed at which the conver­
</p>
<p>sion to normal forward flight could be accomplished, however, the rolling motions had 
</p>
<p>become essentiallY stable. It was not possible to tell whether they were actuallY 
</p>
<p>slightlY stable or slightlY unstable since the model could not be allowed to flY uncon­
</p>
<p>trolled for a sufficiently long period of time before it drifted sideways out of the test 
</p>
<p>section of the tunnel. 
</p>
<p>There are several interrelated problems of static stability and trim that, in combi­
</p>
<p>nation, can become critical on this particular configuration because it is operated close 
</p>
<p>to the border J.ine in several respects - and there is a way to relieve most of these 
</p>
<p>problems. The primary problems are (1) that the model in its normal. configuration was 
</p>
<p>just barely able to propel itself by deflecting the fan louvers to the speed at which it 
</p>
<p>could make the conversion to wing-borne flight, and (2) that it developed such large 
</p>
<p>nose-up pitching moments in transition that the pitch control was barely able to trim the 
</p>
<p>aircraft in the most critical region. The high drag that makes the propulsion critical 
</p>
<p>is caused mainlY by the momentum drag of the three fans which effectively take the static 
</p>
<p>free stream air and accelerate it to the forward speed of the airplane so that it can 
</p>
<p>- 15 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>flow axially through the horizontal fans. The nose-up pitching moment results from the 
</p>
<p>differential lift on the forward and rearward lips of the fans, which results from for­
</p>
<p>ward flight as explained in reference 23, and by a suction effect on the part of the wing 
</p>
<p>behind the fan exhaust stream, which is caused by the interference of the fan exhaust on 
</p>
<p>the free-stream flow as explained in reference 22. 
</p>
<p>At the higher speeds in the transition range where the drag problem is most criti­
</p>
<p>cal, the nose fan is adding to these problems and creating new ones of its own and is not 
</p>
<p>performing any useful function. In these conditions the nose fan causes added momentum 
</p>
<p>drag and it produces increments of static longitudinal and directional instability 
</p>
<p>because of its momentum drag. In this speed range the nose fan was not actually needed 
</p>
<p>for longitudinal trim and control since the horizontal tail had sufficient effectiveness; 
</p>
<p>and it was found to be advantageous to stop the nose fan and thereby eliminate the 
</p>
<p>problems. 
</p>
<p>The nose-up pitching-moment problem was most critical at fairly low speeds in the 
</p>
<p>transition where the horizontal tail did not have any appreciable effectiveness. The 
</p>
<p>nose fan had adequate power to compensate for the nose-up pitching moment provided it was 
</p>
<p>fitted with control scoops which deflected its exhaust upward through a large enough 
</p>
<p>angle to provide the required nose-down control moment. In the actual case, it was nec­
</p>
<p>essary to modify the control system to provide adequate nose-down pitch control. 
</p>
<p>JET-LIFT CONFIGURATION 
</p>
<p>Only one jet-lift V/STOL transport configuration has been covered in free-flight 
</p>
<p>tests - the Dornier 00-31 configuration shown in figure 19. This configuration is pow­
</p>
<p>ered by two vectored thrust engines (each with four rotating exhaust nozzles) mounted in 
</p>
<p>nacelles under the wing and by six lift engines mounted vertically, three to a pod, in 
</p>
<p>the wing-tip pods. Pitch and yaw control was provided by jet reaction controls at the 
</p>
<p>rear of the fuselage, and roll control was provided by differential throttling of the 
</p>
<p>engines in the wing-tip pods. Related information on the dynamic stability and control 
</p>
<p>of jet V/STOL aircraft has also been obtained in tests of four horizontal-attitude jet 
</p>
<p>- 16 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>V/STOL fighter models. These transport and fighter models have shown many common char­
</p>
<p>acteristics; so, even though direct experience with transport configurations has been 
</p>
<p>limited, certain generalizations as to the dynamic stability and control of. jet-lift 
</p>
<p>V/STOL transports can be made. 
</p>
<p>The most important generalization that can be made is that in none of the flying 
</p>
<p>model investigations made to date have any dynamic stability problems been discovered 
</p>
<p>except those which result directly from static stability and trim characteristics that 
</p>
<p>can be evaluated adequately from conventional static wind-tunnel tests. There are sta­
</p>
<p>bility and control problems such as (1) static longitudinal instability (or pitch up), 
</p>
<p>(2) static direc~ional instability wi~h inlets ahead of ·the center of gravity, (3) large 
</p>
<p>nose-up pitching moments caused by jet interference which must be trimmed out with the 
</p>
<p>controls, (4) large jet-induced lift losses such as those described in reference 22, and 
</p>
<p>(5) wing stalling as a result of jet-induced local flow conditions or simply by excessive 
</p>
<p>angle of attack; and all of these static stability and trim effects manifest themselves 
</p>
<p>as dynamic motions of the model, but all of them could be recognized and their serious­
</p>
<p>ness evaluated from conventional wind-tunnel tests on the basis of conventional airplane 
</p>
<p>experience and a few calculations. For this reason it does not seem to be desirable to 
</p>
<p>do any further free-flight model testing on jet V/STOL aircraft - except possibly as 
</p>
<p>cheap insurance in support of a specific airplane development program. 
</p>
<p>The D0-31 model was no exception to the foregoing generalities. The model was flown 
</p>
<p>in hovering, both level and descending transition conditions, and vertical take-offs and 
</p>
<p>landings. It was found that, given adequate control power in accordance with existing 
</p>
<p>requirements such as reference 12, the model could be flown easily in the hovering and 
</p>
<p>transition ranges of flight without artificial stabilization. This is not to say that it 
</p>
<p>can be expected that the full-scale airplane could be flown on instruments for all­
</p>
<p>weather approaches without artificial stabilization, but rather that it should be possi­
</p>
<p>ble to fly the airplane satisfactorily under visual conditions without stability 
</p>
<p>augmentation. 
</p>
<p>- 17 -</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Short take-offs and landings were also made with this model with no evidence of any 
</p>
<p>dynamic problems. These tests were made on the control-line facility described in ref­
</p>
<p>erence 2 which permits the model the three longitudinal degrees of freedom, but restrains 
</p>
<p>it in the three lateral degrees of freedom. If there were any dynamic problems peculiar 
</p>
<p>to STOL operation it would be expected that they would be in the form of abrupt pitching 
</p>
<p>motions or losses of height or excessive landing flare, but no such behavior was evident 
</p>
<p>for the model. 
</p>
<p>CONCIBSION 
</p>
<p>The foregoing discussion has shown that propeller- .and lift-fan-powered V/STOL 
</p>
<p>transport types have. certain dynamic stability problems (mainly unstable stick-fixed 
</p>
<p>oscillations) in hovering and low-speed flight, but that these instabilities can be con­
</p>
<p>trolled by the pilot without the use of artificial stabilization - although artificial 
</p>
<p>stabilization is quite helpful. As the airspeed approaches that required for normal 
</p>
<p>wing-borne flight, these instabilities are markedly reduced and usually disappear. In 
</p>
<p>the approach condition for the tilt-wing type, that is, in descending flight in the tran­
</p>
<p>sition speed range, very poor dynamic behavior can result from the tendency of the wing 
</p>
<p>to stall if the aircraft is not properly designed to avoid this difficulty; and this poor 
</p>
<p>behavior limits the descent angles that the pilot is willing to use. The jet V/STOL 
</p>
<p>types have been found to have no real dynamic stability problems other than those associ­
</p>
<p>ated with static stability and trim characteristics such as pitch-up, directional insta­
</p>
<p>bility, and excessive pitch trim requirements which can be determined by ordinary wind­
</p>
<p>tunnel tests and interpreted adequately without the need for special dynamic tests. 
</p>
