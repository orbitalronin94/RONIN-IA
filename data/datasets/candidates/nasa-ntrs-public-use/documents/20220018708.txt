2022 Edition

JANNAF Spacecraft Propulsion Subcommittee, MicroPropulsion Panel 
 

Spacecraft developers place considerable emphasis on stated Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs) 
to establish system-level maturity; however, the methodology for determining TRL is often 
inconsistent across various technologies.  In 2019, the MicroPropulsion Panel of the JANNAF Spacecraft 
Propulsion Subcommittee developed a set of TRL guidelines for the micro-propulsion community. That 
work expanded on the general guidance provided by Department of Defense (DoD) and the National 
Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) documentation and sought to provide a consistent 
metric by which TRLs may be assigned to spacecraft micro-propulsion systems. Since the release of the 
2019 guide, community feedback is motivating an updated edition of the guidelines to provide 
clarifications on critical TRL entry and exit criteria as well as to facilitate continued usage of the TRL 
guidelines.  

Introduction 
Technology Readiness Levels (TRLs) were first formulated in the mid-1970s to assist the National 

Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in the technology development process in order to improve 
program risk management during a period of declining budgets.1 Their primary purpose is to provide coherent 
communications between technologists and program managers regarding the maturity of technologies for 
transition, particularly with respect to the risks associated with the injection of developing technology into 
programs. Since then, the TRL process has evolved into a critical tool that provides a common understanding 
of science and technology development exit criteria. 

Government Accounting Office (GAO) studies following the military buildup of the 1980s discovered that 
the Department of Defense (DoD) was injecting considerably greater risk than NASA into its programs due to a 
reliance on less well-developed technologies; ultimately, the GAO found that maturity of a technology at 
program inception for a weapons system was one of the most important determinants of program success.2 In 
response during the 1990s, the DoD adopted a similar TRL process as a risk management tool to contain 
unexpected cost growth and to ensure program success.3 Other entities — such as the European Space Agency 
(ESA),4 the Department of Energy (DOE),5 and the petroleum industry6 — have also adopted TRL processes to 
ensure the smooth transition of new technologies and to manage the associated inherent risk. 

In the DoD federal acquisition process, there exists multiple points of entry for new technology during the 
lifetime of a program.7,8 These opportunities primarily lie in the concept refinement and technology 
development stages of a DoD program life cycle. As shown in Figure 1, both of these stages are considered to 
be Pre-Systems Acquisition, and therefore relatively low-level technologies should be developed prior to 
program initiation at Milestone B. Following program initiation, there are still opportunities for technology 
insertion prior to Milestone C, which precedes low-rate initial production (LRIP) and operational test and 
evaluation (OT&E).8 

DoD and NASA acquisition processes are risk adverse by design, but this is especially true for spacecraft 
acquisition due to limited production cycles. With regard to the ideal project life cycle, the final product of a 
DoD or NASA spacecraft program is usually considered no further than what might be considered LRIP for more 
common systems. As a result, technology transition opportunities are reduced for DoD and NASA programs, 
leading to an emphasis on system development and demonstration from the technologist’s point of view.

Micro-Propulsion 

The advent of micro-spacecraft (100-kg class), nano-spacecraft (10-kg class), and even pico-spacecraft (1-
kg class) in the past decade as low-cost platforms for space access may change the paradigm of DoD and NASA 
spaceflight. These systems are low cost, can be launched as secondary or tertiary payloads, and have 
considerable potential as responsive space platforms. Due to their lower cost, it is plausible that greater 
numbers of these spacecraft could be launched for large, dispersed architectures. However at present, the 
emphasis for non-commercial micro- and nano-spacecraft appears to be responsive designs customized to 
specific missions.9 

The utility of these micro- and nano-spacecraft is often highly dependent on propulsion. In some cases, 
propulsion is not strictly necessary, but such spacecraft are derided at times as being comparable to flying bricks 
with limited mission utility. Earth orbits with altitudes of less than 1,000 km exhibit substantial drag, which 
limits lifetime and distorts constellations. Without propulsion, low Earth orbit micro- and nano-spacecraft will 
soon drift from their assigned orbits and eventually disintegrate in the atmosphere.10 For example, a cubesat 
with an initial altitude of 450 km will have a lifetime of only approximately a year before reentry ends its 
mission.11 

Driven by this need, a variety of propulsion concepts suitable for highly constrained micro- and nano-
spacecraft have recently emerged and are presently under development.12,13 The propulsion concepts are often 
completely new technologies that do not have flight heritage, due to the platforms’ severe constraints on power, 
mass, and volume. Furthermore, scaling to very small sizes oftentimes produces unintended consequences. For 
example, very small nozzles can produce severe viscous losses if the Reynolds number (Re) of the flow is below 
1000.14 Such effects are often overlooked by propulsion experts when they have not had to previously deal with 
these size scales. As a result, estimates based on simple extrapolative theory or parametric scaling can be 
ruinously deceptive. Scaling issues not only crop up with thruster head technology, but they can also be an issue 
with other propulsion system components. For example, conventional valves can require several watts of 
holding power, a difficult proposition on a small spacecraft with only a few watts available for an entire micro-
propulsion system. Thus, scaling valves and other critical components for micro-propulsion systems is not 
necessarily a straightforward process, often requiring entirely new design approaches due to the severe micro- 
and nano-spacecraft power, mass, and volume constraints. 

By virtue of their small size, micro-propulsion systems have several advantages that facilitate faster 
technological development cycles, permitting new technologies to be rapidly fielded. The small scale may 
enable the technology to be developed less expensively and with assemblies that can be more easily tested. 
This is advantageous since many of the spacecraft utilizing micro-propulsion are secondary or tertiary payloads 
that may not adversely impact the launch schedule or risk posture of the primary payload. Such non-primary 
payloads and experiments are presently required to have very large safety factors.11 However, these safety 
factors will likely be reduced as micro- and nano-spacecraft evolve into operational platforms and users 
demand higher-performance propulsion systems. One can easily envision a time when these advantages could 
lead to constellations of DoD or NASA micro- or nano-spacecraft manifested on dedicated launchers.

TRL Vagaries and Uncertainties 

It has been long acknowledged that consistent guidance on TRL assessment is lacking.15 TRLs are presently 
seen as overarching definitions for all technologies in the acquisition process and hence open to considerable 
interpretation. Prior to the 2019 edition of this guide, there was no standard or common approach for applying 
TRLs to micro-propulsion short of the overarching definitions presented in the DoD and NASA 
documentation.3,16 Without a process to vet self-evaluated TRLs, presentations at conferences regularly 
demonstrate inconsistent TRL assignments, with TRLs being assigned at times casually to components, 
subsystems, and systems. Due to the competitive nature of the micro-propulsion field, it becomes readily 
apparent that TRLs are oftentimes overstated, sometimes quite dramatically. It is also obvious that a standard 
set of TRL guidelines for micro-propulsion is needed to ensure uniform application to the community, where 
potential users abound and new technologies are constantly being presented and proposed. However, this 
poses the fundamental question: How are potential users to critically evaluate the maturity of emerging micro-
propulsion technologies? 

Differing priorities between the DoD and NASA also contribute to differences in TRL wording. The DoD has 
larger production runs for various systems; as a result, the DoD has developed subtly different TRL 
requirements, particularly at the higher TRL designations. While NASA TRL designations have substantial 
overlap with DOD TRLs, harmonization between the two systems is not always straightforward and has 
produced some uncertainty in how TRLs are applied in common fields such as spacecraft propulsion. 

Goals of this Work 

DoD and NASA TRL guidance explicitly calls for experts in the field to interpret TRLs for specific 
technologies. This work is an interpretation of TRLs for micro-propulsion systems. The authors are only 
choosing the narrow technology class of micro-propulsion, which is being defined as primary propulsion 
suitable for a micro- (100-kg class) or nano- (10-kg class) spacecraft. Propulsion technologies under 
consideration in this definition range from cold gas and small chemical to small electric thrusters. 

The authors furthermore posit that it is the complete micro-propulsion system’s TRL, rather than 
component TRLs, that is of greatest utility to the potential user. While component TRLs have utility within the 
micro-propulsion development community, the spacecraft developer and end-user are rarely interested in 
component-level maturity, as the complete micro-propulsion system is the product. As such, undue emphasis 
on component maturity does not ultimately facilitate the transition of micro-propulsion technology to the 
users. Micro-propulsion systems are small and should generally be highly integrated to be considered for flight. 
Propulsion subsystems such as power processing, command and control architecture, and telemetry retrieval 
are just as critical for system-level performance as the thruster head. In fact, the thruster head can be 
significantly less complex than the propellant management assembly (PMA), the power processing unit (PPU), 
or other supporting elements of the micro-propulsion system. The nature of micro- and nano-spacecraft thus 
pushes spacecraft developers to evaluate complete systems. Small spacecraft developers generally do not have 
access to, or cannot afford, cross-disciplinary teams with specialized expertise to evaluate micro-propulsion 
system maturity and integration requirements; meanwhile, end-users tend to focus on the mission and rarely 
have the resources to mature nascent micro-propulsion technologies. 

Performance verification is crucial in a true evaluation of micro-propulsion system suitability. Micro- and 
nano-spacecraft development programs, either from limited operational experience or funding, tend to 
leverage an abbreviated acquisition process and generally require their individual systems to verify most 
qualification factors such as performance, environment, and thermal testing. The authors believe that 
performance — including thrust, lifetime, and propulsive efficiency — must be measured at the system level. 
As noted previously, early estimates of performance can be misleading due to the small scales or novel physics 
of the micro-propulsion systems under development. Adequate environments must also be used for evaluation, 
as the vacuum environment includes issues such as the effect of back-pressure, thermal exchange, and electrical 
power transfer. For low-density plasma thrusters, the back-pressure of the vacuum test facility can cause 
considerable uncertainty in the performance and subtle changes in the operation of the thruster.17,18 Thermal 
effects due to heat generation and dissipation can increase performance of some thrusters or produce failures 
in some PPUs. In some cases, the placement of PPUs relative to the thruster head can affect system efficiency 
dramatically. Such issues demonstrate a clear need for a system-level performance verification. 

The environment can also be construed to include the space environment beyond what can be produced in 
the laboratory or test facility. The thermal, vacuum, and microgravity effects on the micro-propulsion system 
must be considered and ultimately tested prior to obtaining full confidence in the system performance. Launch

The purpose of this work is to present micro-propulsion system TRL guidelines with critical entry and exit 
criteria clearly identified. This presentation is limited to the narrow micro-propulsion technology class as 
defined earlier with TRL designated for the complete propulsion system. The authors feel that this effort will 
provide the micro-propulsion community with subject matter expert interpretation of the generalized DoD and 
NASA TRL guidelines while providing a framework to have thoughtful discussions regarding TRL assignments. 
Ultimately, the authors desire to standardize the application of the TRLs to micro-propulsion systems. This will 
allow potential users to more accurately assess the risks associated with implementation of micro-propulsion 
systems and thereby further enable the fielding of micro- and nano-spacecraft with capable propulsion.  

Defined Technology Readiness Levels 
DoD Technology Readiness Levels 

The guidance used in the work for the micro-propulsion system interpretation of TRLs is taken from the 
DoD Technology Readiness Assessment (TRA) Guidance dated 13 May 2011 as prepared by the Assistant 
Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering (ASD(R&E)).3 Table 1 summarizes the DoD TRL definitions. 
It is immediately obvious that the DoD guidance provided by this overarching document is broad and not 
specific to any particular technology class. Also, the DoD guidance is centered on the system rather than 
component level. This supports an emphasis on the complete micro-propulsion system but more critically 
illustrates the need for technology-dependent interpretation of TRLs. 

NASA Technology Readiness Levels 

The NASA TRL guidance is provided by NASA Procedural Requirements (NPR) 7123.1C (NASA Systems 
Engineering Processes and Requirements) dated 14 February 2020 as prepared by NASA’s Office of the Chief 
Engineer. Table 2 summarizes the NASA TRL definitions. Like the DoD guidance, the NASA TRL guidance is also 
broad and not specific to any particular technology class.16 Unlike the DoD guidance, the NASA TRL guidance 
specifically calls out hardware and software descriptions, thus providing detail beyond the scope of the DoD 
guidance. The particular emphasis on software recognizes that software integration can be a high hurdle for 
integrated systems. As with the DoD designations, the NASA TRL designations are centered on the system and 
not the component level. 

Comparing DoD and NASA Technology Readiness Levels 

To a large extent, the TRL definitions are very similar between the DoD and NASA. As seen in Tables 1 and 
2, the first column is essentially identical for both sets of designations, showing their common heritage. 
However, there are some global differences that are immediately obvious. First is NASA’s breakout of the 
hardware and software components of a complete system. While the increased emphasis on software in the 
NASA TRL designations is appreciated, this separation has the potential to result in differing NASA TRLs for the 
hardware and software components of a system. In such a scenario, in keeping with the spirit of assessing the 
TRL of the complete micro-propulsion system, the lower of the TRL designations (i.e., the ”weakest link” 
concept) should be used to reflect the system TRL. The second global difference that is readily visible is the 
increased emphasis on reporting and documentation in NASA TRL designations as compared to the DoD TRL 
designations. This increased NASA emphasis on documentation is crucial, and while similar emphasis is hinted 
at in the DoD designations, the authors prefer the NASA emphasis on documentation called out as specific exit 
criteria. The use of standardized terminology such as breadboard and brassboard hardware in the NASA 
designation is also preferred to that of the DoD, which calls out low-fidelity and high-fidelity hardware. Table 3 
summarizes such standardized NASA terminology as defined by NASA Procedural Requirements (NPR) 
7120.8A (NASA Research and Technology Program and Project Management Requirements) dated 14 
September 2018 as prepared by NASA’s Office of the Chief Engineer.19 Unless otherwise stated, the definitions 
in Table 3 are used in the remainder of this document.

Anecdotal accounts have previously claimed that the DoD and NASA TRL designations diverge at TRL 4 and 

subsequently re-converge at TRL 7. A close examination of the TRL definitions provided in Tables 1 and 2 does 
not support a substantial difference between the two sets of TRL designations. The development curve in the 
underlying documents is not steeper for the NASA TRL designations than for those of the DoD. In fact, the DoD 
and NASA definitions appear to be remarkably consistent. While some global differences in the definitions have 
been previously noted, as well as some differing emphasis on documentation, these differences can be 
interpreted as institutional biases based on different institutional missions. The DoD is typically interested in 
larger production runs, block production with incremental technology insertion, and the potential for future 
upgrades of existing hardware; the DoD also has a higher emphasis on reducing total life-cycle costs due to the 
need to deal with a large, long-lived, and diverse logistics base. NASA’s mission of remote scientific exploration 
results in a greater emphasis on software-hardware integration; in addition, NASA constructs a smaller number 
of larger, more integrated systems. The subtle differences in TRL designations shown in Tables 1 and 2 can be 
attributed to these differences in mission. Close examination finds these differences to be relatively 
insignificant, and the authors believe that the two sets of TRL designations are complementary. 

Thus, both sets of TRL designations have been consulted in defining TRLs for micro-propulsion systems. For 
the integrated micro-propulsion system, NASA software TRL designations are used to provide milestones for 
system software development. 

Micro-Propulsion Technology Readiness Levels 
The following sections will detail the authors’ interpretations of TRLs for micro-propulsion systems. Rather 

than focus on any specific micro-propulsion technology (e.g., electric, chemical, tether, solar sail, etc.), this work 
attempts to provide general guidance directly related to micro-propulsion systems based on the DoD TRL 
guidance provided in Table 1 and the NASA TRL guidance provided in Table 2. Note that there is a bias toward 
systems that produce thrust using propellant acceleration, but the TRL interpretation is generally suitable for 
propellantless concepts as well.

Technology Readiness Level 1  

DoD guidance calls out TRL 1 as: 

Lowest level of technology readiness. Scientific research begins to be translated into applied research and 
development (R&D). Examples might include paper studies of a technology’s basic properties. 

While NASA guidance calls out (hardware / software) TRL 1 as: 

Scientific knowledge generated underpinning hardware technology concepts / applications. 

Scientific knowledge generated underpinning basic properties of software architecture and mathematical 
formulation. 

TRL 1 is interpreted in this guideline as the formulation of a micro-propulsion concept. This TRL is nearly 
exclusively focused on the thruster and propellant acceleration mechanism. The propellant acceleration (or 
alternatively, momentum transfer for propellantless concepts) is clearly defined and is compared to relevant 
literature. TRL 1 includes speculation based on fundamental principles. 

At TRL 1, there may be some extrapolation toward a system in a notional sense, but research is primarily 
focused on the thruster’s propellant acceleration. Specifically, propellant storage and feed systems are not 
addressed, nor is power management, even though these subsystems have significant impact on micro-
propulsion system performance. As this is the lowest TRL, no measurements are expected, and the research is 
limited to paper studies and observations from available literature. 

TRL 1 is the Eureka! moment and the immediate period thereafter when the propulsion concept is first 
formulated. Systems are not critical; merely the possibility of a new propulsion concept is considered. It is 
important to understand that this is a transitory TRL that is quickly surpassed. Few concepts will linger at TRL 
1, unless they are far in advance of what can be produced using available manufacturing techniques or what 
can be demonstrated. 

The key properties that must be fulfilled for a micro-propulsion technology to be considered at TRL 1 are 
as follows: 

 
• Propellant acceleration mechanism is identified. 

• Propulsion concept’s similarity to relevant literature is demonstrated. 

• Research is limited to paper studies or observations of prior work. 

• Extrapolation of a concept for thrust generation capability is made.

exclusively focused on the propellant acceleration mechanism. Thruster head scaling should be considered 
analytically to ensure that the concept has merit for a potential application. The system is only considered to 
the extent that a notional block diagram identifying the critical subsystems is formulated; notional propellant 
storage, propellant feed, power sources, and other subsystems should be identified. 

Another critical parameter for TRL 2 is an analysis of thruster head performance. Critical performance 
parameters include thrust levels as well as derived quantities such specific impulse (Isp) and, in the case of 
electric thrusters, power efficiency (η). At this TRL, it is acceptable to estimate these values from fundamental 
physical principles; direct measurements are not required. Since the system is only crudely defined and the 
subsystems are not considered in detail, issues such as power processing efficiencies (if applicable) do not need 
to be considered. 

TRL 2 will be the default for many new micro-propulsion concepts early in development. Most concepts will 
not be presented prior to TRL 2 simply due to the natural engineering process. It is important to note that TRL 
2 is the point at which serious technical and scientific inquiry begins. At this stage, the fundamental micro-
propulsion concept is fleshed out, and the major subsystems are identified. 

The key properties that must be fulfilled for a micro-propulsion technology to be considered TRL 2 are as 
follows: 

 
• Propellant acceleration mechanism is observed directly or from literature. 

• Subsystems and their general functions are identified based on application. 

• Thruster performance is estimated parametrically or from first principles. 

Technology Readiness Level 3  

DoD guidance calls out TRL 3 as: 

Active R&D is initiated. This includes analytical studies and laboratory studies to physically validate the 
analytical predictions of separate elements of the technology. Examples include components that are not yet 
integrated or representative. 

While NASA guidance calls out (hardware / software) TRL 3 as: 

Research and development are initiated, including analytical and laboratory studies to validate predictions 
regarding the technology. 

Development of limited functionality to validate critical properties and predictions using non-integrated 
software components. 

TRL 3 is interpreted to include all criteria of TRL 2 as well as a number of additional criteria. The most 
important criterion is the direct measurement with quantified uncertainty analysis of performance. 
Performance is defined as thrust, specific impulse (Isp) or impulse bit (Ibit), and thruster head power efficiency 
(most applicable to electric thrusters). These measurements require active experimental research and 
development programs supported by analytical and computational studies to optimize thruster head 
performance relevant to the intended micro-propulsion application. It is believed that a nominal operating 
performance of the thruster head is determined at this stage of development. 

For TRL 3, the test environment’s suitability should also be confirmed via analysis as a part of the active 
research process. Since many micro-propulsion thrusters require testing in vacuum for representative 
measurements, it is critical that the test facility be analyzed with regard to thruster and support subsystems 
operation. For example, analytical checks of the suitability of the background pressure for nozzles or plasma 
discharges should be performed. Furthermore, it is critical that thruster and subsystem interactions with 
measurements be identified and eliminated as sources of uncertainty early in the development process. 

For many micro-propulsion thrusters, lifetime can be a concern. Small scales coupled to high energies, via 
chemical or plasma processes, can potentially limit useful lifetime of these devices. Understanding how these 
thrusters age and ultimately fail provides a quick check on the expected lifetime for the micro-propulsion 
thrusters. At this stage of development, thruster life-limiting mechanisms should be identified based on limited 



 

10 of 20 

laboratory operating experience. From these observations and models, thruster lifetime is estimated. Lifetime 
should be quantified as an unambiguous metric to potential users. For example, the total delivered impulse for 
a specific duty cycle at the nominal operational condition more easily allows for the direct comparison of both 
pulsed and continuous systems. 

At TRL 3, the notions of flight subsystems may be refined based on laboratory experience. However, it is 
expected that at this early stage of system development, subsystems (e.g., power processing and propellant 
feed) may be limited to laboratory apparatus. In many cases, such subsystems can be identified for future 
development via the use of laboratory surrogates. It should be noted that in some cases, specialized subsystems 
may be required if they are necessary for adequate performance measurements. 

It is the authors’ opinion that some technology self-assessments placing micro-propulsion technologies at 
TRL 3 are incorrect. For many micro-propulsion concepts, performance has not been measured in a suitable 
environment, and estimates of useful lifetime have not been quantified. These metrics are needed in the field 
of micro-propulsion to demonstrate a realistic and applicable development program for potential users. 

The key properties that must be fulfilled for a micro-propulsion technology to be considered at TRL 3 are 
as follows: 

 
• Performance (e.g., thrust, Isp, Ibit, etc.) is directly measured with quantified uncertainty estimates at an 

operational mode closely approximating intended flight operations. 

• Ranges of thruster head performance are measured to determine optimization. 

• Thruster life-limiting mechanisms are identified, and thruster lifetime is estimated from limited 
measurements and models. 

• Thruster lifetime is expressed in unambiguous engineering units (total impulse suggested). 

• Test environment suitability is verified via analysis and models. 

• Subsystems are identified via laboratory surrogates. 

Technology Readiness Level 4  

DoD guidance calls out TRL 4 as: 

Basic technological components are integrated to establish that they will work together. This is relatively 
low fidelity compared to the eventual system. Examples include integration of ad hoc hardware in the 
laboratory. 

While NASA guidance calls out (hardware / software) TRL 4 as: 

A low-fidelity system / component breadboard is built and operated to demonstrate basic functionality in a 
laboratory environment. 

Key, functionality-critical software components are integrated and functionally validated to establish 
interoperability and begin architecture development. Relevant environments defined and performance in 
the environment predicted. 

TRL 4 is interpreted to include all criteria of TRL 3 as well as a number of additional criteria. It is at this 
stage that a system architecture emerges from the previous research and development program. Also, it is now 
that the concept of breadboard emerges for the subsystems. Both the DoD and NASA define a breadboard as the 
integrated components that provide a representation of the system / subsystem functionality. As defined in 
Table 3, breadboards may resemble the final subsystem in function alone without respect to form or fit. It 
should be noted that the development in TRL 3 was focused on the breadboard development of the thruster 
head. Now, achieving TRL 4 requires that the other propulsion subsystems be matured similarly.  

With the shift in focus to the complete propulsion system, it is important at this stage of development for 
the technologists to engage with end-users in defining requirements (i.e., performance, interfaces, 
environmental, safety, etc.) for the micro-propulsion technology. These requirements will allow for meaningful 
TRL 5 and TRL 6 verification activities described below. Otherwise, in the absence of well-defined requirements 



 

11 of 20 

provided by stakeholders, blindly satisfying TRL 5 and TRL 6 criteria may simply result in a micro-propulsion 
technology that cannot be utilized onboard missions of interest. In the case of a technology-push scenario in 
which end-users may not be readily identifiable, the authors recommend that the technologist at a minimum 
formulate a design reference mission (DRM) that envisions usage of the technology and derive the 
requirements accordingly.  

At this stage of development, a representative micro-propulsion system is assembled from breadboard 
subsystems. The subsystems include at a minimum the thruster head, propellant management subsystem 
(storage and distribution), and rudimentary command and control. The inclusion of other subsystems is 
dependent on the micro-propulsion architecture. The breadboard subsystems have the function of the 
perceived final function of each subsystem, and these functions should be clearly understood and implemented. 
The appropriate test environment, with suitability confirmed by analysis, should be used for each 
demonstration discussed below. 

At the breadboard level of fidelity, the system is to demonstrate functionality and performance to a nominal 
level. The breadboard subsystems are then optimized with respect to system-level performance. The micro-
propulsion system is able to operate with its functional breadboard subsystems such that total power 
consumption, volume, and mass can be measured or estimated with explicitly defined parametric scaling. For 
electric micro-propulsion thrusters, the breadboard subsystems should allow for the electrical efficiency of the 
entire micro-propulsion system (i.e., wall plug efficiency) to be measured with quantifiable uncertainties. 

Another crucial factor in reaching TRL 4 is conducting long-duration firings of the thruster head in order to 
produce a high-fidelity estimate of thruster lifetime. Due to the relatively small tankage of most micro-
propulsion systems, a complete life test may be completed, or alternatively, an estimate of the total thruster 
head lifetime based on extrapolations of experimental measurements may be performed and documented. 
Early determination of thruster lifetime capability is critical to justifying further investment to maturing the 
micro-propulsion technology. The previous edition of this paper recommended that long-duration firings 
should demonstrate at a minimum 25%, and preferably 50% or greater, of the desired propellant throughput 
of the micro-propulsion system.20 For electric propulsion technologies whose operational lifetimes are 
expected to be on the order of weeks or months, this recommendation may pose a severe burden at this stage 
of technology development; in such cases, it may be reasonable to consider conducting long-duration firings 
sufficiently long to explore and document major failure modes. As previously stated, lifetime should be 
expressed in unambiguous engineering units such as total impulse (N-s) as a function of propellant throughput 
(kg). In addition, the selected methodology for estimating thruster head lifetime should be documented for 
future reference and made available for review. 

The definition of propulsion system requirements based on end-user inputs or DRMs means that TRL 4 is 
the stage at which the propulsion technology’s plume behavior can start to be assessed in terms of potential 
impacts on a host spacecraft, whose configuration and interfaces are delineated during requirements 
formulation. While spacecraft-plume interactions are generally a spacecraft-level consideration that strongly 
depends on a mission’s concept of operations and operational environment, plume measurements of the micro-
propulsion system at applicable operating points should be conducted at TRL 4 to supporting anchoring and 
refinement of these spacecraft-level analyses and models (e.g., sputtering / redeposition, contamination, 
spacecraft charging, etc.). 

From this interpretation of TRL 4, quite a few technology self-assessments at TRL 4 are overly optimistic. 
Much of this overstatement is due to a dependence on demonstrating system capability via analysis or scaling, 
based on limited experimental demonstrations. It is the authors’ position that for micro-propulsion, where the 
systems are small, are comparatively inexpensive, and require relatively short lifetimes, most milestones must 
be actually demonstrated for TRL advancement. Meeting technical capability via analysis or scaling should be 
viewed as no better than a conditional demonstration, and only as a first step to actual demonstration. This 
philosophy is advantageous to micro-propulsion since it allows for the unambiguous demonstration and 
quantification of the requirements. In fact, the authors feel that ideally, statistical demonstration of critical 
parameters is required for maximum system-level confidence. TRL 4, 5, and 6 are system-focused, so placing 
initial system metrics in TRL 4 provides the groundwork and definitions for the final system-level parameters. 
As a result, the system-level parameters introduced at TRL 4 are all the more critical.

The key properties that must be fulfilled for a micro-propulsion technology to be considered at TRL 4 are 
as follows: 

 
• Focus shifts from thruster head to overall system performance. 

• Micro-propulsion system requirements are defined with end-user engagement or derived from a design 
reference mission (DRM). 

• System architecture is completely identified. 

• All subsystems are demonstrated for functionality at breadboard-level fidelity. 

• Micro-propulsion system is integrated and demonstrated at breadboard-level fidelity in an appropriate 
environment. 

• Significant portion of thruster head lifetime is demonstrated (50% or greater recommended, or sufficient 
to explore major failure modes). 

• Thruster lifetime is estimated based on long-duration tests; any required extrapolation is reviewed and 
documented. 

• Thruster performance is measured at beginning of life (BoL) and estimated for end of life (EoL).  

• Thruster plume behavior is estimated from limited measurements and models. 

Technology Readiness Level 5  

DoD guidance calls out TRL 5 as: 

Fidelity of breadboard technology increases significantly. The basic technological components are 
integrated with reasonably realistic supporting elements so they can be tested in a simulated environment. 
Examples include high-fidelity laboratory integration of components. 

While NASA guidance calls out (hardware / software) TRL 5 as: 

A medium-fidelity component and/or brassboard, with realistic support elements, is built and operated for 
validation in a relevant environment so as to demonstrate overall performance in critical areas. 

End-to-end software elements implemented and interfaced with existing systems / simulations conforming 
to target environment. End-to-end software system tested in relevant environment and meeting predicted 
performance. Operational environment performance predicted. Prototype implementations developed. 

TRL 5 is interpreted to include all criteria of TRL 4 as well as a number of additional criteria. Reaching TRL 
5 involves the finalization of the subsystems’ designs. For micro-propulsion, the realistic support elements 
stated in the DoD and NASA TRL guidance is interpreted to include system form, fit, and function due to the 
special engineering constraints of micro-spacecraft and most especially nano-spacecraft. In particular, thruster 
head design is finalized as appropriate for flight without further modification or optimization. Intrinsically, the 
thruster head’s nominal operating conditions should be clearly identified. In addition, the thruster head design 
should also be analyzed to be appropriate for launch and operational environments. These environments 
include vibrational, thermal, vacuum, radiation, and others as appropriate. For TRL 5, thruster head 
survivability of these environments should be clearly documented.  

To reach TRL 5 designation, the other subsystem breadboards are also upgraded to brassboard designs. 
Brassboard-level subsystems have the function and start to approach the form and fit of final flight subsystems, 
including mass, power consumption, and interfaces (mechanical, thermal, etc.). Furthermore, all subsystems 
should utilize hardware control interfaces equivalent to that anticipated on the flight system. These control 
interfaces should be fully implemented and demonstrated through a model spacecraft bus simulator (e.g., 
voltage ranges of an unregulated spacecraft bus). 

An integrated micro-propulsion system, composed of all the subsystems at brassboard-level fidelity, should 
be demonstrated in a simulated operational environment deemed to be functionally equivalent to flight. 



 

13 of 20 

System-level performance of the brassboard micro-propulsion system should be measured. Parameters of 
interest include thruster head performance (thrust, Isp, Ibit, etc.) as well as relevant subsystem performance 
parameters (e.g., propellant flow rate, power consumption, timing, etc.) with quantified uncertainty estimates. 
To the greatest extent possible, the subsystems for TRL 5 and TRL 6 should undergo flight-like integration 
rather than be loosely coupled for these demonstrations. For micro-propulsion technologies, which are 
generally more tightly integrated than larger propulsion systems where the subsystems are often separated, 
not having tight integration may lead to a distorted understanding of system-level impacts (such as thermal).  

As TRL 4 involves conducting long-duration firings of the thruster head, reaching TRL 5 involves producing 
a high-fidelity estimate of the propulsion system lifetime. The brassboard propulsion system should be operated 
for sufficiently long to explore and document major failure modes, with test data used to anchor predictive 
models. As previously stated, lifetime should be expressed in unambiguous engineering units such as total 
impulse (N-s) as a function of propellant throughput (kg). In addition, the selected methodology for estimating 
propulsion system lifetime should be documented for future reference and made available for review. 

Reaching TRL 5 ends true technical development and sets the stage for technical demonstration. Key to 
achieving TRL 5 is finalizing designs of all subsystems and demonstrating their system functionality in a 
simulated operational environment. The use of brassboard-level hardware allows for the realistic 
demonstration of control interfaces. This stage of development also requires that the potential impact of the 
propulsion system (e.g., thermal soakback, electromagnetic interference, plume contamination / charging, etc.) 
on the host spacecraft be quantified. Should the propulsion system potentially interfere unacceptably with the 
host spacecraft, this is the development level at which required changes should be made. For micro-propulsion 
systems, reaching TRL 5 is important for the end-user in order to judge appropriateness and effectiveness in a 
realistic form factor.  

The key properties that must be fulfilled for a micro-propulsion technology to be considered at TRL 5 are 
as follows: 

 
• Thruster head design is finalized as appropriate for flight. 

• All subsystem designs are upgraded to brassboard-level fidelity with control interfaces implemented 
through a model spacecraft simulator. 

• Integrated (as flight-like as possible) brassboard system performance is verified in a simulated flight 
environment. 

• Significant portion of propulsion system lifetime is demonstrated (50% or greater recommended, or 
sufficient to explore major failure modes). 

• Propulsion system lifetime is estimated based on long-duration tests; any required extrapolation is 
reviewed and documented. 

• Propulsion system performance is measured at beginning of life (BoL) and estimated for end of life (EoL).  

• System impact (e.g., thermal soakback, electromagnetic interference, plume contamination, etc.) on host 
spacecraft is quantified. 

Technology Readiness Level 6  

DoD guidance calls out TRL 6 as: 

Representative model or prototype system, which is well beyond that of TRL 5, is tested in a relevant 
environment. Represents a major step up in a technology’s demonstrated readiness. Examples include testing 
a prototype in a high-fidelity laboratory environment or in a simulated operational environment. 

While NASA guidance calls out (hardware / software) TRL 6 as: 

A high-fidelity prototype of the system / subsystems that adequately addresses all critical scaling issues is 
built and tested in a relevant environment to demonstrate performance under critical environmental 
conditions. 



 

14 of 20 

Prototype implementations of the software demonstrated on full-scale, realistic problems. Partially 
integrated with existing hardware / software systems. Limited documentation available. Engineering 
feasibility fully demonstrated. 

TRL 6 is interpreted to include all criteria of TRL 5 as well as a number of additional criteria. The authors 
have interpreted the requirements for TRL 6 to be a demonstration of the integrated prototype system in a 
relevant environment, necessitating substantial environmental testing beyond TRL 5. It should noted that there 
is substantial debate within the spacecraft community as to the correct relevant environment. At a minimum, 
the authors consider the relevant environment to be a laboratory space simulation chamber containing a full, 
independent system demonstration. Factors that should be considered include corona discharge, radiation 
environment (LEO versus GEO), and thermal-vacuum testing. The launch environment for micro- and nano-
spacecraft can be severe since these are typically secondary or tertiary payloads; as such, the system should be 
tested at shock, vibrational, and thermal loading consistent with the anticipated launch vehicle and conditions. 

In some cases, when the effects of microgravity may be critical, demonstration beyond the laboratory may 
be necessary. If analysis cannot show that microgravity effects are sufficiently well understood and have been 
mitigated, then component- and/or system-level testing in microgravity should be performed. It should also be 
noted that, should a flight demonstration be required at this stage of technical development, it may result in the 
TRL transitioning directly from TRL 5 to TRL 7, assuming all criteria are successfully met.  

Failure during testing of the propulsion system in relevant environments can have major consequences. 
Although also true at lower TRLs, failure of a subsystem when striving for TRL 6 can reduce the system maturity 
to as low as TRL 4 in the case of an unexpected failure that requires significant redesign. However, care should 
be taken to ensure that failure avoidance does not lead to relaxation of standards. 

TRL 6 requires that the propulsion system be performance- and lifetime-verified. The lifetime expressed as 
total impulse should be experimentally measured, and the critical performance parameters (thrust, Isp, Ibit, etc.) 
should be measured with quantified uncertainty estimates at beginning of life (BoL) and end of life (EoL). The 
claimed lifetime should be provided as a fraction of the demonstrated lifetime with substantial margin 
(suggested that demonstrated lifetime equal 150% of qualified life). If analysis is used to determine that the 
micro-propulsion system’s lifetime may have statistical uncertainty, lifetime and related performance 
measurements of more than a single system should be performed. Such repeated measurements may also be 
used to reduce the lifetime margin, dependent on the number of measurements. If lifetime testing of the 
thruster head was not previously conducted, lifetime testing of the prototype system may result in the TRL 
transitioning directly from TRL 4 to TRL 6, assuming all criteria are successfully met. 

At TRL 6, the complete propulsion system design, including interfaces to the host spacecraft, is finalized as 
appropriate for flight. As such, the prototype micro-propulsion system should be evaluated for thermal 
soakback and electromagnetic interference (EMI) when functioning within its operational envelope. Any plume 
contamination or charging issues that could adversely affect the host spacecraft should be mitigated to reach 
TRL 6.  

Another major step required for TRL 6 is compatibility with flight-like software. TRL 6 cannot be met unless 
software interfaces are fully identified, developed, and verified using prototype-level hardware or better. 
Software and related interfaces can be a major cost and schedule driver in technology development, so care 
should be taken to ensure that software development remain a high priority. Until software functionality is 
demonstrated with host and micro-propulsion system prototype-level hardware, TRL 6 cannot be achieved. 

As a check to ensure TRL 6 maturity has indeed been achieved, the authors strongly suggest an independent 
peer review of the system-level maturity to verify that all technical milestones have been achieved. Such a 
review can easily be integrated into the program management review process of the system development.  

The key properties that must be fulfilled for a micro-propulsion technology to be considered at TRL 6 are 
as follows: 

 
• Integrated (as flight-like as possible) prototype system is demonstrated and verified in a relevant 

environment consistent with the anticipated operational environment (e.g., vacuum, microgravity, 
radiation, thermal cycling, electromagnetic compatibility, etc.). 

• Launch environment survival (vibrational, shock, and thermal) is demonstrated and verified for the 
integrated prototype system. 

• Micro-propulsion system lifetime is directly measured with lifetime margin explicitly defined; the 
lifetime margin may be reduced if multiple life tests provide improved statistical certainty. 



 

15 of 20 

• Micro-propulsion system performance is measured and verified at BoL and EoL. 

• Thermal soakback, EMI, and plume contamination / charging impacts on the host spacecraft are 
mitigated. 

• Software interfaces are fully identified, developed, and verified with prototype-level hardware. 

• Peer review verifies achieving TRL 6.  

 

Technology Readiness Level 7 

DoD guidance calls out TRL 7 as: 

Prototype near or at planned operational system. Represents a major step up from TRL 6 by requiring 
demonstration of an actual system prototype in an operational environment (e.g., in an aircraft, in a vehicle, 
or in space). 

While NASA guidance calls out (hardware / software) TRL 7 as: 

A high-fidelity prototype or engineering unit that adequately addresses all critical scaling issues is built and 
functions in the actual operational environment and platform (ground, airborne, or space). 

Prototype software exists having all key functionality available for demonstration and test. Well integrated 
with operational hardware / software systems demonstrating operational feasibility. Most software bugs 
removed. Limited documentation available. 

TRL 7 is interpreted to include all criteria of TRL 6 as well as a number of additional criteria. The authors 
have interpreted the requirements for TRL 7 to include a protoflight system demonstration in an operational 
environment; alternatively, TRL 7 may also be reached via demonstration in an operational environment of a 
micro-propulsion system that went through acceptance testing after conventional qualification of the 
technology. Both of these cases involve an engineering-fidelity system with fully verified space-rated 
components and manufacturing processes. Compared to a qualification unit — which is flight-like hardware 
(but will not be flown) that is tested to full qualification levels and durations, protoflight hardware is intended 
for actual flight and tested to typically qualification levels but for reduced durations. The operational 
environment is defined to be onboard a space vehicle; sub-orbital testing may be sufficient in some cases. 

During the flight demonstration of the micro-propulsion system, there are a number of operational 
verification steps that should be performed. The micro-propulsion system’s software interfaces should be 
verified to function as designed. This includes timing of control sequences, automatic detection and mitigation 
of invalid commands, as well as normal operational control. Micro-propulsion system performance should be 
verified via orbital or attitude data from the spacecraft and ground observations. Any propulsion system 
anomalous events should be identified and traced to root causes. Toward this goal, it is suggested that 
propulsion system engineering hardware be retained in a functional state such that the hardware may be 
ground-tested in a relevant environment to assist with identifying and correcting any software or hardware 
issues that may arise during flight test. 

Another important requirement that should be met before the system can reach TRL 7 is archiving the 
technical results of the flight system demonstration. Reports of the data and lessons learned should be made 
available to the relevant audiences. Should the security level allow it, one highly suggested method is via peer-
reviewed literature, which is readily available in the open and ITAR-restricted levels. 

The key properties that must be fulfilled for a micro-propulsion technology to be considered at TRL 7 are 
as follows: 

 
• Protoflight or acceptance-tested micro-propulsion system is developed and demonstrated onboard 

spacecraft. 

• Protoflight or acceptance-tested system’s flight performance is verified via orbital or attitude flight data. 



 

16 of 20 

• Protoflight or acceptance-tested system’s anomalous events are traced to root causes and verified with a 
ground testbed. 

• Protoflight or acceptance-tested system’s flight demonstration measurements and results are archived 
in relevant literature. 

 

Technology Readiness Level 8  

DoD guidance calls out TRL 8 as: 

Technology has been proven to work in its final form and under expected conditions. In almost all cases, this 
TRL represents the end of true system development. Examples include developmental test and evaluation of 
the system in its intended weapon system to determine if it meets design specifications. 

While NASA guidance calls out (hardware / software) TRL 8 as: 

The final product in its final configuration is successfully demonstrated through test and analysis for its 
intended operational environment and platform (ground, airborne, or space). If necessary, life testing has 
been completed. 

All software has been thoroughly debugged and fully integrated with all operational hardware and software 
systems. All user documentation, training documentation, and maintenance documentation completed. All 
functionality successfully demonstrated in simulated operational scenarios. Verification and validation 
(V&V) completed. 

TRL 8 is interpreted to include all criteria of TRL 7 as well as a number of additional criteria. The authors 
have interpreted the requirements for TRL 8 to primarily consist of the final flight system configuration for a 
routine, operational micro-propulsion system, after which actual technology development is complete. This 
requires that all flight demonstration anomalies be mitigated and the final system design be implemented and 
flight verified. Flight verification of design modifications is deemed to be necessary, but it does not imply a 
dedicated experimental flight. One additional critical aspect of TRL 8 is that repeatable system production and 
performance should be demonstrated. This is critical because advances in space technology require that 
knowledge be retained such that it can be repeated some number of years later. This capability may require the 
application of suitable design and manufacturing processes beyond the scope of this work (i.e., manufacturing 
readiness levels or MRLs). 

Retaining TRL 8 requires substantial design and manufacturing expertise as well as a sustainment of that 
technical capability. It is possible that a program may reach TRL 8, then due to loss and dispersion of expertise 
as well as changes in available components, later find the technology to be at a lower TRL, especially if 
insufficient institutional knowledge has been retained. For these reasons, it is absolutely critical that archival-
quality records of materials, manufacturing, and software processes be retained. With a similar rationale, it 
may also be beneficial to retain engineering-level hardware. 

Due to the relatively small scale of micro-propulsion systems, there may be cases where substantial overlap 
exists between TRL 7 and TRL 8, especially if multiple units are deployed on a spacecraft or as a constellation 
of micro- or nano-spacecraft. The primary differentiator between TRL 7 and TRL 8 then must lie in the 
manufacturability metrics previously discussed. These are captured by a capability to repeatably produce the 
micro-propulsion systems with the anticipated nominal performance.  

The key properties that must be fulfilled for a micro-propulsion technology to be considered at TRL 8 are 
as follows: 

 
• All flight system demonstration anomalies are mitigated with demonstrated resolution. 

• Final system design is implemented and flight verified. 

• Repeatable system production, manufacturability, and performance are demonstrated and documented. 

• System is ready for operational deployment and no longer deemed an experiment with specialized 
support.

• Final system represents the end of actual technology development. 
 

Technology Readiness Level 9  

DoD guidance calls out TRL 9 as: 

Actual application of the technology in its final form and under mission conditions, such as those encountered 
in operational test and evaluation. Examples include using the system under operational mission conditions. 

While NASA guidance calls out (hardware / software) TRL 9 as: 

The final product is successfully operated in an actual mission. 

All software has been thoroughly debugged and fully integrated with all operational hardware / software 
systems. All documentation has been completed. Sustaining software support is in place. System has been 
successfully operated in the operational environment. 

TRL 9 is the highest technological maturity in the process and is somewhat difficult to define precisely for 
spacecraft micro-propulsion flight systems as it represents an asymptotic final state of maturation. It can be 
defined by a history of successful routine operations. At TRL 9, it is now assumed that the micro-propulsion 
technology is operated as a routine system onboard a spacecraft and not as an experiment. Specifically, TRL 9 
functionality calls for the users to have full capability to operate and interpret operational routine without 
specialized technologist support. At this TRL, operations of the micro-propulsion system are routinely 
successful within primary mission constraints. This history of successful user operations requires fully mature 
hardware and software interfaces. As stated previously, loss of expertise and art can lead to maturity 
downgrades due to loss of ability to successfully execute the technology. This mandates that the flight history 
of the micro-propulsion system be archived to create a living history of operations. 

It is only expected that TRL 9 will be achieved for systems that see repeated production and routine use for 
a number of missions. This TRL will be difficult to achieve and perhaps more difficult to maintain due to limited 
flight opportunities. However, this may change in the future should large constellations of micro- and nano-
spacecraft be routinely launched requiring micro-propulsion systems. 

Revisions from Previous Assignments of Technology Readiness Levels 
Readers will note that the technology readiness levels presented in this JANNAF guide differ in several small 

but significant ways from those presented by the Air Force Research Laboratory (AFRL) in earlier 
publications21,22 and also from the 2019 edition20 of this JANNAF guide. Previous AFRL TRL classifications 
designated a complete thruster head lifetime demonstration to achieve TRL 4. Now, TRL 4 and 5 only require a 
documented high-fidelity estimate of thruster head and propulsion system lifetime, respectively, based on an 
experimentally demonstrated long-duration operation sufficient to explore major failure modes.  

As a result, the present JANNAF micro-propulsion TRL guide has delayed a full experimental system lifetime 
demonstration with margin until TRL 6. This delay in full lifetime demonstration with margin was determined 
by the authors to be more congruent with TRL guidelines in Table 1 and Table 2, requiring increasing fidelity 
between TRL 5 and TRL 6 assessments. The authors believe that delay of the full system life test until TRL 6 
provides a more economical path to maturity and simultaneously produces a process whereby the TRL 5 and 
TRL 6 designations follow more continuously. 

Compared to the 2019 edition, the current JANNAF guide has delayed the need to estimate thruster plume 
behavior from TRL 3 to TRL 4 to better align with TRL 4’s focus on propulsion system requirements definition. 
TRL 7 now includes use of acceptance-tested hardware (from conventional technology qualification) in 
addition to protoflight hardware. 

There has been discussion in the community that a formal review should be performed prior to claiming to 
have advanced a TRL. A formal, documented review is certainly useful for a larger, more expensive propulsion 
system; however, for a micro-propulsion system where there is an emphasis on low cost and rapid transition 
of technology, the authors feel that informal internal reviews, ideally documented in the technical literature, 
are sufficient. As a result, the authors only identify a single critical peer review to reach TRL 6. Yet additional 



 

18 of 20 

reviews, either informal or formal, are certainly necessary as part of the technical maturation process. This 
guideline only seeks to identify the minimum requirements. 

As previously indicated, DoD TRL designations do have differences compared to NASA TRL designations. 
However, upon review of the micro-propulsion development categorization followed by NASA, the Jet 
Propulsion Laboratory (JPL), and AFRL, the authors have come to the conclusion that DoD and NASA TRL 
designations in this work are sufficiently compatible, and generally complementary, such that the TRL 
milestones presented in this work provide useful guidance to both technology developers and users. In fact, the 
combination of the DoD and NASA TRL designations has served to provide increased detail to the definition of 
micro-propulsion TRLs. 

Conclusions 
The authors’ stated goal is to standardize micro-propulsion system TRL designation. The focus is on the 

complete micro-propulsion system and system-level TRLs, since component TRLs are less helpful to end-users 
in their evaluation of the suitability of available technologies from developers. 

It is thus to the benefit of both the user and developer communities that application of TRLs to micro-
propulsion systems be rationalized and made consistent. In this work, the authors have interpreted the general 
guidance provided by the DoD Technology Transition Guide for micro-propulsion systems and posed suggested 
criteria for meeting all micro-propulsion system TRLs; NASA TRL definitions were used to provide additional 
guidance, particularly for software integration and documentation. As discussed previously, the authors do not 
believe that the DoD and NASA TRL designations differ in a fundamental manner; apparent differences are 
actually complementary views on addressing technology development challenges. 

Despite the efforts of this work, the authors recognize that some ambiguity remains in the TRLs and indeed 
is inherent to the process of maturing new technologies. Each technology and application is unique, and as a 
result, it is difficult to account for all cases. For example, propellantless propulsion technologies may adapt 
these guidelines, but they will require some modification. These TRL designations may also have application to 
larger spacecraft propulsion systems, most particularly electric propulsion systems that share many of the 
technological issues involved with severely miniaturizing propulsion systems to provide propulsive capability 
on micro- and nano-spacecraft. 

While critical of present self-assessment of TRLs for micro-propulsion systems, the authors have not been 
tempted to institute systematic audits or further formalize the system beyond the simple checklists provided 
in this work. This keeps with the sense that from technology development spring new concepts that should not 
be throttled by committee. The only formal verification of TRL that is recommended is for TRL 6. At this point, 
the micro-propulsion system should be at sufficient maturity that the review should be little more than a 
formality, and a similar process should already be incorporated as long as institutionalized program 
management processes are in place. TRL 6 is also the final stage prior to preparation for protoflight 
development; as such, a formal check here is most appropriate. 

Many technologists appear to believe that the TRL process is linear and sequential, and that time on task 
will invariably improve the TRL. From the authors’ analysis and experience, this is not true. As has been 
previously stated, it is possible for a micro-propulsion system to revert one or more TRLs due to a failure to 
demonstrate a TRL-required milestone. As an example, consider if while striving to achieve TRL 6, the 
demonstration in the relevant environment reveals a substantial design shortcoming not identified in previous 
lower-fidelity testing; in this case, the micro-propulsion system should be downgraded to TRL 3 or 4 depending 
on the severity of the issue identified. Also, as previously noted, retaining TRL 8 and TRL 9 may be difficult due 
to short production runs or the loss of technical expertise with time. As a result, systems out of production for 
substantial periods should be considered to be a notional TRL 7, perhaps lower if the interfaces or vehicle 
change substantially. On a more positive note, there may be technologies that require demonstration in 
microgravity in order to show system functionality. Such systems such as tethers or orbital drag devices cannot 
demonstrate full system functionality without a flight test. As a result, such technologies could quickly rise, by 
the definitions in this JANNAF guide, from as low as a TRL 4 to as high as a TRL 7 in the course of a single flight 
test. It is important for both the technologists and the end-users to remember that the TRL process is dynamic 
with both progression and regression paths. 

The technology development process between TRL 4–5 is at times referred to by technologists as the valley 
of death and the steep portion of the S curve. Examining these sections in some detail in the preparation of this 
work, the authors believe that there is indeed a transition in these levels of technology maturation, but it is not 
necessarily as severe as some frustrated technologists would have it seem. Clearly TRL 4–5 is the handoff point 



 

19 of 20 

of the micro-propulsion system from specialized technologist to system-level designer, and as with any 
transition, there can be fumbles. The tendency of technologists to overstate their TRLs has not endeared 
themselves, nor the micro-propulsion technologies they espouse, to the system-level designers. It is common 
to find micro-propulsion thruster heads highly developed, while the remainder of the subsystems that fully 
form the micro-propulsion system is nebulous. As a result, the technologist may feel that the system is at TRL 
4 or even TRL 5, when by the analysis in this work (and more importantly, the users’ point of view), it is more 
of a TRL 2 or TRL 3 due to a lack of thought on the system as a whole. From this point of view, it is not surprising 
that many technologies cannot transition, hobbled as they are by overly optimistic TRL designations from 
technologists. This nicely reiterates the need for systematic guidance on application of micro-propulsion 
system TRLs. As lower cost, comparatively simple micro-propulsion systems for micro- and nano-spacecraft 
promote rapid technological transition for agile, responsive spacecraft, meaningful technology maturity 
metrics must be provided to developers and users alike. 

As presented, the application of these TRL designations are limited to micro-propulsion systems. However, 
the authors feel that the included micro-propulsion TRL designations provide a starting point for a community-
wide discussion of TRLs for spacecraft propulsion. While the scale of propulsion and related systems may make 
TRL designations differ, there is sufficient similarity that a propulsion community-wide discussion should be 
initiated.
