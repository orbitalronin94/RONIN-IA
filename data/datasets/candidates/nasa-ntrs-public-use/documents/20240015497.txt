<p>Validation of Two-phase Flow Modeling Techniques in
Loci-Stream using Axial Jet Pressure Control and Autogenous
</p>
<p>Pressurization Experiments
</p>
<p>Chintan S. Patel ∗
</p>
<p>Qualis LLC (Amentum Space Exploration Group), NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, AL
</p>
<p>Improvements to computational fluid dynamics modeling of cryogenic fluid management
operations are presented with experimental validations. Modeling improvements includes
utilizing improved delayed detaches eddy simulation to more effectively model turbulence
compared to the Reynolds-averaged Navier Stokes modeling, modeling temperature dependent
tank solid properties to capture large variation in thermodynamic properties at cryogenic
conditions, modeling real gas equation of state for hydrogen instead of ideal gas behavior, and
an on-going effort to improve interface turbulence transport for the volume of fluid method.
Two flight-scale ground tests, five K-site tank axial jet pressure control experiments and an
Engineering Design Unit tank autogenous pressurization experiment are validated and show
the effectiveness of the improved modeling techniques. The successful validation establishes
further trust in these models for flight project support and provides a pathway for maturation
of in-development interface turbulence damping model for the volume of fluid method for more
generalized applications.
</p>
<p>I. Nomenclature
</p>

<p>II. Introduction
</p>
<p>NASA’s near-future long-term space missions necessitate advancements in cryogenic fluid management (CFM)
technologies, which is a critical technology for NASA’s Artemis missions. CFM deals with safe, low cost, and
</p>
<p>reliable long-term propellant storage; pressure control and propellant location control in the tank for engine operations; as
well as tank draining and filling. Consequently, NASA Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) has established
the CFM Portfolio Project (CFMPP) to improve CFM technologies for upcoming missions. In addition to advanced
hardware development and demonstration, there is a need for accurate modeling of cryogenic system behavior to support
rapid, reliable, and low-cost spacecraft design and operation. CFM Modeling Portfolio within CFMPP aims to improve,
develop, and validate modeling capabilities.
</p>
<p>As part of the CFM modeling portfolio, the Propulsion Engineering Fluid Dynamics branch (ER42) at NASA
Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC) has been tasked with assessing and improving computational fluid dynamics
(CFD) tools used to support future and current flight projects such as Human Lander System and Commercial Lunar
Payload Services. CFD modeling is indispensable for accurately simulating and understanding these problems with
high fidelity. We are engaged in improving and validating two-phase models in the Loci-STREAM CFD code to predict
various CFM phenomena.
</p>
<p>∗Computational Fluid Dynamicist, Fluid Dynamics Branch (ER42), NASA MSFC
</p>
<p>1</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>An accurate capture of the gas-liquid interface dynamics is the primary modeling bottleneck. The interface dynamics
includes altered interface turbulence transport, phase change, and thermal transport. Additionally, turbulence modeling
away from the interface is another significant challenge for CFM modeling. Low speed but high Rayleigh number
natural convection is often encountered in CFM problems and is difficult to model using Reynolds average Navier-Stokes
(RANS) turbulence models. Moreover, CFM operations with high Rayleigh number as well as high Reynolds number
flows might also pose more modeling challenges.
</p>
<p>In this paper, previous autogenous pressurization predictions [1] are updated with an improved modeling and axial
jet based pressure control test [2] is expanded to contain five tests. Implementation of turbulence transport model for
volume of fluid (VOF) method is described. Utility of a hybrid RANS and implicit LES approach was also described
to improve the temperature predictions of the RANS model. Validation of our modeling techniques and CFD solver
Loci-Stream-VOF are demonstrated.
</p>

<p>IV. Computational Model
The salient physics pertaining to the problem is retained and inconsequential and secondary effects are ignored for
</p>
<p>practical computational modeling.
</p>
<p>A. Governing Equations
The governing equations for mass, momentum, and energy conservation; phase change model; two phase sharp
</p>
<p>interface model; and interface turbulence transport are already described in our previous work [1]. Since both CFM
</p>
<p>4</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>operations involve turbulent flows, RANS equations are used.
</p>
<p>B. Turbulence Modeling
Menter’s 2003 shear stress transport (SST) model is primarily utilized for turbulence modeling [4], but improved
</p>
<p>delayed detached eddy simulation (IDDES) approach [5] is also used to address the problem of excess turbulence
transport in RANS turbulence model. It is a hybrid RANS-LES model where unresolved scales - typically near-wall
regions and high shear or vorticity regions where mesh refinement for LES resolution is impractical - are modeled with
RANS equations and resolved scales are modeled with sub-grid scale (SGS) modeling. An implicit LES approach is
used where the grid-induced dissipation provides the sub-grid scales. The blending parameter, 𝑓𝑑 distinguishes the
resolved and unresolved regions. RANS mode is indicated by 𝑓𝑑 = 1 and SGS mode is indicated by 𝑓𝑑 = 0.
</p>
<p>1. Interface Turbulence Transport
Experiments like those by Fabre et al [6] show altered turbulence at the gas-liquid interface. Damped turbulence
</p>
<p>kinetic energy on the gas side of the interface and mild enhancement of turbulence kinetic energy on the liquid side of
the interface was observed. Rashidi et al [7] observed streak formations at the interface that are similar to the wall.
Direct numerical simulation (DNS) data from Lombardi et al [8] also show wall-like near interface characteristics
on the gas side and enhanced turbulence transport on the liquid side. DNS study by Fulgosi et al [9] found gas side
near-interface turbulence to be similar to a wall’s viscous sub-layer, but the turbulence kinetic energy is not zero at the
interface. Still, they observed similarities in the near-interface turbulence distributions which indicates that a general
RANS model is plausible. Jofre et al [10] studied bubbly flow using LES and found that modeling turbulence damping
on the gas side of the interface produced satisfactory comparison to DNS near the interface.
</p>
<p>These observations are encapsulated in our simple but effective interface turbulence model for the two-phase sharp
interface method [1] where gas-side of the interface is treated wall-like, and liquid side of the interface has no boundary
influence.
</p>
<p>For VOF simulations, asymmetric interface turbulence transport (ITT) model is implemented based on the work by
Fan et al [11], which builds upon Egorov’s approach of increasing turbulence frequency, 𝜔 in the interfacial regions
where low turbulence is desired [12]. An asymmetric damping approach is implemented where the turbulence on the
gas side is damped, and the liquid side is enhanced in the near-interface region.
</p>

<p>Menter indicates that the asymptotic behavior of 𝜔 as described by Wilcox [13] is satisfied by Equation 3 as long as
𝑦+ &lt; 3 [14]. He supplies 𝐵 = 10 for a smooth no-slip wall. The turbulence damping on the gas side of the interface
due to a liquid cannot be higher than the damping caused by a wall and therefore values much higher than 10 may not
be justified without a well-elucidated reasoning. Yet many studies [15][16][17] including Fan et al do so. They are
likely compensating for an insufficient mesh resolution, i.e., an interface first cell height, Δ𝑦 &gt; 3𝑦+ and possibly other
factors described in the last paragraph of this section. But such arbitrarily tuned high values could lead to non-physical
damping. Indeed, while using very high B, Fan et al deviated away from the turbulence kinetic energy data from their
validation experiments and also underpredicted the pressure gradients.
</p>
<p>Author’s first recommended approach is to employ a well-resolved mesh with low 𝑦+ and not to exceed the damping
factor value of 10. When such refinement is impractical, a pre-devised scaling factor as a function of an estimated
interface 𝑦+ should be used in order to avoid non-physical behaviors. The author intends to take up the problem of
</p>

<p>developing a scaling technique in the future. Developing an interface modeling approach based on Wilcox’s original
method of calculating near-wall 𝜔 distribution (Equation 2) instead of Menter’s simplified method of computing only
the wall 𝜔 value (Equation 3) may also be particularly fruitful.
</p>
<p>Beyond the compensation for insufficient mesh refinement by tuning B, these studies including the present one may
be missing some important flow physics that arise from the gas-liquid interactions differing from a wall-fluid interaction.
The author’s hypothesis is that because the turbulence transport depends on the interface tangent velocity gradient, the
missing flow physics may include the effects of relative gas-liquid molecular viscosities and densities, as well as the
presence and degree of flow reversal (i.e., "boundary-layer" separation) at the interface. If the aforementioned modeling
techniques do not provide satisfactory modeling fidelity, these hypotheses may need to be explored in the future.
</p>

A test simulation without the heat leakage to the tank walls yielded no significant differences in the depressurization
trend compared to simulations where heat leakage is modeled – indicating that small amount of heat transfer between

<p>2. Mesh
Six unstructured hexahedral meshes are created. Three of them are shown in the Figure 5. Each mesh has refinement
</p>
<p>in the regions of high shear, i.e., jet shear region and near the interface to sufficiently resolve the flow and thermal
dynamics. The nominal mesh is 123 thousand cells for a 1-deg axisymmetric sector, which is equivalent to 44 million
cells for a 3-dimensional domain. First cell wall-normal height is always 1 × 10−4 m to capture boundary layer flow
with 𝑦+ &lt;= 1. Interface normal first cell height as low as 5 × 10−5 m are tested. Largest cell edge length is restricted to
5 × 10−3 m and is refined up to 1 × 10−3 m. A mesh independent solution is obtained and discussed in V.A.3.
</p>
<p>3. Initial Conditions
Tank conditions shown in the table 1 are used to initialize liquid fill level and ullage pressure. Axially varying
</p>
<p>temperature profile is initialized based on the temperature probe data available for each test. A typical profile has
the coolest temperature at the tank bottom with a sharp thermal gradient at the interface and approximately linear
temperature increase in the ullage away from the interface. Radial temperature variation is not considered owing to a
lack of such data. The initial convection current and corresponding thermal gradients were expected to have negligible
impact because the axial jet velocity is two to three orders of magnitude higher than the natural convection velocity and
is expected to drive the pressure trends. Indeed, a test simulation initialized from a few minutes of self-pressurization
simulation to set-up temperature and velocity distribution showed no noteworthy impact on the depressurization rate
prediction.
</p>
<p>4. Boundary Conditions
Tank walls were supplied with constant heat flux of 4.2 W/m2 to simulate the heat leak. A symmetry boundary
</p>
<p>condition is applied on the sector planes of the axisymmetric model. The pump outlet is modeled in the computational
domain as a constant velocity inlet boundary condition with a constant temperature, and the pump inlet is modeled as a
constant volume outlet from the tank. In reality, as the test progresses, the jet-induced mixing in the liquid hydrogen
warms up the inlet temperatures - reducing the efficacy of the jet to reduce the tank pressure as the test progresses.
Tests were run for about 500 seconds for the test 449 to ascertain sensitivity to the warming jet. A time-varying
(increasing) inlet temperature was applied by averaging outlet temperature from a previous simulation. This sensitivity
tests were quantitatively non-conservative but were expected to be qualitative useful. The tests showed no impact on the

<p>depressurization rate, and the cases were run with the constant temperature. Later it was found that not modeling jet

<p>For the sharp interface turbulence boundaries, gas-side is treated wall-like as usual. But the liquid side treatment
deviated from the no-treatment approach (extrapolated boundary) to wall-like treatment to counterbalance the
overprediction of turbulent kinetic energy arising from not modeling the non-planarity of the interface. The interface is
assumed to be flat for the sharp interface solution, but the flat interface becomes non-planar when the jet impinges it
(Figure 6). The change in flowfield would lower the velocities on the liquid side of the interface as well as turbulent
kinetic energy than predicted by a planar interface. Wall-like turbulence modeling allows counterbalancing this modeling
deficiency by lowering effective turbulent kinetic energy near the liquid side interface.
</p>
<p>D. EDU Tank - Autogenous Pressurization
</p>
<p>1. Geometry
Axisymmetric flow is assumed. Tank internals are removed with the exception of the forward diffuser, which is
</p>
<p>simplified (Figure 7) to have three annular cylindrical slots with same exit area as the original diffuser .
The heat transfer between the room temperature gaseous hydrogen and the tank’s walls at the liquid hydrogen
</p>
<p>cryogenic temperature has significant impact on the ullage pressure. So, conjugate heat transfer analysis is conducted to
model the heat transfer between the tank walls, diffuser walls, and the fluids.

<p>2. Mesh
Mesh is refined to capture the important flow features of the high-speed diffuser flow, low velocity ullage flow with
</p>
<p>convection and heat transfer between ullage gas at cryogenic temperatures and pressurant gas at room temperature, and
the sharp thermal gradient at the fluid-wall boundary as well as the gas-liquid interface. Seven unstructured hexahedral
meshes with varying resolution in the diffuser, ullage and interface regions are used for this validation. A representative
sample is presented in the Figure 8. To accurately calculate the heat transfer between the wall and the fluid, wall
integration approach was used, i.e., mesh y+ is 1 or lower at the tank walls. The wall boundary first cell height was
varied between 1× 10−4 m and 5× 10−5 m. The nominal cell edge length in the ullage varies between 2.5 mm and 7 mm.
The first cell heights on both sides of the gas-liquid interface vary between 1 × 10−4 m and 1 × 10−6 m to accurately
capture the altered turbulence characteristics, mass transfer, and heat transfer across the interface. The baseline mesh has
an equivalent three dimensional cell count of 18.8 million (Figure 8b). Mesh sensitivity is presented in Section V.B.4.

<p>3. Initial Conditions
The initial temperature distribution is uniform throughout the tank fluids, and the walls and corresponds to the
</p>
<p>saturation condition for hydrogen at the initial pressure of 100 kPa.
</p>
<p>4. Boundary Conditions
Time-varying massflow and temperatures were measured during the test and are prescribed at the diffuser inlet. The
</p>
<p>measurements across the 40 MLI temperature probes show that the tank is well-insulated in the vacuum chamber, so
tank external wall is considered adiabatic.
</p>
<p>A diffuser inlet turbulence intensity of 5% with eddy viscosity ratio of 1000 are prescribed based on the expectation
of medium to high turbulence. Several simulation tests were carried out to ascertain impact of initial turbulence and
diffuser inlet turbulence. They show the pressurization predictions to be insensitive to a wide range of diffuser inlet
turbulence conditions.
</p>
<p>For conjugate heat transfer analysis, a tight fit between mating solids was assumed and a small thermal contact
resistance of 1𝑒 − 16 was supplied for their contact surfaces.
</p>
<p>E. Fluid Properties
Fluid and solid material properties were evaluated from the current National Institute of Standards and Technology
</p>
<p>(NIST) databases [19]. The liquid hydrogen converts to para-hydrogen isomeric state after sufficiently long storage.
Since the flow dynamics is dominated by the liquid interactions in the K-site axial jet experiments, liquid hydrogen
is assumed to be in the para-hydrogen state. For the autogenous pressurization, vapor flow dynamics dominates the
pressurization and only the near interface liquid participates in the process. The newly liquefied gas converts to the
ortho-hydrogen isomeric state at the interface. Owing to these reasons, the liquid is modeled in the ortho-hydrogen
isomeric state for the modeling of the autogenous pressurization experiment. The liquid hydrogen is assumed to be a
Boussinesq fluid to model the buoyancy effect. Since the liquid temperature does not vary considerably during the test,
constant thermal and transport properties are modeled.
</p>

vapor’s equation of state was found to have little influence on the pressurization prediction.
</p>
<p>The EDU tank wall is modeled with temperature-dependent aluminum 2219 thermal properties [20], and diffuser is
modeled with temperature-dependent stainless steel 304 thermal properties from NIST. The properties of ortho-hydrogen
liquid (Table 2), hydrogen vapor (Table 3), tank wall, and diffuser wall (Table 4) are listed in the tables below.
</p>
<p>F. Discretization and Solver Set-up
Spatial discretization is either first order upwind (FOU) or second order upwind (SOU) - except for the VOF
</p>
<p>equations, which are always solved using the FOU scheme. Time integration is performed using the implicit first
order backward difference formula or second order backward difference formula. SIMPLEC scheme was used for
pressure-velocity coupling. The second order schemes are preferred but the first order schemes are used when second
order schemes are not stable. All simulations are well-converged with low residuals and 2-4 order of magnitude residual
drops during Newton-Raphson iterations.
</p>
<p>Spatial resolution is discussed in Sections IV.C.2 and IV.D.2. Temporal resolution in the range of 5e-4 seconds
to 1 seconds are tested. All predictions are independent of spatial resolution, temporal resolution and mass transfer
accommodation factor. The insensitivities are demonstrated in the Results section.
</p>
<p>V. Results
Time-accurate tank pressure prediction is the primary validation goal. The probe temperature predictions are also
</p>
<p>compared and discussed as a secondary quantity of interest. The sharp interface method approach is the primary focus
of the study, but the pressure prediction improvement with the VOF method owing to the ITT module is also briefly
discussed.
</p>
<p>A. K-site Tank - Axial Jet Pressure Control
</p>
<p>1. Tank Pressurization
Across the five tests, the CFD predictions agree well with the tests, with a maximum relative error in pressure drop
</p>
<p>being -16.88% for the experiment 457 – demonstrating a reliable modeling capability. The time traces of the predicted
and the actual ullage pressures are shown in Figure 9. Note: As described in Section IV.C.4, in contrast to the earlier
finding, tests with higher flowrates and longer depressurization times, i.e., tests 449 and 457, were found to be affected
by the jet warming effect that is not accounted for. Taking this effect into account, the maximum error is expected to be
much lower than -16.88%.
</p>
<p>10</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Fig. 9 Pressure prediction over time using sharp interface method compared with the test data
</p>
<p>As the cold liquid from the tank bottom impinges the warmer interface, the interface cools due to the forced
convection. A corresponding drop in the saturation temperature at the interface initiates condensation of the vapor. The
vapor also cools due to the conductive and the convective cooling. Both processes lower the ullage pressure. The phase
change phenomenon takes place around 1-100 micrometer length scale and the resulting latent heat exchange could
produce high temperature gradient near the interface. This temperature gradient must be captured accurately because the
vapor pressure is strongly affected by the interface temperature as evidenced by Antoine equation (Equation 11 in Patel
et al[1]). The first cell height of at least 1× 10−4 m normal to the interface was necessary to resolve the thermal gradient
resulting from the complex interactions of the forced convection, the interfacial turbulence and the latent heat exchange.
</p>
<p>Fig. 10 Composite contour plot of Test 434 at 60 seconds shows temperature on the left side and turbulence
viscosity ratio along with the streamlines and velocity vectors on the right hand side. The jet impinges the
interface and depressurizes the tank through condensation and cooling of the ullage vapor. As the jet flows
over the tank wall, it entrains and convects the hotter liquid which rises back up due to the buoyancy creating
Rayleigh-Taylor and Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities resulting in turbulent mixing of the liquid.
</p>
<p>11</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Figure 10 demonstrates the physical processes of axial jet based tank depressurization at the tank-scale through the
temperature and turbulent viscosity ratio contours. The turbulent mixing induced by the mixer flow in the previously
quiescent liquid governs how quickly the tank temperatures homogenizes in the liquid region of the tank. Near the
gas-liquid interface, the cooling of the interface due to the jet is not uniform across the interface. The highest temperature
gradient between the gas and liquid is found near the jet impingement location. This region is responsible for the
maximum cooling and condensation of the vapor. After the impingement, the jet warms up downstream as it mixes with
the warmer liquid, absorbs latent heat energy from condensation of the gas, and absorbs sensible heat from the warmer
gas. The depressurization plateaus as the liquid temperature homogenizes over time.
</p>
<p>The depressurization is the fastest, and the pressure drop is the largest (test 434) when the tank fill level and jet flow
rate are higher because of the availability of the large amount of cooler bulk liquid to absorb thermal energy from the
interfacial liquid. When the jet flow rate is low, slow and long-sustained, linear depressurization occurs (Test 459). Our
CFD is able to predict depressurization at all regimes of jet flowrates, and at both high and low propellant fill levels.
</p>
<p>The newly implemented ITT module in the Loci-Stream-VOF also drastically improves the predictions of VOF
method (Figure 11). But the refined mesh resolution required at the interface to accurately capture the fluid-thermal
dynamics restricts the timestep owing to the explicit time-integration requirement of the VOF advection equation where
the Courant number must be maintained much lower than unity for numerical stability. More importantly, the inevitable
spurious velocities and instabilities introduced at the interface by the geometrically accurate VOF method make the
solution numerically unstable. More work is being done to alleviate these issues with the VOF method.
</p>
<p>Fig. 11 ITT model significantly improves the predictions with the VOF approach, but the VOF approach is
unstable at the required 0.1 mm near-interface length-scale. VOF stability enhancements are planned in the near
future.
</p>
<p>2. Tank Temperatures and Turbulence Modeling
For each of the five tests, CFD temperature data was extracted at the location where experimental temperature rake
</p>
<p>data is available. Here, we note that the probe locations (Figure 3) were extracted as accurately as possible from the
available tank documentations which lacked some location information and judicious assumptions were made based on
the available data and the schematics of probe locations. Small uncertainty in probe locations remain.
</p>
<p>A related pressing problem in the CFM modeling community is that of the accurate prediction of the turbulence
transport. For this particular problem, jet mixing with the quiescent liquid creates high shear stress and consequently
highly turbulent flow in the mixing region, but in the rest of the tank, away from the near-wall regions, the turbulence
transport is expected to be much lower. Further, the altered turbulence transport at the interface is already discussed.
Here, temperature data is used as an indirect indicator for the turbulence transport to gain insight into turbulence
modeling.
</p>
<p>While Menter’s SST model predicts the pressurization satisfactorily (Figure 9), the temperature comparison is less
satisfactory, but IDDES model captures the temperature trends almost always better or at least as well as the SST model
(Figure 12, 13, and 14). It is evident that the temperatures in the tank are more homogenized due to the overpredicted
turbulence transport by the SST model. The IDDES model suppresses the SST model’s overprediction 15. The switch
</p>

<p>With turbulence modeling, there are almost always drawbacks that need to be balanced with the benefits. The
two-equation RANS models are relatively simple and have been time-tested for a wide-range of engineering applications,
but they fail to be satisfactory for many CFM operations. While the IDDES model improves upon the RANS models by
ameliorating non-physically high turbulence mixing regions in RANS model, their mesh-dependent nature requires
</p>
<p>14</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>careful meshing and an a priori understanding of at least some flow physics.
Current recommendation is evaluating the problem with both modeling approaches and then evaluating each against
</p>
<p>known physical processes. In our problem, the jet mixing process provides that grounding. If the mesh is too refined in
the jet shear layer, it is treated with SGS turbulence instead of RANS turbulence and the mixing in the shear layer is
under-predicted. Since jet mixing is a canonical and well-understood turbulence problem, the jet spreading acts as a
check on the accuracy of the turbulence modeling. The solution then is to sufficiently coarsen the jet mixing regions to
ensure RANS modeling in that region with the IDDES method. Note that the IDDES solution is still mesh independent
in any other region. i.e., the pressure prediction is unaffected by further mesh refinement.
</p>
<p>While this mesh-dependence is a drawback for the IDDES modeling technique, our current approach is to rely on
flawed yet time-tested RANS predictions while simultaneously gaining insights from the IDDES for the problem and
future turbulence modeling improvement. While the RANS approach is too diffusive, both approaches predict the
pressurization of the tank well because the turbulence treatment at the gas-liquid interface drives the problem dynamics
and are identically for both approaches.
</p>

<p>5. Accommodation Factor Sensitivity
Accommodation factor in the Hertz-Knudsen-Schrage equation (Equation 11 in Patel et al [1]) is a coefficient that
</p>
<p>linearly modulates the rate of evaporation/condensation. Its value is expected to be a function of fluid property, and
thermodynamic state. Some experimentally calculated values of the accommodation factor are available in literature for
certain fluids but tend to vary in a wide range. This uncertainty is a more serious concern for thermodynamically open
systems where mass in being added or remove from the system. But for the accommodation factor value greater than
1e-4, the closed systems predictions seem to show almost no dependence on the accommodation factor value [21]. This
feature has observed in previously in the self-pressurization tests and is now replicated in the axial jet pressure control
tests (Figure 18).

<p>2. Interface Temperature and Turbulence
The temperature gradient at the interface needs to be modeled and captured with high accuracy because the ullage
</p>
<p>pressure is strongly affected by the interface temperature as evidenced by the Antoine equation (Equation 11 in Patel et

the saturation curve of hydrogen. Again, an interface normal first cell height of at least 0.1 mm is found to be crucial to
resolve the sharp thermal gradients (Figure 21) resulting from the complex interactions of the forced convection, the
altered interfacial turbulence transport and the latent heat exchange. A larger first cell height results in an incorrect
thermal gradient prediction and consequently an incorrect ullage pressure prediction.

with sufficient accuracy, the eddy conductivity near the interface and consequently temperature of the interface can be
captured accurately.

<p>VI. Conclusion
Turbulence modeling plays a central role in the reliable predictions of cryogenic fluid management operations
</p>
<p>because it modulates phase change and interfacial heat transfer. Turbulence modeling approaches for the bulk fluid and
gas-liquid interface are investigated to develop a reliable and practical predictive model for supporting the flight projects.
</p>
<p>Two ground-based flight-sized CFM tests are used to validate our modeling techniques using sharp interface method.
The modeling technique reliably predicts axial jet based pressure control across different jet flow rates and tank fill
heights. Improving the conjugate heat transfer modeling with temperature-dependent thermal properties for the solids
and an enhanced interface heat transfer modeling improved the time-accurate pressure prediction for the autogenous
pressurization test. Interface turbulence transport modeling for the VOF method also shows significant improvements in
predictive capabilities in the early development phase. Sensitivities to spatial resolution, temporal resolution, and phase
change accommodation factor are evaluated to ensure that the validation is independent of these parameters.
</p>
<p>For the K-site tank tests, the unsatisfactory temperatures predictions from the RANS model are improved with
IDDES modeling. IDDES modeling primarily achieves this by preventing over-prediction of turbulent viscosity by
the RANS model. The IDDES model’s blending parameter that switches from RANS turbulence modeling to SGS
turbulence modeling could be mesh sensitive, so some a priori understanding of expected flow physics is hugely
beneficial. A cogent solution could be to compare the IDDES predictions with the RANS predictions to ensure
elimination of the mesh-dependency. Future work may explore validation of other explicit or implicit LES approaches
for better temperature predictions.
</p>
<p>The early development of the interface turbulence damping model for the VOF approach significantly improves the
predictions, but the solution is hampered by the stability issues at the required near-interface length-scale. The stability
improvements and damping model improvements are planned in the near future. Once the stability issues are resolved,
the modeling techniques for larger 𝑦+ interface meshes will be explored.
</p>
<p>The VOF approach will extend the application of the turbulence modeling techniques to the moving and morphing
interface problems including the CFM operations in the micro-gravity environments.
</p>
<p>The successful validation of our modeling techniques against these flight-scale tank experiments builds further
confidence in reliability of our CFD tools for a wider range of CFM applications for supporting design of the long-term
space flight missions.
