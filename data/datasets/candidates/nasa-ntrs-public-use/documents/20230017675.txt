<p>Extreme Temperature Additively Manufactured 
</p>
<p>GRX-810 Alloy Development and Hot-fire Testing     
</p>
<p>for Liquid Rocket Engines 
</p>
<p>Paul R. Gradl1, Darren Tinker2, Benjamin Williams3
 
</p>
<p>NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, AL 35812 
</p>
<p>Timothy M. Smith4, Christopher Kantzos5 
NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 44135 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Additive manufacturing (AM) has revolutionized component design for liquid rocket engines by 
</p>
<p>offering rapid manufacturing capabilities. This has led to significant opportunities for development 
</p>
<p>and flight programs in the propulsion industry, resulting in cost and schedule savings, as well as 
</p>
<p>performance improvements through new designs and alloy development. A noteworthy example is 
</p>
<p>the GRX-810 oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) alloy, which was specifically developed for 
</p>
<p>extreme temperatures. This Ni-Co-Cr based alloy was created using integrated computational 
</p>
<p>materials engineering (ICME) techniques to focus on a new class of materials with exceptional 
</p>
<p>temperature and oxidation-resistant properties. The GRX-810 alloy utilizes AM processes to 
</p>
<p>incorporate nano-scale yttria particles throughout its microstructure, resulting in remarkable 
</p>
<p>enhancements. Compared to traditional Nickel-based superalloys, the GRX-810 alloy offers a two-
</p>
<p>fold increase in tensile strength, 1,000-fold better creep properties, and two-fold improvement in 
</p>
<p>oxidation resistance. NASA successfully demonstrated the development and manufacturing of 
</p>
<p>components using the GRX-810 alloy through laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) and laser powder 
</p>
<p>directed energy deposition (LP-DED) processes. Extensive efforts were made to model, evaluate 
</p>
<p>metallurgical properties, develop heat treatment processes, characterize the microstructure, and 
</p>
<p>determine mechanical properties. The GRX-810 alloy was specifically designed for aerospace 
</p>
<p>applications, including liquid rocket engine injectors, preburners, turbines, and hot-section 
</p>
<p>components, capable of withstanding temperatures up to 1,100 °C. The objective of this alloy 
</p>
<p>development is to bridge the temperature gap between traditional Nickel-based superalloys and 
</p>
<p>refractory alloys. This paper provides a comprehensive comparison of the GRX-810 alloy with 
</p>
<p>other aerospace alloys, discussing its microstructure, mechanical properties, processing 
</p>
<p>advancements, component development, and hot-fire testing results. The ultimate goal of this 
</p>
<p>development was to elevate the Technology Readiness Level (TRL) of the GRX-810 alloy, enabling 
</p>
<p>its integration into NASA and commercial aerospace applications. 
</p>

<p>I.  Introduction and Alloy Comparisons 
</p>
<p>Components on liquid rocket engines, such as combustion chambers, nozzles, injectors, gas generators, preburners, 
</p>
<p>and turbine static and rotating components, operate in highly challenging environments [1]. These environments 
</p>
<p>impose demands on functional design, materials, and fabrication processes, which are critical for the overall 
</p>
<p>performance of the rocket engine system. While each component serves a different purpose, they share many common 
</p>
<p>requirements. They must withstand the extreme conditions of high-pressure propellants, high-temperature gases, high 
</p>
<p>thermal gradients, and high duty cycles all while maintaining structural integrity with appropriate safety margins. 
</p>
<p>Additionally, these components need to be lightweight to meet the requirements of the engine system and launch 
</p>
<p>vehicle. Furthermore, many of these components feature intricate internal structures, including flow passages, orifices, 
</p>
<p>small restrictions, and complex curvatures, to fulfill their design objectives. To achieve these design requirements, 
</p>
<p>thin-walled configurations and combinations of resilient metal alloys are often employed. These components must 
</p>
<p>endure these demanding operational environments for multiple reuses, necessitating robust and durable materials and 
</p>
<p>manufacturing processes that are both repeatable and reproducible [2]. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The selection of materials plays a critical role in the success of aerospace applications, ensuring both component 
</p>
<p>and mission accomplishments. Aerospace propulsion components face unique challenges due to the demanding 
</p>
<p>operational environments and the need to satisfy programmatic and system requirements, including mass 
</p>
<p>considerations, economic factors, and acceptable risk levels [3]. The appropriate selection and utilization of metal 
</p>
<p>alloys, along with their processing throughout the component life cycle, significantly influence the performance of 
</p>
<p>these components when deployed. In these applications, materials must be carefully chosen to resist oxidation, creep, 
</p>
<p>extreme temperatures, propellant compatibility, hydrogen embrittlement, hydrocarbon coking, high pressures, high 
</p>
<p>thermal gradients, low- and high-cycle fatigue, as well as high-static and -dynamic loads prevalent in such harsh 
</p>
<p>environments. As integral parts of the overall system, these components must consistently demonstrate performance 
</p>
<p>and meet various requirements encompassing geometric aspects, physical and mechanical properties, thermophysical 
</p>
<p>characteristics, and manufacturability [3]. To meet these multifaceted requirements, the ideal material must exhibit 
</p>
<p>high strength, high ductility, corrosion resistance, high-fracture toughness, and often high conductivity. Such attributes 
</p>
<p>enable the material to fulfill its intended purpose effectively. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 1 provides an overview of different alloy classes and their respective usable temperature and stress regimes. 
</p>
<p>The most often used family of alloys used for high temperature environments includes nickel-, iron-nickel, and cobalt-
</p>
<p>based superalloys. These include alloys such as Alloy 625 (Inconel 625), Alloy 718 (Inconel 718), Co-Cr, Haynes 
</p>
<p>282, and Haynes 230, among several others [4]. Many of these alloys can typically operate at temperatures in excess 
</p>
<p>of 650°C for sustained durations and some in excess of 70% of their absolute melting temperature up to 1200°C for 
</p>
<p>short durations or reduced life [5]. These superalloys have been used in aerospace and propulsion applications for 
</p>
<p>many decades and have a lot of flight heritage. However, there are many limitations to superalloys. Creep, thermos-
</p>
<p>mechanical fatigue, corrosion, and surface erosion are common failures observed in service [6]. Age-hardenable 
</p>
<p>superalloys are also susceptible to artificial aging when exposed to extended life durations and properties can vary 
</p>
<p>during the lifecycle. The operating temperatures are often in the same ranges as the aging temperatures of these alloys 
</p>
<p>and thus can limit the maximum service temperatures.  </p>
<p></p>

<p> 
</p>
<p>Inconel 718 and Inconel 625 are some of the most widely adopted materials using additive manufacturing (AM) 
</p>
<p>processes such as powder bed fusion (PBF) and directed energy deposition (DED). This demand is driven by well-
</p>
<p>established literature, parameter and process maturity from AM machine manufacturers, established heat treatments, 
</p>
<p>and availability of material properties [7]. While these alloys are used for a wide variety of applications, they are not 
</p>
<p>ideal for all applications, yet are often used based on availability or convenience. Aluminum, titanium, and titanium-
</p>
<p>aluminide alloys have limited use applications for high temperature components. Additionally, Titanium is not used 
</p>
<p>in propulsion applications requiring hydrogen as a fuel due to hydrogen environment embrittlement [8]. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Ceramic matrix composites (CMC), including carbon-carbon (C-C) and carbon/silicon-carbide (C-SiC), are ultra-
</p>
<p>high temperature materials with the potential use up to (or above) 2343°C [9]. Several types of CMC’s have been in 
</p>
<p>developing and in use for rocket engine nozzle extensions for weight reduction and carbon-fiber composite fan blades 
</p>
<p>for aviation engines. The ultra-high use temperatures are attractive, but require the use of silicon-carbide, refractory-
</p>
<p>based, inhibitor, or sealants as anti-oxidation coatings. The CMC processing also requires specialized tooling, high 
</p>
<p>temperature carbonization furnaces, and coating systems that are not fully accessible across the aerospace industry.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Refractory metals like Niobium (Nb), Molybdenum (Mo), Tantalum (Ta), Rhenium (Re), and Tungsten (W) and 
</p>
<p>their alloys are crucial for extreme high-temperature environments. They offer high melt temperatures, the ability to 
</p>
<p>retain strength at elevated temperatures, and higher densities than Ni-base superalloys (except for Nb). Refractory 
</p>
<p>metals extend the operating temperature range from 1100 to 2500 °C, depending on the material. Traditional refractory 
</p>
<p>metal manufacturing is expensive due to high material costs, specialized powder production, and unique machining 
</p>
<p>and joining methods. AM addresses these cost issues through reduction in buy-to-fly material usage, though AM-
</p>
<p>refractory metal components may exhibit reduced mechanical properties [10]. This can be attributed to both AM-
</p>
<p>induced micro-cracking and grain growth at elevated temperatures. Choosing a specific refractory metal or alloy 
</p>
<p>depends on operating conditions like temperature, environment, stresses, and neutron fluence. Challenges in 
</p>
<p>manufacturing refractory components include high ductile-to-brittle transition temperatures, ultra-high-temperature 
</p>
<p>heat treatments, specialized oxidation coatings, and non-destructive evaluation (NDE) requirements [11]. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Propulsion component applications are continuously being pushed for higher performance, which requires new 
</p>
<p>more complex designs and new materials. New alloys being designed for these applications must meet a host of 
</p>
<p>requirements. Complex designs require novel manufacturing methods, such as AM, to fulfill the internal passages and 
</p>
<p>geometry requirements. This means the material must be readily printable using processes like PBF and DED. New </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>AIAA-2024-0997                                                                                        2024 AIAA SciTech, Orlando, FL 
                                                                                                                                                                                                         DOI: 10.2514/6.2024-0997 
</p>
<p>4 
</p>

</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>alloy development must meet programmatic and economic requirements and be both affordable and accessible. NASA 
</p>
<p>has been investigating new alloys to meet the various technical and programmatic requirements including the use of 
</p>
<p>oxide dispersion strengthening (ODS).  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>As indicated on Figure 1, between superalloys and refractory (Nb, Mo, W, etc.) alloys is this novel class of 
</p>
<p>materials known as ODS alloys. These can operate in temperatures that polycrystalline Ni-base superalloys are usually 
</p>
<p>inadequate. Currently, no known AM alloy has been designed for sustained use temperatures between 900-1200°C. 
</p>
<p>As such, the Glenn Research Center eXtreme temperature alloy (GRX-810) was designed to handle this specific 
</p>
<p>regime. GRX-810 is a solid solution Ni-Co-Cr base alloy strengthened by nano-scale yttria particles to be competitive 
</p>
<p>with Ni-base single crystals with up to 70% gamma prime volume fractions, but not necessarily replace [11]. Instead, 
</p>
<p>GRX-810 could replace wrought alloys such as Inconel 625, Haynes 230, and Inconel 718 or Nb-base alloys such as 
</p>
<p>C-103 in extreme environment applications. GRX-810 was designed for higher strength at temperatures above 810°C 
</p>
<p>compared to more traditional Ni-based superalloys. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>There are many targeted applications for the GRX-810 alloy. Modern advances in rotating detonation rocket 
</p>
<p>engines (RDRE) and high pressure staged combustion engines are increasing heat fluxes to higher levels compared to 
</p>
<p>heritage engines [12,13]. These include propulsion components such as liquid rocket engine injectors to allow for 
</p>
<p>higher operating temperatures and reduce faceplate erosion. Rotating components such as turbines, blisks, and other 
</p>
<p>hot section turbomachinery components could benefit from the reduced creep resistance and stress rupture at higher 
</p>
<p>operating temperatures. Other high temperature components such as heat exchangers (i.e., regeneratively-cooled 
</p>
<p>nozzles), control surfaces, and hot sections could also benefit from increased operating temperature alloys. GRX-810 
</p>
<p>has the potential to provide sustained lifecycles at higher operating temperatures and potentially reduce the mean time 
</p>
<p>between maintenance operations. Other benefits include the simplification of heat treatments, allowing use in the as-
</p>
<p>built condition or improved properties in the hot isostatic pressing (HIP) condition.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>This paper provides an overview of the GRX-810 alloy for use in these extreme environment applications. An 
</p>
<p>introduction of the microstructure, characterization, and material properties are provided. A comparison to other 
</p>
<p>traditional Ni-based superalloys will be presented to showcase the advantage and disadvantages. Various examples of 
</p>
<p>demonstration and hot-fire test units are provided, including a summary of liquid oxygen (LOX)/liquid hydrogen 
</p>
<p>(LH2) and LOX/liquid methane (LCH4) hot-fire testing. This paper demonstrates the continued maturation of the 
</p>
<p>GRX-810 alloy into component applications and goal to commercialize this alloy.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>II. Overview of GRX-810 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The GRX-810 alloy was developed and is undergoing further refinement to increase operating temperatures and 
</p>
<p>duty cycles for various propulsion components. This Ni-Co-Cr-based alloy incorporates an ODS coating, achieved 
</p>
<p>economically through AM. This innovative approach addresses the historical cost challenges associated with 
</p>
<p>traditional ODS alloy production. The alloy has demonstrated nearly double the tensile strength at elevated 
</p>
<p>temperatures (1093 °C) compared to traditional Ni-based superalloys like Alloy 625 and Alloy 718 [11]. Additionally, 
</p>
<p>the AM-produced Ni-Co-Cr alloy exhibits remarkable ductility and strain hardening, comparable to its wrought 
</p>
<p>counterparts.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>A coating process for the AM powder feedstock has been refined to enhance the high-temperature properties of 
</p>
<p>GRX-810, resulting in ODS with a well-distributed and stabilized microstructure at elevated temperatures. The 
</p>
<p>production of ODS GRX-810 components involves coating pre-alloyed base powder with &lt;1 wt.% Y2O3 
</p>
<p>nanoparticles, ensuring even dispersion when built using laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF).  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>An integrated computational materials engineering (ICME) approach used to develop the GRX-810 alloy by 
</p>
<p>iterating thermodynamic and density functional theory (DFT) models to predict an optimal chemistry. This chemistry 
</p>
<p>was simulated through thermally cycling from room temperature up to its melting temperature without precipitating 
</p>
<p>detrimental phases or compromising its grain structure. This was achieved by alloying the base Ni-Co-Cr equiatomic 
</p>
<p>composition with both refractories (W, Re, and Nb) and strengthening elements such as (Ti, Al, and C). The 
</p>
<p>thermodynamic models predicted B, Mo, Zr, Hf, and Ta as elements to avoid for phase stability or printability reasons. 
</p>
<p>W, Re, and Al were included to increase solid solution strengthening while Nb, Ti, and C were added to precipitate </p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>AIAA-2024-0997                                                                                        2024 AIAA SciTech, Orlando, FL 
                                                                                                                                                                                                         DOI: 10.2514/6.2024-0997 
</p>
<p>5 
</p>
<p> American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics 
</p>

</p>
<p>out stable MC carbides along grain boundaries [14]. The addition of the carbides and nano-scale oxide particles 
</p>
<p>ensured a stable grain structure up to temperatures near the alloys melting point [15].  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>To fabricate hot-fire components with GRX-810, the material required larger quantity feedstock production, 
</p>
<p>scalability, and analysis. Mechanical and microstructural tests were performed on scaled-up GRX-810 material 
</p>
<p>produced using a new lot (Lot 2) of atomized feedstock from Praxair and printed on an EOS M280. Similarly, to Lot 
</p>
<p>1 (original) GRX-810, this new feedstock was coated with sub 100nm Y2O3 particles using the resonant acoustic 
</p>
<p>mixing process [16]. Lot 2 GRX-810 builds on the EOS M280 achieved the best densities with laser energy densities 
</p>
<p>between of 70-90 J/mm3. Scanning electron images performed using a Tescan MAIA3 in the ultra-high resolution 
</p>
<p>(UHR) configuration at 20kV were employed to compare the oxide dispersion between the “scaled-up” Lot 2 GRX-
</p>
<p>810 vs the original Lot 1 M100 builds as described in previous studies [14][17]. Figure 2 compares the micrographs 
</p>
<p>between the two GRX-810 lots.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>The grain structure and orientation between as-built Lot 1 and Lot 2 GRX-810 builds is surprisingly similar 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

<p>Mechanical testing for this study was performed at Metcut Research Inc. and NASA Glenn Research Center. 
</p>
<p>Tensile tests were performed at room temperature, 427°C, 650°C, 871°C at .127mm/min for the first 1.5% strain then 
</p>
<p>it was increased to 1.016mm/s until failure in accordance with the ASTM E8/E21 standard, and at 1,093 °C with a 
</p>
<p>constant strain rate of 1.016mm/min in accordance with the ASTM E21-17 standard. Following the tensile tests, creep 
</p>
<p>tests were performed at 1,093 °C by Metcut and NASA GRC in accordance with the ASTM E139-11 standard. Testing 
</p>
<p>of creep samples was continued until rupture (unless otherwise stated), after which they were then rapidly air cooled 
</p>
<p>to maintain the fracture surface. All specimens were tested in Z-orientation (parallel the print direction) unless 
</p>
<p>otherwise specified in the description [2]. Figure 3 compares the mechanical properties of these two lots in both the 
</p>
<p>as-built condition (dashed lines) and HIPed condition (solid lines).  </p>

<p>From Figure 4 a few notable differences between Lot 1 and Lot 2 GRX-810 can be observed. At lower temperatures 
</p>
<p>Lot 1 GRX-810 exhibited higher tensile and yield strength (Figure 3a and 3b). This can likely be explained by Hall-
</p>
<p>Petch as the Lot 1 GRX-810 did exhibit a finer grain size [20]. This difference in tensile strength lessened as the 
</p>
<p>temperature increased and both versions of GRX-810 exhibited similar strengths at 1093°C. However, the elongation 
</p>
<p>(Figure 3c) of Lot 2 EOS M280 GRX-810 samples was significantly higher at 1093C compared to the original Lot 1 
</p>
<p>GRX-810. This may also be a consequence of the grain differences between the two materials. This difference in 
</p>
<p>elongation may also represent an improved oxide dispersion in Lot 2 compared to Lot 1. Interestingly in NiCoCr based 
</p>
<p>alloys, tensile elongation improves at elevated temperatures when nano-scale oxides are included in the microstructure 
</p>
<p>[21]. This improvement is shown in Figure 3d, where non-ODS GRX-810 was found to be much less ductile at 1093°C 
</p>
<p>compared to the other ODS versions. At these elevated temperatures, grain boundary oxidation begins to play a strong 
</p>
<p>role in mechanical properties and the oxides are thought to help improve oxidation and stabilize grain boundaries 
</p>
<p>resulting in improved ductility and tougher materials. Whether this is due to the oxides affecting the oxidation behavior 
</p>
<p>itself, or simply stabilizing the microstructure is not well understood.  The better oxide distribution may also be playing 
</p>
<p>a role in the creep properties of Lot 2 GRX-810 as shown in Figure 5.

<p>III. L-PBF Process Examples and Hardware Development 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>NASA prioritized early material development with the GRX-810 alloy to establish optimal process build 
</p>
<p>parameters that align with the anticipated mechanical and thermophysical properties. Concurrently, emphasis was 
</p>
<p>placed on component development in the initial stages of GRX-810 advancement to verify that intended complexities 
</p>
<p>and geometries can be successfully built. GRX-810 is being proposed for various applications, specifically heat 
</p>
<p>exchangers, turbomachinery, and components operating in high heat flux environments. In the context of liquid rocket 
</p>
<p>engines, specific applications include injectors designed to mitigate the necessity for coated faceplates, regeneratively-
</p>
<p>cooled nozzles, and turbines in turbopumps. Select examples are shown in Figure 7.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fabrication demonstrators of both non-shrouded and shrouded turbine blisks are illustrated in Figure 7A and 7E, 
</p>
<p>respectively, designed to function at temperatures surpassing current limits. Figure 7B showcases an inducer aimed at 
</p>
<p>demonstrating blade and internal complexity, while Figure 7F exhibits a large-scale blade featuring internal 
</p>
<p>instrumentation passages. Additionally, Figure 7C presents an injector with impinging elements, and Figure 7D 
</p>
<p>displays a regeneratively-cooled nozzle.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>

</p>

</p>
<p>turbine blade with integral instrumentation ports.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The GRX-810 alloy demonstrated comparable build speeds to typical Ni-based alloys, such as Alloy 625 and Alloy 
</p>
<p>718. Build limitations and geometries demonstrated similarities to other Ni-based alloys. Additionally, the powder 
</p>
<p>exhibited consistent performance through multiple reuses during builds, maintaining uniform build geometry. 
</p>
<p>Ongoing efforts continue for process development to advance the maturity of the GRX-810 alloy across various L-PBF 
</p>
<p>platforms, including post-processing refinement. This entails optimizing the HIP process, as well as refining 
</p>
<p>techniques like polishing, surface enhancements, and exploring other AM processes such as laser powder directed 
</p>
<p>energy deposition (LP-DED). 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The injector and nozzle shown in Figure 7 proceeded through the full manufacturing process flow to service in 
</p>
<p>hot-fire testing. Both parts were designed for L-PBF AM with integral manifolding and channels to minimize 
</p>
<p>secondary fabrication processes (i.e., welding, brazing). Limited access to nozzle manifolds required iterations for </p>

</p>

</p>
<p>

<p>The GRX-810 alloy technology readiness level (TRL) advanced through hot fire testing in a relevant environment. 
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>Revolution (RAMFIRE) collaboration. The RAMFIRE test series only incorporated the GRX-810 injector whereas 
</p>
<p>the ORCA test series used both a GRX-810 injector and nozzle. Liquid oxygen/liquid hydrogen (LOX/LH2) and liquid 
</p>

<p>V. Conclusions and Future Work 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Additive manufacturing has played a transformative role in the design and fabrication of liquid rocket engine 
</p>
<p>components, exemplified by the development and successful demonstration of the GRX-810 oxide dispersion 
</p>
<p>strengthened (ODS) alloy. This Ni-Co-Cr based alloy, engineered using integrated computational materials 
</p>
<p>engineering (ICME) techniques, showcases exceptional temperature and oxidation-resistant properties. The 
</p>
<p>incorporation of nanoscale yttria particles in the powder feedstock results in remarkable enhancements – offering an 
</p>
<p>improvement in tensile strength, creep properties, and oxidation resistance compared to traditional Nickel-based 
</p>
<p>superalloys. The GRX-810 alloy exhibits a two-fold increase in tensile strength, 1,000-fold better creep properties, 
</p>
<p>and a two-fold improvement in oxidation resistance compared to traditional Nickel-based superalloys. The alloy was 
</p>
<p>demonstrated on different scale laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) platforms and with similar properties. Additionally, 
</p>
<p>the single heat treatment using hot isostatic pressing (HIP), minimizes grain growth, enhancing the material's structural 
</p>
<p>integrity during AM. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The successful application of the GRX-810 alloy in hot-fire testing, particularly in liquid oxygen/liquid hydrogen 
</p>
<p>(LOX/LH2) and liquid oxygen/liquid methane (LOX/LCH4) environments, demonstrates its robustness and readiness 
</p>
<p>for aerospace applications. The GRX-810 technology readiness level (TRL) has advanced through hot-fire testing, 
</p>
<p>accumulating 3,117 seconds of hot fire across various pressures and propellant combinations. The GRX-810 injector 
</p>
<p>shows a noticeable improvement in robustness compared to common superalloys, showcasing its potential for 
</p>
<p>commercial aerospace applications. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Future work involves additional scaling and analysis of the GRX-810 alloy feedstock and both L-PBF and laser 
</p>
<p>powder directed energy deposition (LP-DED) builds for large-scale production and broader applications. The focus 
</p>
<p>will be on the continued maturation of the GRX-810 alloy into component applications, with the final goal of 
</p>
<p>   </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>AIAA-2024-0997                                                                                        2024 AIAA SciTech, Orlando, FL 
                                                                                                                                                                                                         DOI: 10.2514/6.2024-0997 
</p>
<p>15 
</p>
<p> American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>commercialization. Research efforts are exploring additional applications within aerospace and into the energy 
</p>
<p>industry. Collaboration with industry partners is crucial for integrating the GRX-810 alloy into both NASA and 
</p>
<p>commercial aerospace programs, elevating its TRL for widespread use.
