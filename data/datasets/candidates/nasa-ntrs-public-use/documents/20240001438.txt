<p>Qualification and Certification – NASA Definition
</p>
<p>6
</p>
<p>• Answer varies by industry and even by culture within industries
• The following interpretations are common within NASA:
</p>
<p>• Qualification applies to 
• Parts and components
• Processes
</p>
<p>• Certification applies to
• Design (e.g., status following Design Certification Review)
• Subsystems (e.g., engine level certification test series)
• Integrated system (e.g., collective certification for flight for launch vehicle)
</p>
<p>Certification is granted by the responsible reviewing authority when the verification 
process is complete, assuring both design and as-built hardware will meet the 
established requirements to safely and reliably complete the intended mission.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>• We have been qualifying flight parts for a long time
• We know how to integrate parts into complex systems
</p>
<p>AM Qual is not that special, BUT…
</p>
<p>• Individual nature of AM
• Potential failure modes on a stand-along operation  
• Process sensitive
</p>
<p>• Rapidly evolving tech 
• Lacking in experiences and standards of performance
• How do we manage the opportunity of a rapid Design-Fail-Fix cycle 
</p>
<p>(or Design-Analyze-Build-Test) cycle while also following an 
established (“slow”) qualification and certification process?</p>

<p>Observations of Current AM Implementations  (Space)
</p>
<p>• Highly motivated to print, which places the most complicated of geometries into critical parts/sub 
systems
</p>
<p>• Design/Fail/Fix – empowered by AM
• Going for the Grand Slam
</p>
<p>• Process is in place to collect a lot of “data” but not organized enough to utilize the big data
</p>
<p>• Individual and local aspect of AM – “I’m solely responsible for my material, structure and part 
integrity”
• There is more to AM alloys than bulk chemistry and tensile strength
• Strength, ductility, fatigue, heat resistance, cryogenic ductility, toughness, tearing resistance, fatigue crack        
</p>
<p>growth, stress rupture, hydrogen embrittlement, oxidizer compatibility, flammability, intergranular cracking, general 
corrosion, stress corrosion cracking, etc.
</p>
<p>• Just like other traditionally made alloy systems, AM alloys are complex and require precise metallurgical control to 
meet engineering expectations against a variety of failure mechanisms; Process → Structure → Property relationship
</p>
<p>• The fact that AM parts needs to be successfully integrated into System</p>

<p>Importance of Structural Integrity
</p>
<p>11
</p>
<p>• This is a notional Rocket Propulsion Turbopump Assembly – multiple AM components
• Let’s say it will be a part of human-rated Launch Vehicle NASA will procure
• These components will be classified as per NASA Fracture Control Tech Standard (NASA-
</p>
<p>STD-5019) – Exempt, Non-Fracture Critical, or Fracture Critical; And it would be subjected 
to the requirements of NASA AM Standards (NASA-STD-6030 and 6033)
</p>
<p>• Fracture Critical – a failure due to the presence of a crack is a catastrophic hazard
• Material and manufacturing processes produce structures with cracks or flaws
• The presence of cracks of sufficient size reduces the strength and life of the structure 
</p>
<p>• Damage Tolerance – demonstration that a component can survive the service life (with a 
safety factor) in the presence of an undetected flaw or damage
</p>
<p>• Components are manufactured from aerospace quality materials using controlled 
processes
</p>
<p>• Components are inspected for damage (cracks, flaws, defects, etc.)
• Components are shown by test or analysis to be tolerant to undetected flaws</p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>AM Fracture Critical Components – why so special?
</p>
<p>12
</p>
<p>AM Materials Wrought Materials
</p>
<p>Material Quality Material quality is highly process-dependent; 
variation from machine to machine.
</p>
<p>More consistent quality due to small number of 
dedicated production mills with extensive 
experience.
</p>
<p>Flaws Flaws are inherent to the process
</p>
<p>Need to also worry about process-escape flaws
</p>
<p>Flaws are typically rare
</p>
<p>Inspectability Inspectability varies based on 
component geometry; inspection techniques 
still under development
</p>
<p>Raw stock is commonly fully inspectable, parts 
generally have high inspectability
</p>
<p>Heritage Little to no experience for critical applications, 
rapid technological development
</p>
<p>Decades of experience</p>
<p></p>

<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Challenge with Highly Complex AM Components
</p>
<p>13
</p>
<p>• NDE Inspections are foundational in that the inspection bounds the flaw size that might go undetected; we’re 
“damage tolerant” - still fulfill the mission requirements if we establish the initial flaw size = largest flaw that an NDE 
might miss
</p>
<p>• Real Problem : types of hardware that users desire to produce places the most complicated of geometries into 
critical applications (blessings of AM), but this often leads to critical AM parts with little or no access for 
quantitative, post-build surface and volumetric NDE (curse of AM)
</p>
<p>• In the absence of reliable inspection to bound the initial flaw sizes, traditional Fracture control rationale does not 
work
</p>
<p> 
• What is the Fracture Control rationale for un-inspectable Fracture Critical AM components; how do we 
</p>
<p>communicate/manage the associated risks
</p>
<p>• Alternate approach based on risk-based acceptance; thinking in the context of “distribution” (flaw size and rate of 
flaw occurrence); identification and control of process escapes, zone-based assessment; Probabilistic Damage 
Tolerance Analysis</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>But Wait! Words Matter
</p>
<p>14
</p>
<p>• Flaw - an imperfection or discontinuity that may be detectable by nondestructive testing and is not necessarily 
rejectable
</p>
<p>• Defects -  one or more flaws whose aggregate size, shape, orientation, location, or properties do not meet specified 
acceptance criteria and are rejectable
</p>
<p>• Engineering Cracks – a flaw with sharp, flat interface that is large enough to be treated using continuum linear-
elastic fracture mechanics</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Flaw State Characterization
</p>
<p>15
</p>
<p>• Inherent Flaws - Flaws that are representative of the characterized nominal operation of a qualified AM process
• Expected to be common enough that direct characterization is feasible. “Characterized” implies that most inherent 
</p>
<p>flaws have been observed as part of AM process development and are included in the metallurgical and mechanical 
qualification data set
</p>
<p>• Not defined by size (e.g., small ≠ inherent), but are expected to be small in a well-controlled AM process
• Process-escape Flaws - Flaws that are not representative of the characterized nominal operation of a qualified AM process
</p>
<p>• Associated with some sort of process failure
• May or may not be larger than inherent flaws, thought generally are expected to be larger
• Have lower occurrence rates than inherent flaws
• May or may not be detectable</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>• Fact #1 – current metallic AM 
processes do not produce perfectly 
dense material; generate some 
degree of porosity, flaws, void even 
operating nominally
</p>
<p>• Fact #2 – Process escape flaws are 
rare
</p>
<p>• Systematically defining, 
characterizing, and controlling for 
[inherent] flaws that represent AM 
process can be used as the foundation 
for developing fracture control 
rationale for un-inspectable parts
</p>
<p>• To handle process escape flaws, one 
should require understanding of the 
potential failure modes associated 
with AM processes (P-FMEA analysis) 
</p>

</p>

<p>Probabilistic Risk vs. Deterministic Life
</p>
<p>19
</p>
<p>• NASA’s current practice of calculating structural integrity is deterministic yielding a (fatigue) life 
calculating number of cycles to failure
</p>
<p>• Could Probabilistic Damage Tolerance Analysis provide a means of assessing the part risks in the 
absence of a bounding initial flaw size?
</p>
<p>• Advantages 
• Automated tools allow for component-scale assessment of risk more robust
• Can potentially reduce conservatism relative to the “worst-on-worst” deterministic approaches
• Does not require extensive fatigue testing
• Is consistent with conventional NASA’s deterministic damage tolerance approach (still need to know the critical initial 
</p>
<p>flaw sizes)
</p>
<p>• Disadvantages
• Requires extensive characterization of flaw state distributions
• Change of paradigm : Risk vs Life
</p>
<p>• NASA working with Southwest Research Institute on DARWIN analysis tool to develop the 
framework</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Opportunities
• Opportunities
</p>
<p>• Since this approach relies heavily on accounting for and understanding all potential flaw states 
in AM parts, it drives the users to pay more attention on flaw characterization during the 
process development
</p>
<p>• Could accelerate the innovations in in-situ monitoring tools, advanced data analytics, ICME
</p>
<p>• P-FEMA practices becomes more prevalent for AM
• Provides a means to thoroughly assess the AM process and identify the potential process 
</p>
<p>escapes that would make lead to flaws. Users then can develop the rationale to mitigate 
that escape as identified from each failure mode
</p>
