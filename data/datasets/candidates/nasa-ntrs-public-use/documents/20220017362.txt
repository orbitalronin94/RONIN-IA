The recent developments in small satellite technology has opened the door to a new paradigm
of space missions. Traditional large-scale planetary orbiters and atmospheric probes can
be potentially augmented with smaller and cheaper small satellite architectures. The recent
developments in entry, descent, and landing (EDL) technology associated with deployable
aeroshells may allow for small satellite orbit insertion via aerocapture. This paper serves to
understand the design trade space for small satellite mission concepts while identifying potential
candidate designs for more detailed analysis.

I. Introduction

The recent developments in small satellite (SmallSat) technology has opened the door to a new paradigm of space
missions. A recent NASA Technical Paper thoroughly documents the current state-of-the-art in small spacecraft

technology [1]. SmallSats are a smaller-sized form factor of a traditional satellite. SmallSats are compelling to
space mission designers due to their lower-cost associated with the utilization of commercial-off-the-shelf components
and rideshare opportunities as secondary payloads. Secondary payload adapters impose restrictive mass and volume
requirements for SmallSats, which must stow prior to launch and deploy once released from the adapter, such as the
EELV Secondary Payload Adapter (ESPA) [2]. Currently there are many variants of the ESPA platform, including some
that are configured to serve as orbital transfer vehicles. Figure 1 depicts the standard ESPA varient. As of 2018, NASA
Science Mission Directorate (SMD) has an active policy of integrating ESPA rings on SMD missions that have excess
ascent performance for secondary payload rideshare opportunities [3].

NASA currently has a significant investment into achieving science missions with SmallSats. A more detailed
explanation of the history of these efforts and current investments can be found in Reference [5]. In 2019, NASA
released its small spacecraft strategic plan [6], which calls for the utilization of small spacecraft to enable unique science
observations including interplanetary missions.

Fig. 2 Visualization of current, past, and future small satellite missions. Credit: NASA

Figure 2 shows a visualization of past, current, and future NASA SmallSat missions. The success of the Mars Cube
One SmallSats in 2018 signaled the potential applicability of interplanetary SmallSats. Consequently, future missions
may require orbit insertion about the destination of interest to achieve the desired science orbit. Unlike traditional
satellites, the lower mass and smaller dimensions of SmallSats may make achieving insertion costs on the order of a few
km/s quite challenging. Rideshare integration with primary payloads reaching the same destination may alleviate some
orbit insertion concerns; however, the desired orbit is not guaranteed. Electric propulsion is a potential alternative for
high ΔV, low propellant mass orbit insertion, but at the cost of long insertion time. For destinations with an atmosphere,
aerodynamic drag can be used to quickly achieve the high ΔV maneuver through a method known as aerocapture, as
shown in Figure 3.

Aerocapture is an aeroassist technique that aims to significantly reduce orbit insertion costs. Despite not having been
demonstrated in space, aerocapture leverages many flight-proven technologies from EDL. These include a protective
aeroshell, active guidance, and thermal protection system, amongst others. In 2016, a panel of aerocapture technologists
from NASA concluded that, given the shared technology with EDL, aerocapture is ready for flight implantation at
Mars and Venus [8, 9]. Nevertheless, a fundamental engineering challenge to the applications of existing NASA EDL
technology is the consideration of SmallSats as secondary payloads.

Rigid aeroshells are the most used configuration for atmospheric flight missions. The secondary payload fairing
specifications will constrain the maximum rigid aeroshell diameter conceivable. Existing atmospheric entry probes, such
as Pioneer Venus, have diameters suitable for fairing specifications. Rigid aeroshells have advantages in certain areas,
such as ability to withstand higher aeroheating environments and mechanical simplicity. On the other hand, deployable
aeroshells can stow compactly during launch and deploy to much larger sizes than rigid aeroshells. The investigation of
deployable aeroshells is an on-going technological investment by NASA, including mechanically or inflatable deployable
hypersonic decelerators. The Adaptable Deployable Entry Placement Technology (ADEPT) is an aeroshell platform that
deploys mechanically like an umbrella. The platform has been analyzed for SmallSat payload configurations and mission
concepts to Venus and Mars [4]. Earth sounding rocket tests [10] of the CubeSat-sized platform variant, known as
Nano-ADEPT [11], demonstrated the potential viability of SmallSat deployable decelerators. The Hypersonic Inflatable
Aerodynamic Decelerator (HIAD) is an aeroshell platform that deploys through toroid inflation. The recent work on the
platform included a November 2022 Earth-based flight test of a 6m diameter variant [12]. Although HIAD has been
proposed for larger Mars human-scale missions, it has seen limited analysis for downscaling to small satellites variants
[13]. Understanding the potential capabilities of such a variant is imperative for producing alternative deployable
aeroshell designs. As a result, this paper focuses on the investigation of small satellite aerocapture concepts using the
HIAD platform. Design analysis for SmallSat-sized HIAD aeroshells and aerocapture orbit insertion performance at
Earth, Mars, and Venus are presented.

An engineering challenge associated with aerocapture is ensuring the aeroshell is designed to survive the aeroheating
environment. Understanding how downscaling HIAD aeroshell effects the environment is important for the selection
of potential configurations. A Sutton-Graves peak stagnation point convective heat rate model is utilized to provide
an estimate for the aeroheating environment experienced for each HIAD configuration at Earth, Mars, and Venus.
Radiative heat calculations are not modeled in this analysis. Figure 5 provides a comparsion of the peak heat rate for

each configuration at each destination targeting the same 600km circular orbit. For the plot, the associated aerocapture
trajectories for Earth and Venus have identical entry velocities of 10.3 km/s while the associated trajectory for Mars has
an entry velocity of 6 km/s. The results show that peak convective heat rates below 100 W/cm2 are achievable with
1.5m, 2m, and 2.5m diameter designs at each destination with the heat rate decreasing with increasing diameter. For the
same HIAD configuration and entry velocity, the heat rate experienced at Venus is roughly 1.5 times larger than values
at Earth. Despite the differing entry velocity, the heat rate experienced at Mars is roughly 33% and 22% the value at
Earth and Venus respectively.

The results presented in Figure 5 neglect payload mass. Consequently, adding payload mass will increase the
ballistic coefficient and thereby the resulting peak convective heat rates. For this analysis, a heat rate limit of 75 W/cm2

is established based off HIAD flexible thermal protection system (TPS) capabilities. Given the trends, Mars aerocapture
with a significantly large SmallSat payload mass (e.g. 150 kg) might be conceivable given the current TPS capabilities.
The large heat rates at Venus may require high-performance TPS materials along with large diameter HIAD aeroshells
to maximize SmallSat payload mass. Earth-return aerocapture may also follow similar trends as Venus (though lower
heat rates allows for larger SmallSat payload masses for a given TPS material limit).

Overall, this section highlights downsized HIAD aeroshell configurations for SmallSat payloads. Stowed configuration
in secondary payload adapters can potentially achieve a deployed diameter up to 2.5m. Analysis of aeroheating
environment from SmallSat HIAD trajectories and current flexible thermal protection system limits supports the potential
implementation of such configurations for Mars and Earth aerocapture scenarios. The high aeroheating environment
experienced at Venus may necessitate larger diameter aeroshell configurations and/or new thermal protection system
materials that can increase maximum heat rate limits.

III. Aerocapture Analysis
This section provides aerocapture orbit insertion performance analysis for SmallSat-sized HIAD vehicles. First, the

design space for candidate aerocapture mission concepts at Earth, Mars, and Venus is presented to provide qualitative
insight into the design space. Second, Monte Carlo simulation orbit insertion results are presented to provide quantiative
insight to the qualitative findings. Analysis in this section is conducted using the Program to Optimize Simulated
Trajectories II (POST2).

A. Aerocapture Flight Envelope
The uniqueness of SmallSat-focused aerocapture vehicles necessitates an understanding of the trade-space. The

potential science missions drives the selection of orbits targeted during aerocapture. Such targeted orbits can vary from
low=altitude circular to highly elliptical orbits. The low-mass and small form-factor designs of the SmallSat vehicles
may lead to a different range of ballistic coefficients and/or lift-to-drag ratios as compared to traditional rigid aeroshells
used to deliver larger satellites and robotic payloads. Each planetary destination has a unique flight environment that
influences the orbit insertion performance during aerocapture. It is imperative to analyze the design-space to identify
feasible SmallSat mission concepts. One method to understand the trade-space is via the aerocapture flight envelope.

The flight envelope is a metric to understand whether different parameterized vehicle designs have sufficient control
authority in the presence of trajectory dispersions. These unforeseeable dispersions, such as atmospheric, aerodynamic,
and delivery state, can potentially affect the risk posture of aerocapture. Most aerocapture literature present a metric
known as the corridor width. This width, defined by the inertial entry flight path angle, is typically determined by
solving for the trajectories associated with the minimum and maximum control limit ∗ that achieve the target apogee
altitude. The steep-side is defined by the most negative flight path angle that achieves the target orbit using the maximim
control limit while the shallow-side is defined by the least negative flight path angle that achieved the target orbit
using the minimum control limit. Individually applying the ±3𝜎 dispersions and solving for the corresponding width

representation of a flight envelope in altitude vs flight path angle space. The solid and dashed blue lines bound the
corridor width associated with the flight control limits. The green, red, and black lines show in the individual application
of each potential trajectory dispersion type, which visually serve to reduce the bounds of the corridor width. They
greyed region, the flight envelope, denotes the resulting restrictive bounds. As a first-order method, having a positive
corridor width indicates that the vehicle design has sufficient control authority to overcome the most restrictive of the
3𝜎 trajectory uncertainties. Although Monte Carlo simulations are best used to quantitatively demonstrate control
robustness, the qualitative observations and results obtained from the flight envelope analysis generally agree with the
overall trends from the Monte Carlo results.

The trends from the flight envelope assessment as applied to SmallSat aerocapture trajectories are presented in the
paper. Detailed analysis pertaining to the flight envelope assessment can be found in Reference [15]. The purpose of
this section is to provide highlights of the key findings that came out of the analysis and how they tie to the overall study
conducted.

The aerocapture flight envelope is computed to understand whether the resulting corridor widths are sufficient
control-wise. The lifting and ballistic aerocapture trajectories are parametrized by lift-to-drag ratio and ballistic
coefficient. For lifting vehicles, the L/D and ballistic coefficient are assumed to be 0.25 and 25 kg/m2, respectively.
The latter value is selected to be representative of values achieved with a SmallSat HIAD aeroshell design explored in
Section II. For ballistic vehicles (L/D = 0), the ballistic coefficient is assumed to be able to increase to a value 20 times
the base value of 25 kg/m2 (ballistic coefficient ratio (BCR) equal to 20). The built-in optimizer in POST2 is utilized to
determine the required inertial entry flight path angle that achieves the target circular orbit altitude between 200 and 600
km at Venus, Earth, and Mars. To normalize the comparison between lifting and ballistic, the same entry velocity is
selected and corresponding ±3𝜎 dispersions for the atmospheric profile and delivery state are assumed. The resulting
atmospheric and delivery state dispersions at each planet are different (e.g different GRAM models, different flight path
angle dispersion amount). Due to the differences in flight control, the resulting aerodynamic dispersions differ (±20%
in L/D for lifting, ±0.05 in L/D for ballistic). For each planet, a breakdown of the resulting corridor widths as a function
of target orbit with each uncertainty source individually applied is shown as well as a visualization of a sample target
orbit’s flight envelope.

Figure 8 shows the results for Venus aerocapture with an entry velocity of 11.5 km/s. The atmospheric uncertainties
produced by VenusGRAM lead to a small reduction in the corridor width for lifting vehicles but a substantial reduction
for ballistic vehicles (both numerically and visually). Aerodynamic uncertainty, as applied to Venus, is shown to produce
a pronounced decrease in the corridor width to both lifting and ballistic vehicles. The combined uncertainty, shown by
the magenta dashed lines in subplots (a) and (b), correspond to the most restrictive bounds produced by the aerodynamic
and atmospheric uncertainties. This can be visualized by the example result shown in subplot (c) where the shallow-side
atmospheric (red dashed) and steep-side aerodynamic (green solid) uncertainties form the most restrictive reduction

to the original corridor width bound by the blue solid and dashed lines. The resulting combined uncertainty width
should be larger than the delivery uncertainty to indicate that the vehicle has sufficient controllability. At Venus, the
lifting vehicle has sufficiently large margin to overcome delivery state dispersions; however, ballistic vehicles have no
margin. For the applied delivery state error of ±0.28◦, the resulting width of 0.56◦ constitutes 68% of the original (no
uncertainty) corridor width (see subplot (b)). This indicates that delivery state uncertainty can potentially influence the
robustness of the assessed ballistic vehicle at Venus. Subplot (d) indicates that the applied delivery dispersion leads to a
negative flight envelope (e.g. grey shaded region does not exist). To achieve a zero-width flight envelope, the delivery
state dispersion would need to be decreased to ±0.18◦. This reduction is potentially achievable with improvements in
deep space navigation but may not be practical for a secondary payload SmallSat mission. As a result, lifting vehicle
configurations for SmallSat payloads might be more desirable for Venusian mission concepts.

B. Performance Under Uncertanity
The aerocapture flight envelope assessment in the previous section provided qualitative insight into how trajectory

dispersions may affect the controllability of SmallSat aerocapture trajectories. To provide quantiative insight, Monte
Carlo simulations are conducted at Venus, Earth, and Mars.

Orbit insertion performance analysis via Monte Carlo simulations is conducted at each destination using POST2. 8001
Monte Carlo simulations are conducted for each planetary destination using a outer-inner loop POST2 implementation
as shown in Figure 11. The outer-loop servers as the truth trajectory model with the same 3𝜎 dispersions sources
of atmospheric, aerodynamic, and delivery state as used in the flight envelope assessment. The inner-loop consists a
recursive instance of POST2 running as a generalized numerical predictor-corrector aerocapture (GENNPC) guidance
algorithm. The inner-loop contains a simplified nominal knowledge of the vehicle aerodynamics as well as atmosphere.
Every guidance cycle, the inner-loop POST2 GENNPC determines the bank angle that minimizes the apoapsis error by
numerically integrating the trajectory from the current vehicle state to atmospheric exit. Perfect state knowledge is
assumed. A first-order fading memory filter is utilized for calculating correction gains to the inner-loop trajectory model
based on comparison of sensed aerodynamic acceleration values from the outer-loop to corresponding values computed
using inner-loop trajectory model. A HIAD SmallSat-sized lifting vehicle is assumed in this analysis with a L/D of 0.25,
ballistic coefficient of 25 kg/m2, and bank angle flight control. For this simplified analysis, no out-of-plane performance
is considered. For Earth the guidance targets a 500km circular orbit, for Mars the guidance targets a 400km circular
orbit, and for Venus the guidance targets a 300km circular orbit. All performance metrics, including propulsive ΔV
costs are computed using POST2.

To obtain propellant mass estimates, the 99th percentile total ΔV values are utilized assuming the ideal rocket
equation. A hydrazine thruster with a specific impulse of 200 sec is assumed along with a entry wet mass of 150kg
( 25 kg/m2 ballistic coefficient using 2m SmallSat HIAD aeroshell + 55kg payload). The resulting propellant mass
estimates are shown in Table 2. Despite seeming large, the resulting 99th percentile ΔV values lead to small propellant
mass requirements, generally around 10-15 kg. This correlates to less than 10% of the entry mass being allocated
to propellant. With a higher specific impulse system, this mass allocation can be potentially reduced. For SmallSat
missions, the entry wet mass can be potentially increased up to 450kg based on secondary port payload max capacity.
This has the effect of increasing the vehicle’s ballistic coefficient and the corresponding aerothermodynamic heating.
Generally, increasing the ballistic coefficient generally doesnt affect the orbit insertion performance significantly due
to the fact that numerical predictor-corrector guidance algorithms are not dependent on ballistic coefficient in their
respective formulations. The resulting 99th percentile total ΔV values can be used as first-order approximations in
determining how the corresponding propellant mass changes with changes to entry wet mass. Figure 15 depicts these
trends and shows a nearly linear relation of increasing propellant mass with increasing wet mass. But, the corresponding
propellant mass allocation percentage is nearly constant generally staying below 10%.

IV. Conclusion
This paper presents the investigation of SmallSat HIAD aerocapture concepts. Candidate HIAD aeroshell designs that

conform to secondary payload requirements are presented along with trends associated with aeroheating environments
and aeroshell mass. The design-space for SmallSat aerocapture trajectories is assessed using the aerocapture flight
envelope. Trends from the flight envelope are used to inform design considerations in quantiative aerocapture orbit
insertion performance simulations. The key findings are discussed below.

Downscaling of aeroshell geometries to accommodate secondary payload constraints is shown to provide a unique
challenge in potentially high aeroheating environments, such as Venus and Earth. Smaller aeroshell geometries (e.g.
smaller nose radius) along with lower max heat rate value associated with flexible TPS, limits the design-space for
SmallSat-sized aeroshell designs. Potential design considerations to mitigate this challenge include lowering the ballistic
coefficient and increasing the nose radius. The 1.5m, 2m, and 2.5m HIAD designs analyzed in this paper are shown to

13



not only potentially achieve this design consideration, but also deliver a significant SmallSat payload mass to Venus,
Earth, and Mars. Aerocapture flight envelope assessment indicate lifting flight control with modest L/D provides
sufficient control margin for SmallSat HIAD aerocapture trajectories at Venus, Earth, and Mars. Although ballistic
flight control is shown to provide sufficient control margin at Earth and Mars, the large required increase in ballistic
coefficient may prove to be difficult to achieve mechanically. At each destination, Monte Carlo aerocapture simulations
of a candidate lifting guidance algorithm indicate 100% capture success rate with orbit insertion ΔV propellant mass
allocations below 10% of the total entry mass.

While the results in the paper are not fully extensive, they provide insight into the applicability of interplanetary
SmallSat missions using aerocapture and HIAD technology. Further analysis can be done in TPS analysis with
higher-fidelity aeroheating models to refine TPS mass fractions and SmallSat payload mass estimates. Furthermore, trade
studies between ADEPT and HIAD SmallSat aeroshell design can provide further insight into design considerations.
