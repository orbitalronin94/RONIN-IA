<p>Extravehicular Mobility Unit System-Level Model (SINDA

<p>EVA 80

<p>Noah Andersen1 and Bruno Miranda2

<p>HX5, LLC, JSC Engineering Technology and Science (JETS2), Houston, TX, 77058 
</p>
<p>During United States Extravehicular Activity 80 (US EVA 80), water was observed in the 
</p>
<p>helmet of an Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU) during cabin repressurization. Through a 
</p>
<p>comparative analysis and Test, Teardown, and Evaluation (TT&amp;E) of this EMU, the most 
</p>
<p>likely cause of the US EVA 80 water failure was determined to be sublimator carryover caused 
</p>
<p>by a comparatively high latent load (water vapor production by the crewmember). High latent 
</p>
<p>load can be reduced by the crewmember adjusting the thermal control valve (TCV) setting on 
</p>
<p>the EMU to prevent the onset of significant sweating. To reduce the risk of high latent load 
</p>
<p>leading to water in the helmet, a potential warning system was developed using the Systems 
</p>
<p>Improved Numerical Differencing Analyzer EMU model (SINDA EMU). The goal of this 
</p>
<p>warning system is to alert the crewmember when they are producing a high latent load and 
</p>
<p>recommend adjustment of the TCV to increase cooling. This warning system was designed to 
</p>
<p>ensure high risk conditions are avoided while simultaneously preventing a system that warns 
</p>
<p>the crewmember too frequently. Theoretical tests of the warning system through modeling 
</p>
<p>calculated that if the warning system were used during US EVA 80, the total latent load could 
</p>
<p>have been reduced by up to 33% which would have significantly reduced the risk of water in 
</p>
<p>the helmet. This analysis also investigated the effectiveness of the warning system on EVAs 
</p>
<p>that occurred after US EVA 80. 
</p>

<p>I. Introduction &amp; Background 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>URING the repressurization phase at the end of United States Extravehicular Activity 80 (US EVA 80) on 23 
</p>
<p>March 2022, liquid water entered the helmet of the Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU) of Extravehicular Crew 
</p>
<p>2 (EV2). The volume of liquid water was estimated to be between 30-50 cm3. This volume of water covered the front 
</p>
<p>of the helmet visor, potentially disrupting the crewmember’s ability to see clearly. Had this volume of water entered 
</p>
<p>the helmet while outside of the crewlock, it would cause significant risk to the mission and crewmember. For this 
</p>
<p>reason, an investigation into the cause of water in the helmet during US EVA 80 was conducted. This investigation 
</p>
<p>also investigated potential risk mitigation methods that could be implemented.  
</p>
<p>The investigation determined that the most likely cause of water in the helmet during US EVA 80 was integrated 
</p>
<p>performance induced sublimator carryover caused by a comparatively high latent load. Figure I-1 shows a simplified 
</p>
<p>schematic of the ventilation loop to help explain sublimator carryover. During an EVA, the crewmember adds water 
</p>
<p>vapor to the ventilation loop through respiration, diffusion through the skin, and sweat evaporation. The latent heat 
</p>
<p>added to the ventilation loop through the evaporation of water is also called the latent load. The  humidity added to 
</p>
<p>the gas by the crewmember leaves the suit and enters the Portable Life Support System (PLSS). In the PLSS, the gas 
</p>
<p>passes through a sublimator which cools the gas, causing liquid water to condense out. The sublimator contains small 
</p>
<p>holes called slurper holes, which were designed to remove the liquid water from the sublimator. However, if the slurper 
</p>
<p>holes aren’t able to remove all of the liquid water, some water may move downstream of the sublimator towards the 
</p>
<p>helmet. This liquid water is called sublimator carryover.  
</p>
<p>The amount of sublimator carryover depends on the latent load and the efficiency of the sublimator’s ability to 
</p>
<p>remove liquid water. Through a comparative assessment of historical EVAs, it was determined that US EVA 80 had 
</p>
<p>a high latent load compared to previous EVAs which did not present water in the helmet1. The comparative assessment 
</p>
<p>also found that other historical EVAs with high latent loads often presented some form of sublimator carryover. For 
</p>
<p>this reason, it was determined that the water in the helmet during US EVA 80 was most likely due to sublimator 
</p>
<p>carryover caused by an elevated latent load. Further evidence from the Test, Teardown, &amp; Evaluation (TT&amp;E) of the 
</p>
<p>EMU confirmed that there were no individual components performing below specification, which implies that the 
</p>
<p>water was caused by the integrated performance of the EMU rather than an individual component failure. Latent load 
</p>
<p>is an important factor when it comes to the risk of water in the helmet since a high latent load results in an increased 
</p>
<p>likelihood of sublimator carryover that may eventually enter the helmet. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>

</p>
<p>The simplest way to reduce the latent load experienced during an EVA is to reduce the sweat produced by the 
</p>
<p>crewmember. When the temperature of the body increases, it produces sweat to remove the excess heat that is stored 
</p>
<p>by the crewmember. Increased sweating results in increased evaporation into the gas and a higher latent load. Figure 
</p>
<p>I-2 shows the results of a parametric analytical study on the impact of crewmember heat storage (measurement of 
</p>
<p>deviations in body temperature from thermal neutrality) on total latent load generated during a 7-hour EVA1. At 
</p>
<p>negative and neutral heat storage levels, the latent load does not depend significantly on heat storage. This is because 
</p>
<p>at negative heat storage levels, the body is not producing significant amounts of sweat. Once the crewmember heats 
</p>
<p>up into the positive heat storage range, the latent load begins to increase rapidly due to the initiation of sweating. 
</p>
<p>The thermal comfort of the crewmember is maintained by the transport loop of the PLSS which flows cooling 
</p>
<p>water through the Liquid Cooling and Ventilation Garment (LCVG) of the crewmember. The crewmember adjusts 
</p>

<p>the temperature and flowrate of this liquid water by adjusting the Thermal Control Valve (TCV), a manual valve 
</p>
<p>located on the Display and Control Module (DCM) of the suit. The TCV settings are H,0,1,2,…,9,10,C with lower 
</p>
<p>settings indicating less cooling being provided to the crewmember. The status of the TCV setting is not monitored and 
</p>
<p>is only available when the crewmember calls down adjustments during an EVA or as they recall settings after the 
</p>
<p>EVA is completed. The EMU contains enough cooling capacity to maintain a crewmember at a thermally neutral 
</p>
<p>condition, however maintaining neutral heat storage requires manual adjustments by the EVA crewmember, which 
</p>
<p>requires predictive action and focus on thermal comfort while performing complex activities. It may also be difficult 
</p>
<p>to adjust the TCV when performing particular tasks. Since a crewmember will never be able to perfectly maintain a 
</p>
<p>perfect thermal neutrality, a warning system that would alert the crewmember when they reach elevated heat storage 
</p>
<p>(and subsequently latent load) could be a critical tool in reducing the risk of sublimator carryover. 
</p>

<p> The goal of this analysis was to use the lessons learned from the comparative assessment of EVAs to develop an 
</p>
<p>operational tool that could be used to assess the latent load of a crewmember during an EVA. This tool could then 
</p>

</p>
<p>water in the helmet. This analysis will be primarily based on determining appropriate limits to quantitatively assess 
</p>
<p>the risk of water in the helmet and determine if mitigations taken by the crewmember in response to a high latent load 
</p>
<p>are effective. This analysis will also assess the performance of the warning system on EVAs conducted since US EVA 
</p>
<p>80.

<p>II. Model Description 
</p>
<p>The Systems Improved Numerical Differencing Analyzer 
</p>
<p>EMU (SINDA EMU) model was used to calculate the latent 
</p>
<p>load for numerous EVAs. SINDA EMU models the 
</p>
<p>crewmember and PLSS, connecting the two subsystems 
</p>
<p>through flow of gas and cooling water in and out of the suit. 
</p>
<p>SINDA EMU models all flow paths through the PLSS, which 
</p>
<p>allows it to accurately calculate the temperature of the water, 
</p>
<p>temperature of the gas, and dew point of the gas. The PLSS 
</p>
<p>model accurately captures the impact of the crewmember 
</p>
<p>adjusting the TCV by modeling the various flow paths through 
</p>
<p>the PLSS. The crewmember side of the model accounts for the 
</p>
<p>metabolism of the crew, sweat generation, and heat transfer to 
</p>
<p>the LCVG and through the suit walls. This model allows for 
</p>
<p>the conditions of US EVA 80 (and any other EVA) to be 
</p>
<p>modeled. Additionally, it allows for theoretical changes to 
</p>
<p>EVAs, such as adjusting the TCV, to be modeled. SINDA EMU was initially developed in the 1970s2,3, and has since 
</p>
<p>been improved and re-correlated through improvement of the crewmember modeling4, re-correlation to EMU 
</p>

<p>A. Model Inputs and Assumptions 
</p>
<p>SINDA EMU uses inputs from flight data, console logs, and thermal modeling to accurately model an EVA. The 
</p>
<p>flight data is used to determine the metabolic rate, suit and environment pressure, and temperature of water exiting 
</p>
<p>the sublimator. The metabolic rate (metrate) is determined by calculating the rate of decrease in the pressure of the 
</p>
<p>oxygen supply tank and then calculating the metrate based on the rate of oxygen consumption4. The console logs are 
</p>
<p>used to determine the TCV setting for the duration of the EVA. TCV setting affects the flowrate of water to the 
</p>
<p>sublimator which impacts the amount of cooling received by the crewmember. The flow split caused by TCV setting 
</p>
<p>varies by specific TCV, so the fleetwide average flowrate was used. Thermal modeling is used to determine the 
</p>
<p>environmental sink temperature which impacts the heat leak through the EMU. Environment thermal modeling 
</p>
<p>identifies the sink temperature for eight different orbit positions, with the model choosing the orbit position that best 
</p>
<p>aligns with the actual timeline of the EVA. Finally, the model assumes the skin area and sweat rates based on average 
</p>
<p>body size and sweat generation rates4. The mass of the crewmember is input into the model, and the average skin area 
</p>
<p>for a crewmember of that mass is used. 
</p>
<p>III. Proposed Warning System 
</p>
<p>For the causal investigation into the US EVA 80 water in the helmet event, SINDA EMU was used to calculate 
</p>
<p>the latent load for numerous historical EVAs and tests in Chamber B at Johnson Space Center (JSC)1. This analysis 
</p>
<p>indicated that EVAs with high latent loads are more likely to present some amount of sublimator carryover (either 
</p>
<p>water in the helmet or a CO2 sensor latching, which indicates water downstream of the sublimator). Examining the 
</p>
<p>latent load for all modeled EVAs showed a clear split between EVAs with and without sublimator carryover. Every 
</p>
<p>EVA that had a calculated total latent load over 1800 Btu (1899 kJ) had some amount of detectable water downstream 
</p>
<p>of the helmet, while most EVAs below this limit did not. Using these results, a two-part warning system was developed 
</p>
<p>to flag EVAs that are at a high risk of presenting significant water in the helmet. The warning would be triggered if 
</p>
<p>either of the following criteria are met: 
</p>
<p>1. Latent load of greater than 300 Btu/hr (87.9 W) maintained for over one hour. 
</p>
<p>2. Total latent load of 1800 Btu (1899 kJ) sustained during an EVA. Total latent load is calculated by 
</p>
<p>integrating the latent load with respect to time. For all investigated EVAs, the first criteria was also met 
</p>
<p>in all EVAs where the second criteria was met. 
</p>
<p>This warning system would be used to alert the crewmember during an EVA if they were producing a latent load 
</p>
<p>that significantly increased the risk of water in the helmet. The warning system would not mandate the crewmember 
</p>
<p>adjusts the TCV, but rather allow operations personnel to make suggestions to the crewmember to consider their 
</p>
<p>thermal comfort and potentially adjust their TCV. The goal of this warning system is to flag EVAs where there is a 
</p>
<p>significant risk of water in the helmet, while also avoiding false warnings. If the warning system calls out warnings 
</p>
<p>too frequently, it may lead to reduced trust in the warning system by crewmembers and flight directors. Additionally, 
</p>
<p>adjusting the TCV requires a manual adjustment, so issuing too many warnings could result in a reduction in 
</p>
<p>productivity during an EVA as crewmembers stop their tasks to adjust their TCV setting.  
</p>
<p>This warning system was tested on the same set of 45 historical EVAs that were used to develop the warning 
</p>
<p>system. Testing the warning system using the same data that was used to develop the warning system may lead to 
</p>
<p>overestimation of the effectiveness, which will be addressed in Section V. These EVAs were modeled using SINDA 
</p>
<p>EMU to calculate the latent load through the duration of the EVA. These consisted of EVAs conducted using the 
</p>
<p>sublimators that were on ISS during the time of US EVA 80 as well as any EVAs that presented some form of water 
</p>
<p>in the helmet.  </p>

<p>On Space Transportation System 117 (STS-117) EVA 1, EV 2 had a latched CO2 sensor which indicated potential 
</p>
<p>water downstream of the sublimator. STS-118 EVA 2 (EV1), US EVA 35 (EV1), and US EVA 80 (EV2) all had water 
</p>
<p>droplets in the helmet of the EMU. The remaining 41 EVAs did not have any water in the helmets or CO2 sensor 
</p>
<p>latching. The latent load limit of 300 Btu/hr (87.9 W) was exceeded for more than one hour on US EVA 35, US EVA 
</p>
<p>80, and STS-117 EVA 1. These cases are identified as proper warnings. This means that if the warning system had 
</p>
<p>been implemented, a warning would have been called. This warning would have been justified since actual events 
</p>
<p>showed significant water was downstream of the sublimator. There were no false warnings, indicating that there were 
</p>
<p>no EVAs that would have experienced a warning but did not require a warning. STS-118 EVA 2 is categorized as a 
</p>
<p>missed warning, since there would have been no warning called out, but water was present in the helmet. This is 
</p>
<p>because STS-118 EVA 2 was an outlier when compared to other historical EVAs. STS-118 EVA 2 presented water 
</p>
<p>in the helmet despite its comparable latent loads to historical EVAs that did not present any water in the helmet. This 
</p>
<p>EVA may have presented water in the helmet due to sublimator degradation. The sublimator used during STS-118 
</p>
<p>EVA 2 was an older sublimator which may have resulted in significant carryover, even at low latent loads. The 
</p>
<p>assumed thermal management of the crewmember during this EVA may also have not been properly determined (TCV 
</p>
<p>and heat storage data is sparser on older EVAs) which could also explain this EVA as an outlier due to less accurate 
</p>
<p>model inputs. Finally, there were 41 EVAs that are classified as proper no warning, since no warning would have been 
</p>
<p>issued and no warning was required. 
</p>
<p>This warning system’s ability to flag EVAs that are more likely to present water in the helmet was shown to 
</p>
<p>effectively identify EVAs that will likely have significant sublimator carryover. This warning system was also shown 
</p>
<p>to be effective at preventing warnings from occurring too frequently, particularly when warnings are not required. 
</p>
<p>While this investigation shows that SINDA EMU is capable of identifying EVAs that are likely to present water in 
</p>
<p>the helmet, additional work is needed to ensure that issuing a warning can provide a significant reduction in risk. 
</p>

<p>C. US EVA 35 
</p>
<p>The TCV parametric investigation was also conducted for US EVA 35, which was another EVA that had 
</p>
<p>significant water in the helmet, likely due to sublimator carryover. Figure IV-2 shows the calculated latent load and 
</p>
<p>TCV setting for US EVA 35. The warning would occur around 1.6 hours, after the latent load exceeded the latent load 
</p>
<p>limit for more than an hour. The blue line indicates the TCV settings and resultant latent load based on the actual TCV 
</p>
<p>settings during US EVA 35. The remaining colors indicate parametric TCV adjustments up to a final setting of 6 
</p>
<p>which is sufficient to maintain adequate cooling to the crewmember. For all parametric TCV runs, there is a period of 
</p>
<p>time where the latent load remains constant shortly after the warning. This is due to the evaporation of sweat runoff. 
</p>
<p>During the first 1.5 hours of the EVA, the crewmember is at such a high latent load that significant amounts of the 
</p>
<p>sweat production cannot be evaporated. This leftover sweat is absorbed by the LCVG and called sweat runoff. Sweat 
</p>
<p>       
</p>
<p>           
     
</p>
<p>               
</p>
<p>           
     
</p>
<p>  
  
 
  
  
  
  
 
  
  
  </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>7 
</p>
<p>International Conference on Environmental Systems 
 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>runoff eventually evaporates into the gas once the latent load drops enough to allow more sweat to evaporate than is 
</p>
<p>being produced. This model accounts for the evaporation of sweat runoff by assuming the sweat runoff evaporation 
</p>
<p>will maintain a constant latent load. Once all of the sweat runoff is evaporated, the latent load drops off (e.g. green 
</p>
<p>line shortly after 3.5 hours). The evaporation of sweat runoff did not occur during US EVA 80 because the latent load 
</p>
<p>was not high enough to cause significant sweat runoff. By contrast, US EVA 35 had an extremely high latent load 
</p>
<p>which resulted in significant levels of sweat runoff. This shows that if the latent load is extremely high and the LCVG 
</p>
<p>gets soaked in sweat runoff, it will take longer to reduce the latent load due to the additional source of humidity on 
</p>
<p>the LCVG, even if the TCV is rapidly adjusted to increase cooling. 
</p>
<p> 
Figure IV-2: Latent Load (Top) and TCV Settings (Bottom) for US EVA 35 with Parametric Adjustments of 
</p>
<p>the TCV Setting 
</p>
<p>Table IV-2 shows the calculated total latent load for the TCV parametric investigation and the percent decrease in 
</p>
<p>latent load caused by adjusting the TCV after a warning is issued. When compared to US EVA 80, the reduction in 
</p>
<p>latent load caused by increasing the TCV after a warning is less significant for US EVA 35. This is due to the 
</p>
<p>evaporation of sweat runoff caused by the extremely high latent load at the start of US EVA 35. Increasing the TCV 
</p>
<p>setting more rapidly has a more significant impact on reducing latent load when there is significant sweat runoff.  
</p>

<p>For US EVA 80 and US EVA 35, adjusting the TCV setting to increase cooling after a warning is issued can 
</p>
<p>significantly reduce the latent load. For both EVAs, the TCV adjustments dropped the total latent load below the total 
</p>
<p>latent load limit of 1800 Btu (1899 kJ). This indicates that it is very likely that if a warning system were implemented 
</p>
<p>on these EVAs, water in the helmet would have been avoided. The TCV setting should be increased as rapidly as 
</p>
<p>possible to increase the reduction in latent load. This shows that a warning system which identifies when a 
</p>
<p>crewmember is at a high latent load and recommends TCV setting adjustments is an effective way to reduce latent 
</p>
<p>load and reduce the risk of sublimator carryover occurring during an EVA. 
</p>
<p>V. Post-EVA Analysis for US EVA 81-84 
</p>
<p>One of the biggest issues with the development of the warning system based on historical EVAs is that, thus far, 
</p>
<p>the warning system could only be tested on the same set of data that was used to develop it. This may result in 
</p>
<p>overconfidence in the capabilities of the warning system. Fortunately, EVAs that occur after US EVA 80 allow for 
</p>
<p>       
</p>
<p>           
     
</p>
<p>               
</p>
<p>                    
                    
</p>
<p>          
</p>
<p>                     
     
</p>
<p>  
  
 
  
  
  
  
 
  
  
  </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>8 
</p>
<p>International Conference on Environmental Systems 
 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>the warning system to be tested on data that was not used to develop the warning system. After the US EVA 80 
</p>
<p>investigation, the importance of TCV setting on controlling heat storage and latent load resulted in increased awareness 
</p>
<p>and reporting of the TCV setting. For this reason, all EVAs after US EVA 80 have much more accurate TCV setting 
</p>
<p>information than was available for prior EVAs. These TCV settings allow for SINDA EMU to be run with significantly 
</p>
<p>greater accuracy than previous EVAs. SINDA EMU was run for both crewmembers on US EVAs 81-84 and the 
</p>
<p>performance of the proposed warning system was tested. To account for uncertainty in mass of the crewmember (since 
</p>
<p>crewmember mass impacts latent load calculation), the model was run three times for each EVA (+/- 20 lbm [9.07 
</p>
<p>kg]).  
</p>
<p> 
Figure V-1: Latent Loads for US EVAs 81-84 
</p>
<p>The latent load calculations for US EVAs 81-84 are plotted in Figure V-1 and compared to the proposed warning 
</p>
<p>limit of 300 Btu/hr (87.9 W). This plot only shows one of the three parametric cases. This analysis showed that the 
</p>
<p>impact of varying mass only affects the latent load by about 5%. For this discussion, the additional +/- masses will be 
</p>
<p>neglected. Of the eight total model runs (2 crewmembers for each EVA), the latent load limit of 300 Btu/hr (87.9 W) 
</p>
<p>was exceeded for a significant amount of time on two of the EVAs (US EVA 81 EV 2 and US EVA 84 EV 1).  
</p>
<p>On US EVA 81, EV 2 exceeded the limit for approximately 1.5 hours at the start of the EVA. Since the limit was 
</p>
<p>exceeded for more than an hour, if the warning system were implemented, a warning would have been sent to the 
</p>
<p>crewmember. Ultimately, there was no water in the helmet of EV 2 on US EVA 81 which would classify this EVA as 
</p>
<p>a false warning. The reason this EVA likely did not produce water in the helmet is because the high latent loads were 
</p>
<p>not sustained for the duration of the EVA. Shortly after a warning would have been issued, the latent load for EV 2 
</p>
<p>dropped below the limit, which is a similar response to what would be expected if a warning had been implemented.  
</p>
<p>During US EVA 84, EV 1 experienced a latent load over the limit for slightly less than one hour. Based on criteria 
</p>
<p>for issuing a warning, this would not have been called up as a warning since the latent load was not maintained for 
</p>
<p>more than one hour. Towards the end of EVA 84, communication with EV2 was lost in one ear, likely due to water in 
</p>
<p>the ear cup of the Crew Communication Electronics Module (CCEM). This could have been due to either sublimator 
</p>
<p>carryover or sweat from the ear. While communication lost occurred near the end of the EVA, it is possible that the 
</p>
<p>high latent load at the start of the EVA contributed to the presence of water. Losing communications during an EVA 
</p>
<p>is a common occurrence and for this reason, this will not be considered a water anomaly and this EVA was classified 
</p>
<p>as an accurate no-warning.  
</p>
<p>The other six EVAs all remained below the limit for the duration of the EVA and did not report any water in the 
</p>
<p>helmet so were flagged as accurate no-warnings. 
</p>
<p>D. Additional Findings from Post-EVA Modeling 
</p>
<p>In addition to testing the warning system, the results from modeling these EVAs with SINDA EMU has led to a 
</p>
<p>few additional findings. First, the highest latent load generally occurs at the beginning of the EVA. This could be due 
</p>
<p>to the higher sink temperature in the crewlock, high metrates, or difficulties in predicting necessary cooling at the 
</p>
<p>beginning of an EVA. When the crew begins an EVA, they are in a warm crewlock which reduces the heat leak from 
</p>
<p>the suit and may increase the heat storage, causing sweating near the start of an EVA. Additionally, the high latent 
</p>
<p>load could be caused by higher metrate at the start of the EVA. Finally, at the start of the EVA it may be difficult to </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>9 
</p>
<p>International Conference on Environmental Systems 
 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>anticipate the workload which may result in incorrect TCV settings at the start of the EVA resulting in warmer 
</p>
<p>crewmembers.  
</p>
<p>Another trend is that the highest latent load cases were on EVAs where the crewmember was performing the first 
</p>
<p>EVA of their career. Both US EVA 81 EV2 and US EVA 84 EV1 were the first EVAs for the respective crewmembers. 
</p>
<p>The following EVAs for the crewmember on US EVA 81 had lower latent loads which may indicate that the crew is 
</p>
<p>learning from experience and improving their ability at maintaining thermal neutrality. At the time of writing, the 
</p>
<p>crewmember from US EVA 84 has not conducted another EVA to see if they are also able to reduce the latent load, 
</p>
<p>but this will be investigated going forward. 
</p>
<p>VI. Discussion and Future Work 
</p>
<p>Testing the warning system on EVAs 81-84 allowed for the warning system to be assessed. Overall, had the 
</p>
<p>warning system been implemented, there would have been seven EVAs where no warning was issued or necessary, 
</p>
<p>and one EVA where a warning was issued but not necessary. US EVA 81, EV 2 most likely did not present any form 
</p>
<p>of water in the helmet because the crewmember was able to reduce the latent load relatively quickly after a warning 
</p>
<p>would have been issued. This EVA shows the impact of reducing the latent load and preventing sustained high latent 
</p>
<p>loads. Had they sustained the maximum latent load for the duration of the EVA, it is likely that significant sublimator 
</p>
<p>carryover would have built up enough to enter the helmet. 
</p>
<p>One significant limitation of testing the warning system on additional EVAs is the lack of positive (i.e. water in 
</p>
<p>the helmet) events that will occur after US EVA 80. Due to the US EVA 80 investigation, corrective actions were put 
</p>
<p>in place to prevent crewmembers from sustaining a high latent load for a significant length of time. For this reason, 
</p>
<p>the likelihood of a future EVA having significant water in the helmet is reduced. It will be difficult to test the warning 
</p>
<p>system’s ability to identify high risk EVAs since high risk EVAs will theoretically be less likely. For this reason it is 
</p>
<p>necessary to rely on the historical dataset or expand knowledge on the integrated performance of the EMU to further 
</p>
<p>quantify the divide between high risk and low risk latent loads. 
</p>
<p> The warning system outlined an effective method of screening for high risk EVAs, however there are still 
</p>
<p>numerous steps that need to be taken to ensure that implementing this warning system is feasible and effective.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>1. Capabilities for live modeling of SINDA EMU are recommended. Currently, SINDA EMU cannot be run in 
</p>
<p>real time during an EVA. Properly implementing this warning system would rely on a version of the model 
</p>
<p>which is able to receive live data and calculate the latent load in real time. The model is currently fast enough 
</p>
<p>to run live, but the workflow is not simple enough to be reliably used. In the future, this model would most 
</p>
<p>likely be run on the ground by an analyst and potential warnings would be reported to flight directors.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>2. Further understanding of the EMU system is required to quantify the amount of sublimator carryover that 
</p>
<p>will occur for a given latent loading of the sublimator. Through SINDA EMU modeling, it was shown that 
</p>
<p>high latent loads are associated with more sublimator carryover. However, there is still a gap in understanding 
</p>
<p>of how much carryover is expected for a given latent load and how much carryover is required to result in 
</p>
<p>water reaching the helmet. With separately planned efforts that include testing and additional analysis and 
</p>
<p>modeling, knowledge is expected to be gained concerning this gap. Once this is better understood, defining 
</p>
<p>the high risk band of latent loads will become easier, improving the accuracy of the warning system. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>3. Corrective actions and procedures in response to a warning are also recommended. This analysis has shown 
</p>
<p>that adjusting the TCV to increase cooling after receiving a warning has the potential to drastically reduce 
</p>
<p>the latent load in high risk EVAs. However, more work is necessary to determine how much the TCV should 
</p>
<p>be should be adjusted and if this adjustment may cause other unintended consequences. 
</p>
<p>VII. Conclusions 
</p>
<p>After the US EVA 80 water in the helmet failure, an investigation determined that the most likely cause was 
</p>
<p>sublimator carryover caused by a comparatively high latent load. One of the most effective ways of reducing the risk 
</p>
<p>of water in the helmet from this failure is to limit the latent load that is produced by the crewmember and sent to the 
</p>
<p>sublimator. High latent load occurs when the crewmember is warmer than usual (high heat storage). This analysis 
</p>
<p>outlined a potential warning system that could be used to alert the crewmember when they are experiencing a high 
</p>
<p>latent load and at increased risk of water in the helmet. This would allow for the crewmember to adjust their TCV 
</p>
<p>setting to increase cooling and reduce latent load. This warning system was based on thermal/fluid modeling of </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>10 
</p>
<p>International Conference on Environmental Systems 
 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>numerous historical EVAs that included both EVAs with and without significant carryover. Using this data, a latent 
</p>
<p>load limit was established. If this rate was exceeded for more than an hour, a warning would be issued and the 
</p>
<p>crewmember would be alerted of the elevated latent load. 
</p>
<p>This warning system was tested on the historical EVAs prior to US EVA 80. This showed that if the warning 
</p>
<p>system had been implemented, a warning would have been issued on three of the four EVAs that presented significant 
</p>
<p>sublimator carryover. There would have been no false warnings issued on the 41 EVAs that did not present significant 
</p>
<p>sublimator carryover. The analysis also showed that, had the warning system been implemented and resulted in a TCV 
</p>
<p>adjustment on US EVA 80 and US EVA 35 (two EVAs with significant amounts of water in helmet), the total latent 
</p>
<p>load would have reduced by up to 33.1% and 26.6%, respectively. The warning system was also tested on US EVAs 
</p>
<p>81-84, which occurred after US EVA 80. None of these EVAs presented water in the helmet, and the warning system 
</p>
<p>would have issued one warning on US EVA 81, EV2. US EVA 81 most likely did not present water in the helmet 
</p>
<p>because the latent load quickly lowered below the limit after a warning would have been issued. This warning system 
</p>
<p>and operational controls will be further improved through future investigation into sublimator performance and 
</p>
<p>modeling of future EVAs.  
</p>
<p>Ultimately, the warning system proposed in this analysis provides an effective way to monitor the latent load 
</p>
<p>during EVAs and provides details on how to reduce the latent load using the TCV setting in high risk conditions. This 
</p>
<p>system would provide an effective way to operationally reduce the risk of water entering the helmet again on future 
</p>
<p>EVAs.
