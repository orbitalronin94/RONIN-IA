Self-Weight Deflection

Self-weight deflection consists of two components: mount and face-sheet deflection.  

Mount Deflection or (or Gravity Sag) is low-order bending of the mirror substrate as it 

is accelerated by gravity against its opto-mechanical support mount.

Face-sheet Deflection (or Quilting) occurs when mirrors are light-weighted – leaving 

parts of the face-sheet without support.  These parts deflect slightly due to the 

acceleration of gravity against the vertical core ribs – depending upon their 

unsupported span size – and deflect more due to fabrication tool forces.

For this discussion, we will ignore face-sheet deflection because it can be mitigated:

• Design the mirror with appropriately face-sheet and core pocket dimensions

• Pressurize mirror core cells to balance gravity during fabrication

• Correct via small mechanical/ion polishing process.

G-Release Error

G-release error is  defined as the knowledge uncertainty of a mirror’s gravity-sag or the 

residual error between the mirror’s predicted and actual on-orbit shape.

• G-release occurs because space mirrors are manufactured in a 1-G environment. 

• In 1-G, mirrors have self-weight deflection.  

• But in 0-G, there is no gravity and thus no self-weight deflection.  

• The change in the mirror’s shape from 1-G to 0-G is called G-release. 

• G-release is most important for the primary because it is the largest, lowest stiffness 

mirror in the telescope.

G-Release Error

To achieve UV/Optical diffraction limited performance, the telescope’s transmitted 

wavefront error needs to be better than 30 nm rms and the telescope’s primary mirror 

surface figure error needs to be better than 10 nm rms.

To achieve this specification, G-release needs to be less than 4 nm rms.

Gravity Sag State of Practice

Gravity-sag has been studied extensively since the 1960s.

There are four possible mitigation approaches:

 Make the mirror as stiff as possible

 Support the mirror during fabrication in a 0-G configuration

 Analytically remove G-sag from the mirror during the fabrication process

 Actively correct the mirror in space

Make the Mirror as Stiff as Possible

In practice, it is not possible to make a mirror sufficiently stiff that it will have a gravity 

sag of less than 4 nm rms.

Therefore, the only practical options are:

 Support the mirror during fabrication in a 0-G configuration

 Analytically remove G-sag from the mirror during the fabrication process

 Actively correct the mirror in space

However, it is still advisable to make the mirror as stiff as possible, because it makes the 

remaining three mitigation approaches easier to implement.

Gravity Off-Loading

Gravity off-loading is typically done via:

• Multipoint Mount

• Air Bag Support

For mid-quality mirrors, air bags are adequate.  

But for high-precision mirrors, multi-point mounts are preferred.

The reason is that discrete mount points are more deterministic than a bag surface.

• Air bag exerts a force that is perpendicular to the mirror’s back – which is not 

necessarily anti-parallel to gravity; and 

• Stiction between bag and mirror can introduce shear forces.

Thus, air bags can introduce rotationally symmetric errors, i.e. power or spherical.

Absolute Metrology

Because interferometry is a relative measurement, the ability to manufacture precision 

surfaces is limited by knowledge of the reference wavefront. 

Absolute tests were needed to ‘calibrated’ the reference wavefront – allowing its error to 

be removed from the surface measurement. 

Absolute calibration tests falls into four basic categories: Rotation Tests, Shear Test, 

Rotation-Shear Test, and Composite Averages. 

An excellent summary of absolute calibration techniques is given by

• Schultz, G., and J. Schwider, “Interferometric Testing of Smooth Surfaces”, Progress 

in Optics XIII, pp.119-26, Wolf, North-Holland Publishing Co., 1976

• Bruning, J. H., “Fringe Scanning Interferometers”, Optical Shop Testing, Malacara, 

Wiley and Son’s, 1978.

Rotation Test Removes Gravity Sag

Because horizontal gravity sag is asymmetric, rotation tests can absolutely quantify 

gravity sag – thus allowing its removal from a surface measurement.

Rotating a mirror in angles around its surface normal that are symmetric with its flight 

mount, only rotates the mirror’s asymmetric component. The gravity sag does not 

rotate, nor does its rotationally symmetric component. 

Averaging these N-rotation measurements results in a measurement consisting of only 

the mirror’s symmetric surface shape and its gravity-sag. 

Back rotating each data set to theta = 0 before averaging, removes the gravity sag term, 

leaving only the mirror’s symmetric and asymmetric terms.

Three Sphere Test

A similar technique for spherical surfaces, first suggested by 

Jensen (1973) was demonstrated by Bruning (1974). 

As with Shultz and Schwider’s three surface test, the Jensen 

test is blind to harmonics of its rotation angle.  

A 180-degree rotation test only removes errors such as coma.  

To remove astigmatism requires four rotations of 90-degrees. 

To trefoil requires six 60-degree rotations. 

An important feature is the cat’s eye retro-reflection. This 

measurement allows the method to eliminate test setup 

symmetric and asymmetric errors from the part under test.  

Without this measurement, a rotation test can only calibrate 

the rotationally asymmetric errors.

N-Rotation Test

Building on work by Bruning and Parks (1978)

In 1996, Evans and Kestner published the ‘N-rotation’ algorithm where the 

interferometer measures a surface at N-rotation angles, i.e. N=3 (0°, 120°, 240°) or 

N=6 (0°, 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°). 

Averaging removes all test errors with angular dependence up to (N-1) Theta; and, all 

higher angular order errors that are not harmonics of N.

So, an N=3 rotation test will remove 2-Theta errors (i.e. coma, secondary coma, tertiary 

coma, etc.; and, astigmatism, secondary astigmatism, tertiary astigmatism, etc.).

An N=6 rotation test is required to remove error terms up to 5-Theta (i.e. trefoil, 

tetrafoil, and pentafoil).

HDOS Algorithm

In the mid-1990s, Hughes Danbury Optical Systems (HDOS) developed a proprietary 

three flat test to absolutely characterize the LIGO Pathfinder optic. \

A fundamental constraint of rotation tests is that their algorithms require the input data 

in polar coordinates; while interferometers use cartesian coordinates (because their 

detectors are square arrays). Thus, data interpolation is required. 

The HDOS approach sought to overcome this limitation by implementing a method 

similar to Primak (1988) and process the data in Fourier space using circular 

harmonic transformation.

Finally, as with Evans and Kestner, HDOS performed N rotations.  But, to overcome the 

(N-1) harmonic problem, extra rotations are added which are prime to each other.

Rotation Tests are proven.

Correction

While it is desirable to manufacture the primary mirror with as small of a G-release 

uncertainty as possible (TRL-9 capability is < 10 nm rms), an active primary mirror 

can easily correct 50 to 200 nm rms of error. 

It is interesting to note that, while they were never used, the Hubble PM (Primary 

Mirror) had 24 actuators to mitigate the risk of astigmatic G-release error.

And, JWST PM has 18 actuators to mitigate the risk of segment radius matching error.  

21

Analysis of an alternative ULE® HabEx primary mirror 

indicates that 15 actuators can reduce low-order (i.e. 

astigmatic) G-release error by 20X, 25 actuators can 

reduce G-release error by 40X, and 50 actuators can 

reduce G-release error by 100X

Conclusions

G-release is a critical error budget element for space telescopes.

For a UV/Optical telescope, G-release needs to be less than 4 nm rms.

Because it is not possible to design a large mirror with a sufficient stiffness to insure a

small enough G-release for a UVO diffraction limited telescope, it is necessary to

fabricate a 0-G mirror, i.e. a mirror that will have its required shape in space.

This can be done by either supporting the mirror in a 0-G configuration or calculating

and compensating for its G-sag during fabrication.

If necessary for further mitigation, one can design the mirror to be correctable to

achieve its required shape in space.

Absolute Metrology techniques developed for high-accuracy optical surface testing

should be able to characterize gravity-sag of space optics and remove it during

fabrication to the required level of precision.
