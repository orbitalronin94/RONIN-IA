<p>The International Space Station (ISS) is presently equipped with an elaborate, heavy, and high-
</p>
<p>power consuming system that recovers approximately 50% of O2 from metabolic CO2 as part of the 
</p>
<p>atmospheric revitalization (AR) at the ISS habitat. Future long duration missions will require a more 
</p>
<p>sustainable and efficient system capable of yielding a minimum of 75% O2 recovery to reach the self-
</p>
<p>sufficiency required for long-duration space missions beyond Earth’s low orbit. A Macrofluidic 
</p>
<p>Electrochemical Reactor (MFECR) technology development effort is currently underway at NASA 
</p>
<p>Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC) to not only increase significantly current O2 recovery 
</p>
<p>efficiency, improving self-sufficiency on AR at the ISS habitat and future long-duration missions, 
</p>
<p>but also reduce system complexity. The authors have developed and deployed a comprehensive 3D 
</p>
<p>multiphysics model that thoroughly replicates the actual configuration and fluid/material domains 
</p>
<p>of the MFECR. The coupled physics in this multiphysics model include multicomponent-multiphase 
</p>
<p>electrochemical-driven reactions, non-ideal mass transport mechanism, free and porous flow, heat 
</p>
<p>transfer, CO2 solubility on alkaline electrolyte, water condensation on porous medium, and DC 
</p>
<p>electrical current generation along with Joule heating effect. This model is aimed to conduct qualitive 
</p>
<p>benchmark on three different MFECR layouts, one without serpentine paths (plain) and two with 
</p>
<p>serpentines leading to four and twelve paths respectively. Once experimental data is generated via a 
</p>
<p>test matrix of 200 tests, the model will be validated to conduct MFECR’s process optimization and 
</p>
<p>revalidate the qualitive benchmark on three different MFECR’s layouts.       
</p>

<p>I.  Introduction 
</p>
<p>emoval of metabolic carbon dioxide (CO2) is crucial in Atmospheric Revitalization (AR) at the ISS and any other 
</p>
<p>livable space habitat to maintain a safe, breathable atmosphere inside the habitat. The critical objective for the 
</p>
<p>AR system is to 'close the loop', that is, to capture gaseous human metabolic products, specifically water (H2O) vapor 
</p>
<p>and CO2, for maximum O2 recovery and to make other useful resources from these products. The AR subsystem also 
</p>
<p>removes trace chemical contaminants from the habitat atmosphere to preserve habitat atmospheric quality, provide 
</p>
<p>O2, and utilize instrumentation to monitor cabin atmospheric quality. Long-duration crewed space exploration 
</p>
<p>missions will require critical advancements in AR process technologies to increase efficiency on O2 recovery, reduce 
</p>
<p>power and mass consumption, and increase reliability compared to prior AR processes used for shorter duration 
</p>
<p>missions typically limited to Low Earth Orbit (LEO). The AR in the International Space Station (ISS) includes four 
</p>
<p>integrated units, which provide critical functions for life support including Trace Contaminant Control (TCC) system, 
</p>
<p>CO2 carbon removal branded as Carbon Dioxide Removal Assembly (CDRA), O2 generation branded as Oxygen 
</p>
<p>Generation Assembly (OGA), and oxygen recovery branded as Carbon Dioxide Reduction System (CDRS).     
</p>
<p> CDRS is a Sabatier reactor that operates at 300–400 °C and reduces CO2 to methane (CH4) and H2O using H2 
</p>
<p>from OGA (an electrolysis unit that generates O2 as main product and H2 as byproduct) as reactant. The CDRS limits 
</p>
<p>the feasibility of having a closed loop system, as it requires more water than the water metabolically generated 
</p>
<p>ultimately resulting in a recovery rate of approximately 50%. NASA Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC) and the 
</p>
<p>University of Texas at Arlington (UTA) are currently developing an Engineering Development Unit (EDU) of a 
</p>
<p>Macrofluidic Electro Chemical Reactor (MFECR) to convert a continuous stream of CO2 and water into O2 and C2H4 
</p>
<p>at standard conditions. The novel design combines CO2 conversion and water electrolysis (currently conducted in 
</p>
<p>OGA and CDRS units respectively) into one compact unit that runs at standard conditions and is theoretically capable 
</p>
<p>of generating O2 with a theoretical maximum metabolic CO2 conversion of 73% while consuming less metabolic 
</p>
<p>water. The metabolic CO2 conversion process that currently operates at the ISS yields CH4 as waste byproduct losing 
</p>
<p>4 moles of H (2 moles of H2 originally generated by 2 moles of water) per 1 mole of C; the novel design yields C2H4 
</p>
<p>as byproduct losing half of the H/H2 moles than the current CH4-byproduct-producer approach. 
</p>

</p>
<p>actual configuration and fluid/material domains of the MFECR. In the model, the EC physics (multicomponent-
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>process including fluid and non-ideal mass transfer of reactant and product species in free and porous media,  CO2 
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>DC electrical current with Joule heating generation.

<p> The eleven MFECR’s material domains include two non-electrical-conducting endplates to press the EDU on the 
</p>
<p>cathode and anode ends respectively, two electrical-conducting serpentine channels to house the CO2 (cathode) and 
</p>
<p>O2 (anode) gas flow streams, one non-electrical-conducting electrolyte serpentine channel to house the electrolyte 
</p>
<p>liquid flow stream, and two GDEs (cathode and anode), each one housing three material subdomains that include the 
</p>
<p>gas diffusion layer (GDL), the MPL, and the CL. MPLs are typically built up by a mixture of carbon black powder 
</p>
<p>and a hydrophobic agent, typically poly‐tetra‐fluoro‐ethylene (PTFE). GDL works as a protective layer for the delicate 
</p>
<p>catalyst structure, provides good mechanical strength and easy gas access to the catalyst, and improves the electrical 
</p>
<p>conductivity. MPL reduces the contact resistance between catalyst layer and microporous substrate. 
</p>
<p>Besides the eleven spatial material domains described above, the model also includes three fluid domains flowing 
</p>
<p>along the cathode, electrolyte, and anode serpentine chambers respectively. These three flow domains were created 
</p>
<p>by generating a solid material domain of the entire MFECR and subtracting all eleven spatial material domains (two 
</p>
<p>endplates, three serpentine channels, two GDLs, two MPLs, and two CLs). Each cathode and anode flow domain has 
</p>
<p>three subdomains to house the cathode and anode streams throughout four different flow media that correspond to one 
</p>
<p>free flow along the serpentine channel and three porous flows along the GDL, MPL, and CL.

<p>Model Definition 
</p>
<p>The EC reaction section of the MFECR consists of two porous CLs and an electrolyte serpentine channel 
</p>
<p>sandwiched in the middle as shown in Figure 5. The CO2 feeds the cathode serpentine channel and part of the O2 
</p>
<p>product is fed back to the anode serpentine chamber. The alkaline solution feeds the electrolyte serpentine chamber 
</p>
<p>wetting the CLs of both, the anode and cathode GDEs allowing the OH- ionic transport between them. The EC 
</p>
<p>reactions in the cell are reactions (1) and (2) in the cathode’s GDE and reaction (3) in the anode’s GDE yielding the 
</p>
<p>total reaction (4).  
</p>
<p>The model includes the following processes: 
</p>
<p>• Electronic charge balance (Ohm’s law) 
</p>
<p>• Ionic charge balance (Ohm’s law) 
</p>
<p>• Concentration-dependent Butler-Volmer and Tafel charge transfer kinetics 
</p>
<p>• Flow distribution in gas and liquid channels (Navier-Stokes) 
</p>
<p>• Flow in the porous GDL, MPL, and CL (Brinkman equations) 
</p>
<p>• Mass balances in gas phase in both gas channels and porous GDL, MPL, and CL (Maxwell-Stefan diffusion and 
</p>
<p>convection)  
</p>
<p>• Evaporation and condensation of water on the GDL and gas channels along with condensed water transportation 
</p>
<p>through the GDL‘s porous via capillary pressure. 
</p>
<p>• Temperature-pressure dependent CO2 absorption/dilution into KOH aqueous electrolyte solution via Henry 
</p>
<p>constant.   
</p>
<p>• Temperature (energy balance equation) via three types of heat transfer mechanisms,  
</p>
<p>  1) conductive within EDU’s components,  
</p>
<p>  2) convective within the channel flows,  
</p>
<p>  3) radiative between EDU surface and ambient 
</p>
<p>• Heat generation/source via Joule heating effect.  
</p>

<p>A. EC Reaction Phase 
</p>
<p>Species generated in EC reactions 2) and 3) are assumed to yield gaseous products, H2 in the cathode (reaction 2), 
</p>
<p>O2 as well as water vapor in the anode (reaction 3). Temperature-pressure dependent condensation of the water vapor 
</p>
<p>generated at the anode (reaction 3) is estimated as well as its transportation through the porous GDE and MPL domains 
</p>
<p>driven by the porous capillary pressure. In the CO2 reduction (CO2R) to C2H4 (reaction 1) the CO2 reactant is still 
</p>
<p>assumed to be in the gas phase but dissolved in the aqueous KOH electrolyte as recent experimental and theoretical 
</p>
<p>work have demonstrated the importance of water and hydrated cations on the elementary processes involved in CO2R 
</p>
<p>superseding the role of CO2 gas phase within the GDLs. Therefore, researchers have proposed that it is necessary for 
</p>
<p>the catalyst to be covered with electrolyte to be active. This means that although CO2 is supplied to the GDE from the 
</p>
<p>gas phase, the reactant at the catalyst site is still dissolved CO2. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>B. Charge Balances 
</p>
<p> The electronic and ionic charge balance in the cathode and anode current feeders, the electrolyte, and GDEs are 
</p>
<p>solved for using a secondary current distribution interface that accounts for the effect of the electrode kinetics in 
</p>
<p>addition to solution resistance. The model assumes that concentration-dependent Butler-Volmer charge transfer 
</p>

<p>C. Multicomponent Transport 
</p>
<p> At the cathode serpentine channel, CO2 gas is supplied as reactant, meaning that the gas in the channel and GDE 
</p>
<p>consists of three components: unreacted CO2 and products C2H4, and H2. In the anode, O2 product is resupplied, 
</p>
<p>consisting of two product components: O2 and water vapor. Nitrogen (N2) is used as a carrier gas for the product O2 
</p>
<p>and water vapor.  
</p>
<p> The material transport is described by the Maxwell-Stefan’s diffusion and convection equations, solved by using a 
</p>
<p>transport of concentrated species interface for each electrode flow serpentine channel. The boundary conditions at the 
</p>
<p>walls of the gas channel and GDE are zero mass flux (insulating condition). At the inlet, the composition is specified, 
</p>
<p>while the outlet condition is convective flux. This assumption means that the convective term dominates the transport 
</p>
<p>perpendicular to this boundary. Continuity in composition and flux apply for all mass balances at the interfaces 
</p>
<p>between the GDEs and the serpentine channels. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>D. Gas and Liquid Flow Equations 
</p>
<p> The Brinkman equation describes flow in porous media where momentum transport by shear stresses in the fluid is 
</p>
<p>of importance. The model extends Darcy’s law to include a term that accounts for the viscous transport, in the 
</p>
<p>momentum balance, and introduces velocities in the spatial directions as dependent variables. In the model, the 
</p>
<p>Brinkman equations interface is used for solving the velocity field and pressure in both the cathode and anode 
</p>
<p>serpentines channels.  
</p>
<p> The compressible Navier-Stokes equations govern the flow in the open channel sections and the Brinkman equations 
</p>
<p>in the porous GDEs. Couplings for the density, velocity, pressure and net mass sources and sinks are made to the 
</p>
<p>transport of concentrated species interfaces by using reacting flow multiphysics. The incompressible Navier-Stokes 
</p>
<p>equations govern the liquid flow in the electrolyte serpentine channel. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>E. Heat Transfer Equations 
</p>
<p> A general energy balance equation is implemented in the model to obtain the temperature distribution throughout 
</p>
<p>the MFECR including all solid parts and three flows running through the cathode, anode, and electrolyte serpentine 
</p>
<p>channels. The model considers two heat transfer mechanisms for the heat exchange between the MFECR’s surface 
</p>
<p>and the ambient, natural convection and thermal radiation. For natural convection, constant heat transfer coefficients 
</p>
<p>are originally assumed based on values found in the literature and finally determined via experimental validation using 
</p>
<p>modeled and experimentally measured temperature values throughout the MFECR’s surface. Thermal radiation 
</p>
<p>emissivity of the MFECR’s materials is not found in the literature and used in the model to compute the radiative heat 
</p>
<p>transfer mechanism between MFECR’s surface and the ambient.  
</p>
<p> As an electrical potential is applied between the two GDE’s, an electrical current is generated and carried 
</p>
<p>throughout the walls of the cathode, anode, and electrolyte serpentine channels, the model accounts for this source of 
</p>
<p>energy transformed in heat generation via the Joule heating effect equation.   
</p>
<p>IV. Model Implementation  
</p>
<p>   The 3D model was implemented in COMSOL4, a cross-platform finite element analysis, solver and multiphysics 
</p>
<p>simulation software. It allows conventional physics-based user interfaces and coupled systems of partial differential 
</p>
<p>equations (PDEs). COMSOL provides an integrated development environment (IDE) and unified workflow for 
</p>
<p>electrical, mechanical, fluid, acoustics, and chemical applications. The complexity and the number of coupled 
</p>
<p>multiphysics involved in the model lead to demanding significant computational power; a typical run case of the model 
</p>
<p>requires 25-30 GB of RAM.     </p>

<p>V. Model Validation  
</p>
<p>The model developed and deployed by the authors at MSFC has the multiphysics foundations and rigor to simulate 
</p>
<p>the actual EC process allowing the user to optimize the design and operation of the MFECR aimed and manufactured 
</p>
<p>at EDU scale to efficiently yield metabolic CO2 reduction to C2H4 and generate H2 and O2 as byproducts at ambient 
</p>
<p>conditions. The MFECR is installed in a test stand at MSFC equipped with all the instrumentation and sensors that 
</p>
<p>will allow the validation of the model including determination of the EC kinetics parameters for the key EC reactions 
</p>
<p>that take place on the cathode and the anode CL domains.  
</p>
<p>All inlet-outlet (IO) streams of the MFECR are fully monitored inline yielding and logging data on a) flow rate, 
</p>
<p>temperature, and pressure for all IO streams, b) pH on both input and output KOH solution streams, and c) gas 
</p>
<p>composition on the cathode and anode outlet gas streams. The IO streams include 1) the inlet CO2 gas and outlet 
</p>
<p>product streams at the cathode serpentine channel, 2) the inlet O2 carrier gas and the outlet product streams at the 
</p>
<p>anode serpentine channel, and 3) the inlet and outlet KOH-solution streams at the electrolyte serpentine channel.  
</p>
<p> Assuming electron transfer is the rate-determining step on both GDEs, the concentration-dependent Butler-Volmer 
</p>
<p>charge-transfer kinetics yields three parameters (exchange current density i_o, cathodic charge transfer α_c, and 
</p>
<p>anodic charge transfer α_a) for each one of the three EC reactions considered in the MFECR’s model, reactions 1 
</p>
<p>(CO2 reduction), 2 (H2 formation) and 3 (O2 formation). 
</p>
