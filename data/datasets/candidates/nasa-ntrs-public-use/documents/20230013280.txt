<p>NASA’s Space Launch System: Artemis I 
</p>
<p>Results and the Path Forward 
</p>
<p>John Honeycutt1 and Dr. John Blevins2 
NASA’s Space Launch System Program, Huntsville, AL, 35808, USA 
</p>
<p>Dr. Sharon Cobb3 
NASA’s Space Launch System Program, Huntsville, AL, 35808, USA 
</p>
<p>William Bryan4 
NASA’s Space Launch System Program, Huntsville, AL, 35808, USA 
</p>
<p>The Artemis era of human space exploration beyond low Earth orbit launched at 1:47 a.m. EST on November 
</p>
<p>16, 2022. Artemis I was the first integrated flight of the agency’s new super heavy-lift rocket, the Space 
</p>
<p>Launch System (SLS), and the next-generation spacecraft for astronauts, Orion. The mission sent an 
</p>
<p>uncrewed Orion spacecraft into a distant retrograde orbit about the Moon. During the 25-day mission, NASA 
</p>
<p>collected valuable data on the performance of the launch vehicle and crew spacecraft and information on the 
</p>
<p>deep space environment where crews will soon operate. Orion splashed down approximately 80 miles off the 
</p>
<p>coast of Baja, California, at the conclusion of the mission December 11. This paper will provide a summary 
</p>
<p>of the launch campaign from vehicle integration and testing through completion of the upper stage phase 
</p>
<p>with primary emphasis on SLS. It will also discuss highlights of progress on manufacturing and testing of 
</p>
<p>SLS hardware and software for upcoming Artemis missions. 
</p>
<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>At 1:47 a.m. EST on November 16, 2022, NASA’s new super-heavy lift rocket, SLS, launched for the first time on 
</p>
<p>the Artemis I mission, sending an uncrewed Orion spacecraft on a trip around the Moon (Fig. 1, 2). 
</p>
<p>SLS is a key part of NASA’s Artemis campaign – the agency’s efforts to return humans to Moon. The SLS Program 
</p>
<p>is managed at NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama, as part of the agency’s Moon to Mars 
</p>
<p>office. The launch vehicle produces more maximum thrust than any previous NASA rocket and can send 27 metric 
</p>
<p>tons (t) of payload to translunar injection (TLI) in its initial Block 1 crew configuration. SLS is the only rocket capable 
</p>
<p>of launching that much payload to the Moon in a single launch. Future variants of the rocket will increase the TLI 
</p>
<p>mass to more than 46 t, and the rocket will be the only launch vehicle capable of launching astronauts in Orion and a 
</p>
<p>10 t co-manifested payload to the Moon in a single launch. The rocket can also be configured with wide-diameter 
</p>
<p>payload fairings to launch a wide variety of large and heavy cargo and spacecraft, such as flagship science payloads 
</p>
<p>or Mars habitats, to various deep space destinations (Fig. 3). 
</p>

<p>NASA is leading an international team to establish a sustainable, permanent presence in near-rectilinear halo orbit 
</p>
<p>(NRHO) about the Moon and at the lunar South Pole, where astronauts can explore the surface, perform breakthrough 
</p>
<p>scientific investigations, and test technologies to prepare for human missions to Mars. 
</p>
<p>II. SLS Architecture 
</p>
<p>The SLS architecture is designed to be flexible, evolvable, leverage proven spaceflight propulsion systems, and 
</p>
<p>utilize new avionics hardware and software. Remaining consistent – in both the crew and cargo configurations – are 
</p>
<p>a core stage powered by four RS-25 liquid hydrogen/liquid oxygen (LH2/LOX) engines and two five-segment solid 
</p>
<p>rocket boosters (Fig. 4). Maximum thrust in this configuration for the Block 1 vehicle is 8.8 million pounds (39,100 
</p>
<p>kN). The rocket stands 322 feet (98 m) tall and weighs 5.75 million pounds (2.6 million kg) in the Block 1 crew 
</p>
<p>configuration at liftoff (Fig. 3). The Block 2 variant, which will onramp evolved five-segment solid rocket boosters, 
</p>
<p>will produce 9.4 million pounds (42,000 kN) maximum thrust. </p>
<p></p>

<p>The interim cryogenic propulsion stage (ICPS), a derivative of the highly successful United Launch Alliance 
</p>
<p>(ULA) Delta Cryogenic Second Stage (DCSS), is the upper stage on Block 1. It uses one RL10 engine and produces 
</p>
<p>approximately 24,750 pounds of thrust (110 kN). Beginning on the fourth flight, a new upper stage developed by 
</p>
<p>NASA and Boeing, called the Exploration Upper Stage (EUS), will debut using four RL10 engines. The stage will 
</p>
<p>produce 97,360 pounds (433 kN) of thrust. Mass to TLI will increase from 27 t to 38 t in crew configuration. When 
</p>
<p>new, evolved solid rocket boosters are debuted on the ninth flight, mass to TLI in the crew configuration will increase 
</p>
<p>to 43 t.

<p>Including space shuttle flights, ground testing, and the Artemis I flight, the RS-25 design has accumulated more 
</p>
<p>than 1 million seconds of hot-fire experience. Following the fourth flight of SLS, new production engines will be 
</p>

<p>III. Artemis I Test Flight Results 
</p>
<p>Post-flight data analysis and flight reconstruction recently concluded, and the post-flight analysis report is 
</p>
<p>complete. Data indicate that the launch vehicle executed at a high level of precision and accuracy. All elements and 
</p>
<p>systems performed well within safety and performance margins. Based on that data, preparations are in work to fly 
</p>
<p>astronauts on the second flight – the Artemis II lunar flyby mission – as originally manifested with only mission-
</p>
<p>specific changes. 
</p>
<p>The SLS core stage and boosters inserted the ICPS and Orion into an initial Earth orbit at a velocity of 25,579.86 
</p>
<p>ft./sec. (7,796.74 m/sec.) – 6.58 ft./sec. (2.01 m/sec.) off nominal – a difference of 0.026 %. Orbital insertion apogee 
</p>
<p>was just 2.9 mi. (4.67 km) shy of nominal. Predicted orbital insertion parameters were 975 mi. (1,560 km) by 16 mi. 
</p>
<p>(25.7 km). Actual parameters were 972.1 mi. (1,564 km) by 16 mi. (25.7 km) – a difference of 0.30 %. The low perigee 
</p>
<p>ensured the core stage would re-enter Earth’s atmosphere. Following orbital insertion, the ICPS performed a perigee 
</p>
<p>raise maneuver. 
</p>
<p>The ICPS and Orion spent approximately one revolution in Earth orbit before the upper stage burned its RL10 for 
</p>
<p>record duration of approximately 18 min. for TLI. Orion separation from ICPS occurred shortly after. At the time of 
</p>
<p>separation, the ICPS was traveling more than 22,000 mph (35,406 km/hr.). The stage’s final burn was a disposal burn 
</p>
<p>to place it in a heliocentric orbit. The 10 CubeSat secondary payloads, all 6 Unit (6U) form factor, deployed after 
</p>
<p>completion of the disposal burn. The CubeSats, developed by NASA, universities, industry, and international partners, 
</p>
<p>have experienced varying degrees of success to date. 
</p>
<p>Related to the performance of the individual elements of SLS, the twin solid rocket boosters burned out within 0.4 
</p>
<p>sec. of each other, hit peak thrust within 0.1 sec. of each other, and performed within 0.25% of each other during 
</p>
<p>ascent. The 50-psi separation cue was within 0.04 sec. for all pressure gauges (three each) for both boosters. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>5 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The SLS core stage’s 999 sensors and 45 miles (72 km) of cabling executed all functions. The cork-clad base heat 
</p>
<p>shield successfully protected the structure from exhaust temperatures up to 3,200° F. Main engine cut off (MECO) 
</p>
<p>occurred approximately eight min. into flight, while the vehicle was travelling more than 16,000 mph (25,750 km/hr.). 
</p>
<p>The stage’s RS-25 engines’ thrust and mixture ratio control valves were within 0.5% of predicted values. Internal 
</p>
<p>pressures and temperatures were within 2% of predicted values. Maximum acceleration on the launch vehicle occurred 
</p>
<p>shortly before MECO and topped off at approximately 3.25 G. 
</p>
<p>The flight software’s overall performance was well within required values. The transition from Ground Launch 
</p>
<p>Sequencer (GLS) to Automated Launch Sequencer (ALS) at T-:33 seconds was nominal, and all ALS functions were 
</p>
<p>performed without issue. No avionics hardware issues occurred during flight, nor were there any trigger-level “close 
</p>
<p>calls” in the abort monitor system. There was excellent core stage LH2 and LOX closed loop ullage control. 
</p>
<p> 
Fig. 4 The Artemis I SLS’s twin solid rocket boosters and four RS-25 engines propel the rocket and 
</p>
<p>spacecraft off the launch pad for the first time on Nov. 16, 2022. 
</p>
<p>IV. Artemis I Launch Attempt Data 
</p>
<p>The first launch attempt countdown started approximately 46 hours ahead of the opening of the Aug. 29, 2022, 
</p>
<p>launch window. Tanking operations commenced approximately seven hours before launch. Start of tanking was 
</p>
<p>delayed by nearly an hour because of lightning in the area around Launch Complex 39B. In the early stages of loading 
</p>
<p>cryogenic LH2 into the core stage LH2 tank, a leak was detected in the tail service mast umbilical (TSMU). A pre-
</p>
<p>established procedure to attempt to re-seat the seal – allowing the seal to warm up and then gradually cooling the seal 
</p>
<p>– was successful. LH2 flow was momentarily paused again due to an over-pressurization in the lines. Essentially, 
</p>
<p>when flow was started back after leak hold, the lines were warm and another minor chilldown was needed. Once 
</p>
<p>chilled, the flow went smoothly (Fig. 5). 
</p>
<p>Approximately three hours before the opening of the launch window, an engine thermal conditioning test was 
</p>
<p>conducted. During the test, data indicated that three of the four engines cooled adequately, though not fully. Engine 
</p>
<p>3, though, appeared to be further out of the expected temperature range than the others. Teams first warmed the bleed 
</p>
<p>valve and cooled it back down to see if that procedure would cause it to respond properly. When that procedure did 
</p>
<p>not yield satisfactory results, the bleed valves on the other three engines were closed, and the cryo flow was pushed 
</p>
<p>through Engine 3. The temperature sensor still did not report the desired temperature, and a potentially dangerous 
</p>
<p>high-pressure environment was introduced in the system. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>6 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Due to the tanking delay, weather considerations, and a suspected leak in a quick disconnect (QD) between the 
</p>
<p>intertank and its umbilical, Launch Director Charlie Blackwell-Thompson – NASA’s first female launch director – 
</p>
<p>scrubbed for the day. Ultimately, engineers determined that the bleed valve problem was most likely an issue with the 
</p>
<p>temperature sensor and not a problem with the engine itself. The leak in the intertank QD was determined to be the 
</p>
<p>result of the seal getting warm during the time the engine conditioning anomaly was being resolved. When cryogenics 
</p>
<p>started flowing across the seal again, it leaked. Warming the seal up and slowly cooling it back down allowed it to 
</p>
<p>seal properly. 
</p>
<p>The procedure to condition the engines on the launch attempt was different than the one used during the core stage 
</p>
<p>green run testing at Stennis, due in part to the need to conserve LH2 commodities at NASA’s Kennedy Space Center 
</p>
<p>in Florida. After data review, the procedures for conditioning were modified to closer mimic the Stennis procedures 
</p>
<p>on future launch attempts.  
</p>
<p>A second launch attempt occurred on Sept. 3. The countdown resumed approximately seven hours before launch 
</p>
<p>as the launch vehicle was still mostly configured from the first launch attempt. Soon after LH2 tanking began, a leak 
</p>
<p>was detected in the 8-in. QD. Teams followed the warming-then-cooling procedure once again, but it was 
</p>
<p>unsuccessful. Ground systems teams then closed valves and tried using the helium pressurization system to reseat the 
</p>
<p>seal. It also did not work. Following troubleshooting and analysis, the warming-cooling cycle was attempted again. It 
</p>
<p>was unsuccessful, and Blackwell-Thompson once again called a scrub. 
</p>
<p>Ground systems teams decided to remove and replace the 8-in. QD seal, eliminating further launch opportunities 
</p>
<p>in that launch period (Launch Period 25). Technicians constructed a temporary structure on the mobile launcher 
</p>
<p>platform at the launch pad, where the repairs were performed. Following repair, a cryogenic demonstration test was 
</p>
<p>conducted to evaluate the repairs, during which the team confirmed a successful repair and collected valuable data 
</p>
<p>about the vehicle. 
</p>
<p>Due to necessary repairs and flight termination system certification, teams then targeted a November launch 
</p>
<p>attempt. While the SLS and Orion stack was parked at the pad, a low-pressure weather system developed that would 
</p>
<p>quickly form into Hurricane Nicole. After careful consideration, engineers made the decision to leave the rocket at the 
</p>
<p>pad, in part due to the short amount of time between when it became apparent that Kennedy was in the hurricane’s 
</p>
<p>path and when it would strike. Teams analyzed predicted forces on the vehicle and included consequences of a 
</p>
<p>mechanical failure during the multi-hour rollback where the vehicle would be stuck part way between the launch pad 
</p>
<p>and the Vehicle Assembly Building – the worst-case scenario. Post-storm data analysis concluded that the forces the 
</p>
<p>vehicle experienced during the major weather event were within SLS limits and no significant damage occurred. 
</p>
<p>Teams then pressed on to a launch campaign targeted for Nov. 16. 
</p>
<p>V. Thorough Testing Leads to Successful Flight 
</p>
<p>Critical to the successful Artemis I flight was a thorough test campaign that evaluated every component, element, 
</p>
<p>and system of the SLS rocket and Artemis I system. Structural testing at Marshall provided teams with confidence of 
</p>
<p>the strength of the core stage components, along with a quantitative understanding of the margins of those components. 
</p>
<p>During the structural test campaign, five structural test articles (STAs) underwent a combined 199 tests and produced 
</p>
<p>421 gigabytes of data that were fed into computer models. The LH2 STA withstood more than 260% of expected 
</p>
<p>flight loads for over five hours before a buckling point was detected. Point of failure then came at that location. 
</p>
<p>Flight software testing in Marshall’s Software Integration Lab (SIL) and Software Integration Test Facility (SITF) 
</p>
<p>flew the rocket thousands of times through a large envelope of flight conditions, enabling the actual flight to be 
</p>
<p>successful. 
</p>
<p> The Green Run test series at Stennis evaluated the integrated core stage in tanking and launch environments. 
</p>
<p>Various characteristics were collected during the campaign, which culminated in tanking the stage and two hot fire 
</p>
<p>tests of the core stage. While the first hot fire test did not reach the full-duration, thus requiring a second hot fire test, 
</p>
<p>important lessons were collected. Simply put, without the green run testing, the Artemis I mission would not have 
</p>
<p>been successful. Many of the lessons learned from the test campaign proved vital during launch operations at Kennedy. 
</p>
<p> During final preparation of the launch vehicle at Kennedy, the Integrated Test and Check-Out (ITCO) test 
</p>
<p>campaign collected data on the integrated Artemis launch vehicle – including the Orion spacecraft and Exploration </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>7 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Ground Systems. The 10 tests included modal testing, end-to-end communication check-outs, and software checkout 
</p>
<p>and testing, and culminated with the wet dress rehearsal. 
</p>
<p>VI. Wet Dress Rehearsal 
</p>
<p>Artemis I first rolled out to Launch Complex 39B during the overnight hours of March 17-18, 2022, for the wet 
</p>
<p>dress rehearsal (WDR) test. 
</p>
<p>Between April 1 and 14, teams conducted three WDR attempts, collecting data and lessons learned each time. 
</p>
<p>WDR 4 began on June 18 with tanking on June 20. During the tanking attempt – using lessons learned from the first 
</p>
<p>three attempts – both the core stage and ICPS tanks were fully loaded with cryos and put in top-off replenish mode. 
</p>
<p>During tanking, an LH2 leak was discovered in a QD in the TSMU. The warm-then-cool procedure was once again 
</p>
<p>implemented but was unsuccessful. In an effort to get the most data possible, the team developed a plan that would 
</p>
<p>mask the problem in the ground control computers and software, hiding hold-initiating data associated with the leak 
</p>
<p>to get as far as possible into the countdown. Testing of this plan was performed in an alternate firing room in the 
</p>
<p>Launch Control Center to ensure safety of the launch vehicle. 
</p>
<p>Following this procedure and software change, the team was able to get deep into the terminal countdown and 
</p>
<p>performed several critical operations, including transfer of the count from the GLS to ALS. As predicted, the ALS on 
</p>
<p>SLS detected an out-of-specification setting shortly after the handoff and stopped the countdown at T-29 sec. Enough 
</p>
<p>data were collected that the teams felt confident to progress toward a launch campaign. Before rollback to the Vehicle 
</p>
<p>Assembly Building and launch preparations, a hot fire test of the hydrazine-powered booster thrust vector control 
</p>
<p>(TVC) system occurred at the pad, completing the WDR objectives. 
</p>

</p>
<p>While the hardware, software, and systems were being tested and prepared, the teams also conducted multiple 
</p>
<p>countdown and launch simulations, which prepared them for the Artemis I countdown and launch. Countdown 
</p>
<p>simulations have been the backbone of NASA launch operations from the beginning. Hundreds of people at Kennedy, 
</p>
<p>Marshall, NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston, and multiple partner sites across the country support launch, 
</p>
<p>ascent, and flight operations. 
</p>
<p>VII. Progress to Artemis II and Beyond 
</p>
<p>While much of the attention in 2022 and 2023 focused on the Artemis I mission, tremendous progress was made 
</p>
<p>towards Artemis II and beyond. 
</p>
<p>A. Artemis II 
</p>
<p>At the time of this writing, the Artemis II SLS core stage is structurally complete, with the intertank, forward skirt, 
</p>
<p>engine section, and LH2 and LOX tanks mated at NASA’s Michoud Assembly Facility in New Orleans. The RS-25 
</p>
<p>engines are ready and are being integrated to the stage this month. The core stage is expected to ship to Kennedy late 
</p>
<p>this year, depending on alignment with enterprise schedules of the many elements of the Artemis II mission. The 
</p>
<p>booster segments for Artemis II have been complete for some time; they are currently being loaded on rail cars at 
</p>
<p>prime contractor Northrop Grumman’s facilities in Utah for shipping to Kennedy this fall. The aft skirt assemblies, 
</p>
<p>with TVC, are also complete at Kennedy. 
</p>
<p>The ICPS completed primary assembly at ULA’s manufacturing facility in Decatur, Alabama, and was shipped to 
</p>
<p>the company’s facilities at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in July 2021. It is in storage before the hand off to 
</p>
<p>NASA this fall. Both the launch vehicle stage adapter (LVSA) and Orion stage adapter (OSA) have completed 
</p>
<p>manufacturing. The LVSA is complete and is in storage at Marshall. The OSA has completed painting, and additional 
</p>
<p>processing is underway at Marshall. 
</p>
<p>In addition to the SLS rocket, significant progress is being made on the Orion spacecraft and Exploration Ground 
</p>
<p>Systems for the Artemis II mission.  The Orion spacecraft is undergoing testing before being mated to its service 
</p>
<p>module. The mobile launcher, which will launch SLS and Orion, is outfitted with the Crew Access Arm and is at 
</p>
<p>Launch Complex 39B for testing. The three NASA astronauts and one CSA (Canadian Space Agency) astronaut who 
</p>
<p>will fly the mission are training for their lunar expedition (Fig. 6). 
</p>

<p>B. Artemis III 
Artemis III is scheduled to be the first human landing on the Moon since the Apollo 17 mission in December 1972 
</p>
<p>and is targeted for no earlier than late 2025. The core stage LOX tank, LH2 tank, intertank, and engine section are 
</p>
<p>being manufactured at Michoud. The intertank and forward skirt have completed thermal protection system (TPS) 
</p>
<p>spray installation. The forward skirt is being outfitted for flight. The RS-25 engines are complete. The booster 
</p>
<p>segments for Artemis III are also complete and ready at Northrop Grumman facilities in Utah. Refurbishment and 
</p>
<p>buildup of the booster TVC systems are underway. 
</p>
<p>The Artemis III ICPS completed manufacturing at ULA’s Decatur, Alabama, facilities and has been shipped to 
</p>
<p>ULA’s facilities at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida for final processing. The Artemis III OSA has 
</p>
<p>completed manufacturing and is undergoing secondary structure and cable installation. The LVSA has been welded, 
</p>
<p>and TPS installation is complete. The adapter’s frangible joint assembly and pneumatic actuator system brackets have 
</p>
<p>also been installed. 
</p>
<p>C. Progress to Artemis IV and Block 1B 
Artemis IV is scheduled to be the debut of the SLS Block 1B variant, featuring the new EUS. Weld confidence 
</p>
<p>articles are being manufactured at Michoud as well as Marshall. The EUS assembly area at Michoud has opened. 
</p>
<p>Work on the Artemis IV intertank, LOX tank, LH2 tank, and engine section is underway at Michoud (Fig. 7). The 
</p>
<p>RS-25 engines are in processing and are scheduled to be complete later this year. Work on the Artemis IV boosters is 
</p>
<p>also underway. Propellant segments are being cast, and refurbishment of forward structures is underway. Test and 
</p>
<p>demonstration panels and articles for the EUS, interstage, payload adapter, and universal stage adapter are being 
</p>
<p>manufactured and analyzed. 
</p>
<p>Manufacture, testing, and certification of new-production RS-25 engines that will be used on Artemis V and 
</p>
<p>beyond is underway. These re-engineered engines are designed to be at least 30% more affordable than shuttle-era 
</p>
<p>engines through the use of modern manufacturing technologies, including additive manufacturing. The engine 
</p>
<p>certification test program, consisting of 12 certification tests of a new-production engine successfully concluded in 
</p>
<p>June. The Retrofit 3b test series will start this fall, consisting of an additional 12 tests on the Fred Haise Test Stand at 
</p>
<p>Stennis. It is necessary as some new-production components require two certification test series to be certified for use. 
</p>
<p>The flight software that will fly the new SLS variant is being developed at Marshall. 
</p>
<p>D. Progress to SLS Block 2 
On the ninth SLS flight, the Block 2 variant of SLS will make its debut, featuring the EUS and the evolved BOLE 
</p>
<p>solid rocket boosters. The boosters will feature composite carbon fiber-wound cases, replacing the heritage steel cases, 
</p>
<p>thus reducing mass and increasing payload. A new propellant mixture will also be used. Thrust on the new boosters 
</p>
<p>will increase from 3.6 million pounds (16,014 kN) to 4.2 million pounds (18,683 kN) each. The first full-scale 
</p>
<p>horizontal static test-firing of the BOLE Demonstration Motor-1 is currently scheduled for Fall 2024. Motor segments 
</p>
<p>are currently being cast at Northrop Grumman facilities in Utah for that test. In addition, process simulation articles 
</p>
<p>are being manufactured to test and validate procedures to produce the BOLE boosters. 
</p>

<p>E. Conclusion 
</p>
<p>The Artemis era of space exploration is underway, and in this era, humanity will explore the Moon more 
</p>
<p>extensively than ever before and develop and test technologies that will be the foundation for human missions to Mars 
</p>
<p>and back in the 2030s. Building on the success of the first flight of SLS, NASA and its partners are building, testing, 
</p>
<p>and developing the hardware for the next four flights and Block 1B and Block 2 variants. This work ensures the nation 
</p>
<p>will have assured access to deep space and a reliable, capable rocket as the backbone for its most ambitious missions. </p>
