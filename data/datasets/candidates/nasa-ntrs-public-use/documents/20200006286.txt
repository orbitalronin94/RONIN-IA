Motivation & Objective
 Transition location can significantly impact vehicle performance:
• Potential for significant fuel-burn reduction (~5-10%) on subsonic transport aircraft through the implementation of
natural laminar-flow surfaces.
 Translates to a savings of 2-3.5M lbs of fuel per airplane per year ($10M-$17.5M per airplane).

 Goal: To improve our fundamental understanding of laminar-to-turbulent boundary-layer transition – so
that we can better predict and control transition.
• Transition is a highly complex flow phenomenon.
• Stability and transition theory is well developed, but is often insufficient in the real world.
• Real flows over real surfaces (such as airplane wings) will encounter flow phenomena that are not easy to predict:
 Impact of surface roughness and freestream disturbances
 Impact of large 2D or 3D surface irregularities (such as steps – the focus of this work)
 Nonlinear interactions
 The breakdown process
• Need to know which instabilities are important so we can target the right one(s) for prediction or control.
• Even the most comprehensive CFD is not always sufficient for these tasks. Experiments are necessary to inform and provide
direction for the computations and to improve our physics-based modeling (i.e., you need to know what you’re looking for).

Background: Steps
 Steps can cause drastic local changes to the mean boundary layer.
• Existing instabilities may be more or less destabilized by these changes.
• New instabilities can be destabilized.

 We want to predict which step height(s) will cause transition to move upstream.
• Real wings will have steps, gaps, rivets, etc. – need to know what can be tolerated to maintain laminar flow.
• Empirical correlations are typically used.
 Does not take into account all important aspects of the flow.
 The affected instabilities may change depending on the situation.

• Showed that an unsteady traveling-crossflow-like instability was destabilized in the separated (helical flow)

Backward-Facing Step: New Understanding
 Experimental Results:
• Only a small local effect was seen on the stationary crossflow.
• Unsteady instabilities were destabilized in the helical flow region.
 Several different types of unsteady instabilities were identified, and they continued to grow downstream of the
separated region, due to nonlinear interactions.
 These unsteady instabilities interacted with the stationary crossflow, causing them to become modulated.
• The interaction of the unsteady instabilities and the stationary crossflow led to large velocity fluctuations.
