<p>Liftoff Environments Overview
</p>
<p>•Liftoff Environments
– Liftoff environment – represents a source of loading during the hold-down or liftoff phase of a rocket launch.
– A launch vehicle is subjected to transient and oscillatory pressure loading on its exterior surfaces induced by 
</p>
<p>engines and boosters during vehicle hold-down and liftoff
•A principal source of structural vibration which may result in the malfunction of vehicle components or the fatigue of 
</p>
<p>exterior skin panels or component brackets (Loads)
• External pressure loading gives rise to sound pressure within the vehicle which may impact components and other 
</p>
<p>equipment (Vibroacoustics)
• External pressure loading gives rise to sound pressure inside the crew cabin which may impact the crew's health, 
</p>
<p>safety, or ability to communicate (Crew)
</p>
<p>– There are eight Liftoff Environments for SLS
</p>
<p>• Pressure Transients
• Booster Ignition Overpressure &amp; 
</p>
<p>Duct Overpressure
• Excess Hydrogen Pop
• Booster Igniter Shock
• Core Stage Engine Overpressure
</p>
<p>• Acoustics
• Liftoff Acoustics
• Hold-down Acoustics
• RS-25 Nozzle Flow Transient 
</p>
<p>Acoustics
• Infrasonic Acoustics
</p>

<p>Excess Hydrogen Pop
</p>
<p>•Background
– Excess hydrogen accumulation
• Results from ignition of excess hydrogen
• RS-25 engines are hydrogen fuel lead: fuel valve 
</p>
<p>command, fuel valve moves, flow initiated, ignition
• Core Auxiliary Power Unit (CAPU) exhaust ducts emit 
</p>
<p>hydrogen
– Deflagration or detonation depends on how much H2
</p>
<p>accumulates and how well it mixes in air
– Can result in damaging steep-fronted waves
</p>
<p>Core Heat Shield Excess H2 Pop
</p>
<p>•Mitigation Approach
– Hydrogen Burn-off Igniters (HBOIs) - intended to burn 
</p>
<p>off the hydrogen/air mixture before it accumulates 
</p>
<p>•Modeling Approach
– Predictions based on reconstruction of heritage data 
</p>
<p>including correlations from tests without HBOIs
</p>
<p>Imagery showing HBOIs
</p>

<p>RS-25 Nozzle Flow Transient Acoustics
</p>
<p>•Background
– Produced by unsteady flow that develops in the RS-25 
</p>
<p>nozzle during its start and shutdown operations (during 
filling/emptying of nozzle)
</p>
<p>– Short duration 0.75 seconds during hold-down
– Response generally peaks in the 125 Hz 1/3 octave band
– Loudest liftoff event on Space Shuttle
– Not always present on Shuttle – SLS had some low 
</p>
<p>amplitude residual response in the 125 Hz octave band
</p>
<p>•Modeling Approach
– Predictions are based on reconstruction of heritage data 
</p>
<p>and correlations from Space Shuttle launches
– Applicable especially on Core Heat Shield
– Peak is bounded due to expansive data history
</p>
<p>•Mitigation Approaches
– None - RS-25 start transient dependent; IOP/SS has some 
</p>
<p>influence on the acoustics
Nozzle Flow Transient on Space Shuttle 
</p>
<p>Orbiter Aft Heat Shield</p>

<p>RS-25 Nozzle Flow Transient Acoustics
</p>
<p>•RS-25 NFT Acoustics Mechanism
– Flow fills nozzle during start transient as 
</p>
<p>Pc increases.
– When the location of flow separation 
</p>
<p>reaches the end on nozzle, it becomes 
unstable. 
• The plume shock structure oscillates in and 
</p>
<p>out of the nozzle: dynamic flow reattaching 
to the nozzle
</p>
<p>– Flow unsteadiness is the source of NFT
Acoustics
• The unsteadiness is also the source of 
</p>
<p>sideloads leading to ovalization of nozzle 
(observed in video) RS-25 Engine Test</p>

<p>Infrasonic Acoustics
</p>
<p>•Background
– Noise generated during liftoff by the exhaust flow 
</p>
<p>and its interactions with surrounding launch pad 
structures
</p>
<p>– An extension of Liftoff Acoustics into the low 
frequency range at 20 Hz and below
</p>
<p>– The low frequency acoustics create loads on the 
external vehicle surfaces which then transmit 
through the vehicle into the Crew cabin
</p>
<p>– Data analysis is challenging due to low frequency 
and short duration
</p>
<p>•Modeling Approach
– SLS Scale Model Acoustic Test
</p>
<p>•Mitigation Approaches

<p>Time Correlation Assessments
</p>
<p>• Time alignment issues of Ares I-X flight test in 2009
– Acoustics team identified Ares I-X timestamps were out of sync between the Ground system 
</p>
<p>and Vehicle DFI data
• Ares I-X helped justify the data acquisition verification tests 
</p>
<p>– Characterization needed to quantify the frequency response and time alignment
• SIL DAU Characterization Test
</p>
<p>– SLS System Integration Lab (SIL) – replica of SLS Data Acquisition Units (DAUs)
– Evaluate the effect of each DAU and channels – considers filters, delays, and system effects
– Record a truth signal and ‘processed’ signal
– Cross correlation signal processing technique is a mathematically rigorous approach to 
</p>
<p>compute a metric at the point in time where the signals are best aligned 
– Eliminated the time alignment issues by providing appropriate delay correction values to the 
</p>
<p>avionics team
</p>
<p>Excellent Alignment across all SLS DAUs
Analysis Technique used for Alignment Assessment</p>

<p>Sensor Port Response
</p>
<p>• Reduction on some sensors over 10 dB 
</p>
<p>OASPL and 20+ dB in specific bands
</p>
<p>• Resonances impact Liftoff Acoustics, 
</p>
<p>Engine NFT Acoustics, and Hold-down 
</p>
<p>Acoustics
</p>
<p>• Some sensors showed a significant 
influence due to the port response
</p>
<p>• Removal is necessary to obtain accurate 
</p>
<p>acoustics
</p>
<p>• Methodology developed during Green 
</p>
<p>Run Hotfire test to remove response
– Using approach described in Casiano, M. J., 
</p>
<p>“Analytical and Numerical Modeling of Sensor 
</p>
<p>Port Acoustics,” NASA TP-2021-0000024, May 
</p>
<p>2021.
</p>
<p>– To remove the port resonance, data is ‘filtered’ 
</p>
<p>using a sensor port response model.  And 
</p>
<p>therefore, time data can be reproduced for 
</p>
<p>further analysis.

<p>Summary
</p>
<p>•A Liftoff Environment, or Launch Environment, represents a source of loading during the
</p>
<p>hold-down or liftoff phase of a rocket launch.
– Transient and acoustic loading on vehicle exterior surfaces
– 8 environments (Excess Hydrogen Pop, Core Stage Engine Overpressure, RS-25 Nozzle Flow Transient Acoustics,
</p>
<p>Hold-Down Acoustics, Booster Igniter Shock, Ignition Overpressure &amp; Duct Overpressure, Liftoff Acoustics, and
Infrasonic Acoustics)
</p>
<p>•Methods and Procedures in quantifying Launch Environments
– Computational Acoustics, Computational Fluid Dynamics, Scale Model Acoustic Testing, Reduced-order Modeling,
</p>
<p>Data Processing, and Signal Processing
</p>
<p>•The confidence in our Artemis I models and data ultimately help improve our confidence
</p>
<p>as we prepare for the first manned mission.</p>
