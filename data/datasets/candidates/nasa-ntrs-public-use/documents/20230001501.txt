Human Research Program (HRP) Use of Analogs
What are Spaceflight Analogs?
Analogs are environments that provide conditions similar to some (but not all) conditions encountered in spaceflight such as:
Isolation
Confinement
Physiological stressors
Environmental danger

Benefits to HRP
Studies completed more quickly and less expensively than flight studies.
Used as a “proving ground” before studies or countermeasures are implemented in space.
A more “controlled” environment, often more repeatable scenarios, and higher “n” than available in space.

Antarctic Stations as a Spaceflight Analog
Like spaceflight the Antarctic environment provides exposure to isolation, confinement, extreme/hostile conditions, limited rescue/resupply opportunities, long mission durations, and altered day/night cycles.
Currently, 3 primary stations used for HRP research – McMurdo, Palmer, and Amundsen-Scott South Pole Stations.
Access to additional stations (e.g., non-NSF stations) currently arranged on a per-study basis.
Study participants recruited from expeditioners deployed at the stations.
Research topics include mental health, team dynamics, performance, and immune system adaptation.

Project Title: Neurobehavioral Mental Health Checklist (MHCL)
PI: Dr. Candice Alfano  (University of Houston)




Methods
A comprehensive Mental Health Conditions Checklist (MHCL) was implemented at Amundsen–Scott South Pole and McMurdo Antarctic Stations, with data collected from 110 total participants winter-overing. 

The MHCL was repeatedly administered with a physical symptoms checklist to examine concurrent and sequential overlap between psychological and physical health symptoms. 




MHCL
Measure consists of 23 individual items (e.g., “effective”, “racing thoughts”) that ‘load’ on 3 primary categories: Positive Adaptation, Poor Self-Regulation, and Anxious Apprehension. 




Evaluated responses on measure relative to related measures that often serve as a “Gold Standard” and found good convergent validity (e.g. direct, positive relationship between “positive adaptation“ and sleep duration measured objectively). 




Results: MHCL Positive Adaptation decreased over time, whereas Poor Self-regulation and Severity of Physical Symptoms increased across the study period. The use of emotion regulation strategies during deployment and dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) levels predicted end-of-study MHCL scores, whereas trait-based psychological measures collected at the start of the mission showed little predictive utility.

Question: Is isolation and confinement as experienced in Antarctica, associated with brain and performance changes?

Methods
Prior to and following a winter-over at Neumayer station, nine participants were compared to control group (nine participants residing in Germany).

Measures
Pre-and-post deployment MRI.
Pre, post and in deployment cognitive testing.


MRI scanner


Project Title: Neurostructural, Cognitive, and Physiologic Changes During a 1-year Antarctic 
Winter-Over Mission
PIs: Dr. Mathias Basner and Dr. Alexander Stahn (University of Pennsylvania)
	




Results – Antarctica Participants Demonstrated:
Reduced volume in aspects of the brain, particularly in Dentate Gyrus. Reductions in dentate gyrus volume were associated with lower cognitive performance in tests of spatial processing.
Reductions in brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), needed for learning and memory associated with decreases in dentate gyrus volume.
No measurable decrements in cognitive performance.
Publication - Brain Changes in Response to Long Antarctic Expeditions. Stahn AC, Gunga HC, Kohlberg E, et al.N Engl J Med. 2019 Dec 5;381(23):2273-2275.

Hybrid Training (HT) System combined Virtual Reality technologies with exercise equipment at Neumayer Station. The system included:

Operational performance measures that participants could complete while cycling.

Contact electrodes for logging heart rate as indicator of exercise intensity.

Haptic feedback when increasing work intensity during uphill cycling, storms, or different terrains.

Real-time monitoring of comprehensive metrics for each training session.

Methods: 18 participants during two, 12-14 month winter-over missions in Neumayer station were expected to exercise at least 3 times per week for at least 30 minutes per session.

Matrix Reasoning (MRT)

Project Title: Hybrid Training - A Sensory Stimulation Countermeasure for Long Duration Space Exploration Missions
PIs: Dr. Mathias Basner and Dr. Alexander Stahn (University of Pennsylvania)
	




Results: Brain imaging post-mission showed that those who participated in HT did not show decreases in brain volume, compared to those without HT. HT participants also showed an increase in BDNF post-mission. Results suggest that Hybrid Training exhibits a protective effect on structural brain changes associated with social isolation and confinement, and should be considered as a target-specific neurobehavioral countermeasure.

Methods: Data collected from individuals working within teams during the Winter-Over 2018 season in Antarctica. The final data set consisted of 33 individuals, representing 12 Winter-Over Crews that were located at either the McMurdo Research Station or Amundsen-Scott Research Station. 
The types of teams that members represented include: 
Fire, Emergency Response, Search and Rescue, Dispatch, Science, Logistics, Management, Galley, Facility and Vehicle Maintenance.
Background: Research has found that in long duration, isolation environments, interpersonal dynamics occupy a key role in effective functioning. The investigators therefore conducted research across a variety of platforms to help determine key aspects of team roles and how to compose teams for future missions. 
Findings included: 
There are unique aspects to team roles in long duration.
Team roles are most often distributed, but there is variation across time.
Both task and social roles are important; social roles become of greater importance on longer duration missions.
Roles vary in their functionality depending on the temporal point in the mission.
The sharing of task roles may not be functional early in mission.



Project Title: Specialized Team Roles
PI: Dr. Eduardo Salas (Rice University)	




This research is part of a multi-pronged approach towards informing guidelines for composing and supporting future spaceflight teams

Background
This project aims to characterize team problem-solving strategies for future long duration space exploration tasks, to develop frameworks and recommendations for cultivating team-based problem-solving in the context of Lunar and Cis-Lunar operations (construction process of a future lunar base). 

Methods
Identify and characterize team-based decision-making lessons learned from military and civilian construction environments using interviews, task analyses and embedded task observation.
Leverage qualitative data analysis methodologies to extrapolate measures and metrics for team-based performance and problem-solving skills.
Trade-space exploration and systems engineering methodologies to model effective team performance outcomes and generate a framework for promoting team functioning resilience through pre-mission training and optimal interface with on-board support systems is planned.






Project Title: Problem-Solving Skills for Training Metrics, Standards, & Guidelines
PI: Dr. Aleksandra Stankovic (Massachusetts General Hospital)
In Progress
