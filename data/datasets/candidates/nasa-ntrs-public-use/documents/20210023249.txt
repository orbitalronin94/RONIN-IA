It currently requires significant computational cost to simulate the flow physics of the booster
separation event on the Space Launch System. This comes from the large parametric space in
which the event occurs, as preseparation flight conditions and separated booster core-relative
trajectories can vary. Functionally removing the risk of core-booster collision mandates careful
assessment of the fluid dynamics in terms of several trajectory parameters. However, simulating
the entire trajectory envelope is computationally intractable given the high parametric dimension.
In order to reduce the uncertainty in the resulting low-parametric-resolution aerodynamic
booster separation database, a data-driven approach was developed to select which breakpoints
should be studied by simulations and experiments and which should be relegated to a regression-
based interpolation procedure. This technique works by simulating cases where the flow physics
are most sensitive to changes in the parameters and leaving the less parametrically sensitive
regions for interpolation. The result is a booster separation run matrix whose computational
cost is comparable to that of previous database generations but has lower interpolation errors.

I. Introduction
The solid rocket booster (SRB) separation event (Figure 1) is a common and complicated necessity in space launch
system (SLS) missions. Solid rocket boosters employed in the liftoff and transition flight stages are jettisoned as the core
stage proceeds through its ascent into orbit. However, on a given trajectory, the boosters are not guaranteed to always
separate at the same airspeed, altitude, or rocket inclination. The duration of the booster separation motor firing, and the
resulting unpowered departure from the core stage, can likewise vary significantly due to the nuanced aerodynamics of
the flow regime [1]. Many of these booster trajectories lead them to impinge on the core engine plume, which is itself
highly variable in shape. This plume impingement can lead to several modes, in one of which, the boosters are entrained
in the core exhaust in such a way that they collide. Such an occurrence, naturally, must be avoided as much as possible.

Fig. 1 Illustration of the SLS booster separation event. Core-relative booster coordinates are noted, as is the
assumption that booster separation is symmetric in this paper.

A Monte Carlo approach is used to simulate the high-dimensional booster separation trajectory window. For a new SLS
configuration, it is conventional to begin with a high-uncertainty trajectory window based on an existing, similar SLS
configuration’s aerodynamic forcing database and the new configuration’s flight conditions [2]. An initial computational
fluid dynamics (CFD) run matrix is then constructed to fill, relatively sparsely, the convex parameter space defined
by this trajectory window. The resulting force and moment (F&M) database is then used to create a new trajectory
ensemble, from which additional run points are identified iteratively until there is adequate confidence that the final
database yields an accurate trajectory window [3].

In the case of symmetric booster separation, the parameters of interest are the boosters’ core-relative position
(Δ𝑥, Δ𝑦, Δ𝑧), the boosters’ core-relative rotation (Δ𝜓, Δ\),∗ the booster separation thrust coefficient 𝐶𝐵𝑆𝑀

core-stage parameters 𝛼𝑐, 𝛽𝑐, and 𝑞 [3]. Each of these parameters is treated independently in the database, meaning the
trajectory parameter space is eight-dimensional and populating the entire space is computationally intractable. In this
work, all parameters are tracked on the right SRB (RSRB).

Existing database construction methods ensure that the nominal trajectory and the trajectory bounds are included in the
CFD run matrix, but beyond that, the database’s breakpoints are at least initially linearly distributed. The important
exception is with Δ𝑥, which is used for organizing the other parameters; the result is a run matrix organized into several
Δ𝑥 slices with a relatively uniform distribution of breakpoints in the other parameters in each of these slices.

A limitation of this technique is that there is no guarantee that the flow physics behave predictably between these

breakpoints. The regression model employed by the trajectory simulator does not ensure its F&M interpolations are
physically accurate. Therefore, if the breakpoints miss local extrema in the F&M values, these are smeared over in the
trajectory simulations. This causes higher-than-desired uncertainty in the resulting trajectory simulations, which then
requires more costs to account for in the rest of the system design cycle.

This paper outlines a data-driven approach by which parameter breakpoints can be identified to reduce the F&M database
uncertainty. By selecting breakpoints based on the observed F&M sensitivity to each parameter, the resulting database
will be less likely to have missed important flow physics within the parameter space. The regression model, which uses
the database, then is less likely to interpolate incorrect flow physics amidst the breakpoints. This will be demonstrated
for a booster separation run matrix slice that was constructed using both the old and the new methods.

II. Methodology and Results
In particular, it is known that the booster side force coefficient 𝐶𝑌 and the booster yawing moment 𝐶𝐿𝑁 are centrally
influential in bringing the boosters to problematic points in the parameter space, e.g., core recontact. A reasonable first
step, then, is to study this force and moment in terms of each parameter of interest. The top half of Figure 2 shows the
individual values of CY and CLN plotted versus Δ𝜓, for all the trajectories, plotted in black. The thick red line in each
plot represents the nominal trajectory, and the thinner red line represents a random dispersed trajectory. The lower
half of Figure 2 shows the range, or absolute value of the difference between the maximum and minimum CY or CLN
value, at each booster yaw angle. While individual trajectories do not yield much insight, the trajectory collection as a
whole presents a clear trend: there is a larger variation in the side force and yawing moment in the region of Δ𝜓 closer
to the middle angles on the trajectory plots, apparent visually by the larger spread of coefficients at those values. For
roughly the middle third of the Δ𝜓 range, these F&M coefficients tend to be less sensitive to Δ𝜓 and therefore, fewer
breakpoints in these ranges will be necessary.

A. Downselection of Breakpoints via Covariance Decomposition
A covariance-based run matrix attempts to take into account the covariance of booster position variables in order to
more effectively allocate computational resources. It does this by restricting the run matrix points for highly correlated
variables to remove points that are nonphysical. Specifically, it eliminates points where the combination of position
variables cannot be reached even when the individual values are all reachable. The most important example of this is
that the boosters cannot simultaneously have the minimum lateral translation (Δ𝑦) and maximum yaw (Δ𝜓).

The position variables of particular interest are Δ𝑦, Δ𝑧, Δ\, and Δ𝜓 for the left and right boosters. The covariance
matrices for these variables are first calculated from Monte Carlo booster trajectory data sets. To be precise, a 4×4
covariance matrix is calculated at each value of Δ𝑥 (which for this limited study is fixed at one constant value).

Next, a matrix of points is found via Latin hypercube sampling (LHS). The 𝑀×𝑁 LHS matrix has 𝑀 points valued
between zero and one for each of the 𝑁 variables of interest. The columns of the LHS matrix are then centered to have a
mean of zero. This centered LHS matrix is multiplied by the lower triangular result of the Cholesky Decomposition of
the covariance matrix. The resulting matrix is discretized to a fixed number of breakpoints and mapped to the desired
value for each position variable. The process yields a matrix that has covariances similar to that of the covariance matrix
used to generate it. A matrix of breakpoints can be generated in this way for each Δ𝑥.

III. Results at one Δ𝑥
Breakpoints were constructed via both the traditional and this new method to quantify how the new method can reduce
the interpolation error in the usage of the resulting database. This was done only for one symmetric booster x-distance
to save on the computational cost of the verification.

For the sake of comparing fairly the breakpoints of the two methods, the minima and maxima in each other parameter
were set manually, consistent with the old method. Thus, the only differences between the two breakpoint sets were
interior to the parametric convex space. A third breakpoint set was also created for validation, which included mostly
points not in the other two sets. These were used to compare the interpolated sets to high-fidelity values.

IV. Conclusions and Future Work
A new, more data-driven method of selecting breakpoints for aerodynamic databases was developed, which has been
shown to reduce the resulting interpolation errors during database use. For complicated systems with a high number of
independent parameters, like the booster separation phase of the Space Launch System’s ascent trajectory, the necessarily
parametrically coarse nature of these databases mandates both a reduction and understanding of this interpolation error.
By ensuring that the trends in forces and moments are as smooth as can be expected between high-fidelity data points,
database users can apply interpolation schemes with confidence that dangerous excursions in the aerodynamic forcing
are not likely missed.

In addition to the reduction in interpolated database error, this data-driven method enables the number of breakpoints
in a given parameter to be informed by anticipated trends in that parameter’s particular sensitivity. Traditionally, all
independent parameters were treated equally – with the highest affordable level of resolution – because there was not
a reliable a priori metric to justify a coarsening of one parameter’s breakpoints over another. With this method, the
resolution of each parameter can be determined independently and the number of breakpoints can be selected by more
than just engineering judgment. For example, with the arbitrary data used to test the method in this paper, it was clear
that fewer than seven breakpoints would be necessary in 𝐶𝐵𝑆𝑀

𝑇
within the particular Δ𝑥 range observed. This can both

reduce the computational cost of database development and ensure that, for a given development cost, the parametric
resolution is prioritized where it is most needed.

While this method is promising enough that it has been implemented by NASA in ongoing database development efforts,
it does not rigorously address any possible coupling of the parameters, which are currently being treated as independent.
The covariance analysis ensures that physically reasonable breakpoints are those actually studied in the resulting
simulations and experiments, but whether the parameters identified as independent are both necessary and sufficient
still remains an engineering challenge. Increasing this method into higher dimensions - creating high-dimensional
histograms instead of those plotted here - is one possible way to further refine the breakpoint selection process while
simultaneously quantifying the quality of the independent parameters selected for observation.
