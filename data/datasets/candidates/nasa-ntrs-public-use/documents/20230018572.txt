<p>Artemis I Rollout from VAB to Launch Pad 39B
</p>
<p>• NASA is developing an expendable heavy lift launch vehicle capability, the 
Space Launch System (SLS), to support lunar and deep space exploration.
</p>
<p>• The Artemis I Dynamic Rollout Test (DRT) recorded accelerations on Artemis I, 
the Mobile Launcher (ML), and Crawler Transporter (CT), during its rollout 
from the Vehicle Assembly Building (VAB) to Launch Pad 39B in March 2022 
and back in April 2022.
</p>
<p>www.nasa.gov 01/29/2024 Operational Modal Analysis of the Artemis I DRT &amp; WDR 2</p>
<p></p>

<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Wet Dress Rehearsal
</p>
<p>• While Artemis 1 was at Launch Pad 39B, between the DRT rollout and rollback, the Wet Dress 
</p>
<p>Rehearsal (WDR) was performed to demonstrate launch readiness, which was to include 
</p>
<p>running through the planned launch countdown timeline and automated sequences all the 
</p>
<p>way down inside of T-10 seconds before stopping. 
</p>
<p>‒ Provided an opportunity to acquire modal characteristics of Artemis I in a partially fueled state.
</p>

<p>Ground Forces During Rollout &amp; At Launch Pad 39B
</p>
<p>• The rollout ground forces generated by the CT are the primary low frequency 
</p>
<p>excitation into the ML and SLS when the CT is moving.
</p>
<p>‒ CT Truck Track Shoes contacting the ground and passing under the CT Truck rollers.  
</p>
<p>• When the CT is stopped or when the ML and the SLS integrated vehicle are 
</p>
<p>sitting on the launch pad, the primary excitation consists of wind loading on 
</p>
<p>the ML Tower and the SLS  integrated vehicle.
</p>

<p>DRT and WDR OMA Challenges
</p>
<p>• Operational Modal Analysis (OMA) assumptions:
</p>
<p>‒ Linear time invariant structure.
</p>
<p>‒ Excitation is spatially distributed, uncorrelated, stationary,                                                
</p>
<p>broadband (i.e., no harmonics or tones).
</p>
<p>‒ Time histories have sufficient duration (i.e., ~1,000 x T).
</p>
<p>• DRT/WDR challenges these assumptions.
</p>
<p>‒ Structure is nonlinear (e.g., Vehicle Stabilizing System and                                       the 
</p>
<p>umbilicals).
</p>
<p>‒ When CT is moving the excitation is not spatially distributed (i.e., comes up from the CT), 
</p>
<p>has a high density of harmonics, and not perfectly stationary.
</p>
<p>‒ When the CT is stopped, wind acting on the ML and SLS integrated vehicle, the primary 
</p>
<p>excitation, which may be nonstationary.  Possible vortex shedding.
</p>
<p>‒ Data duration is limited with respect to how closely spaced the modes are.
</p>

<p>OMA Analysis I
</p>
<p>• Finite Element Model (FEM) was tremendously helpful.
</p>
<p>• Rigorous time-domain and frequency-domain data quality checks 
</p>
<p>identified bad/questionable channels and remove spurious data effects.
</p>
<p>• Proper selection of projection channels is important.
</p>
<p>• Bandpass filtered and decimated test data to only having 
</p>
<p>frequency content in the range of interest is important.
</p>
<p>‒ Careful to exclude filter transients.

<p>OMA Time &amp; Frequency Domain Modal Extraction Techniques 1
</p>
<p>• Multiple OMA techniques needed to identify all target modes.
</p>
<p>• Started with time-domain technique (i.e., SSI-UPCX) then moved to the frequency-domain 
</p>
<p>techniques in the order of Curve-Fit Frequency Domain Decomposition (CFDD), Enhanced 
</p>
<p>Frequency Domain Decomposition (EFDD) and finally Frequency Domain Decomposition 
</p>
<p>(FDD) to verify the modes already identified and to identify modes not already identified.
</p>
<p>– SSI-UPCX could not identify all modes associated with the dominant peaks in the SVD.
</p>
<p>– SSI-UPCX, CFDD, and EFDD techniques estimated mode shapes, modal frequency, and modal 
</p>
<p>damping.
</p>
<p>– FDD can only estimate mode shapes and modal frequency.
</p>

<p>Conclusions 1
</p>
<p>• OMA techniques successfully identified Artemis I DRT and WDR FEM 
</p>
<p>target modes.
</p>
<p>• CT harmonics lie within the frequency range of the fundamental target 
</p>
<p>modes and continue to be a significant challenge when applying OMA 
</p>
<p>techniques to rollout data.
</p>
<p>• The Artemis I DRT and WDR configurations exhibited nonlinear behavior.
</p>
<p>‒ Modal parameters dependent upon CT speed and ground wind speed. 
</p>
<p>• OMA techniques will not replace traditional modal testing.
</p>
<p>‒ Provides important supplemental information and insights into the structural 
</p>
<p>dynamics of hardware in its operational environment.
</p>
<p>• Proper selection of Projection Channels is very important.
</p>
<p>www.nasa.gov 01/29/2024 Operational Modal Analysis of the Artemis I DRT &amp; WDR 22</p>
<p></p>

<p>Conclusions 2
</p>
<p>• Having a FEM, its predicted modal frequencies and mode shapes, and the 
</p>
<p>associated Test Analysis Model (TAM) are tremendously helpful.
</p>
<p>‒ Helps focus the OMA Analysis.
</p>
<p>‒ Allows cross-orthogonalities for judging the similarity between FEM and test shapes.
</p>
<p>‒ Back expansion of test mode shapes to all three translational DOF at each 
</p>
<p>accelerometer location (i.e., TDM grid point) facilitates visual interpretation.
</p>
<p>• Effectiveness of OMA techniques is highly dependent on rollout data 
</p>
<p>quality.
</p>
<p>‒ Time-domain and frequency-domain data quality checks and “correcting” “bad” or 
</p>
<p>“corrupted” acceleration time histories prior to starting an OMA analysis are critical.
</p>
<p>www.nasa.gov 01/29/2024 Operational Modal Analysis of the Artemis I DRT &amp; WDR 23</p>
<p></p>
