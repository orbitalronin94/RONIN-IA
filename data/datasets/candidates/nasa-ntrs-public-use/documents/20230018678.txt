<body><h1>Introduction</h1>
<p></p>
<p>As you read this, humanity’s first interstellar probe has left our solar system and is moving at nearly 17 km/s on its journey through interstellar space. Launched in 1977 and carrying a message from Earth on an inscribed golden disk, Voyager 1 completed its grand tour of the outer planets and has since travelled more than 24 billion km on its outward journey; it’s twin probe, Voyager 2, has also left the heliopause and entered interstellar space, traveling over 20 billion km from Earth. Yet even at these speeds and distances, our intrepid Voyagers have barely moved beyond the influence of our local star. If the distance between our Sun and the closest neighboring star system, Alpha Centauri, were scaled to the size of a meter stick, Voyager 1 would be located just over the ½-mm mark, having traveled 0.06% of the way to the next star (assuming it was pointed in the right direction, which it isn’t). At this rate it will take the probe nearly 75 thousand years to cover the equivalent distance to Alpha Centauri.</p>
<p></p>
<p>Why is it so hard to get there? The distance to the Alpha Centauri triple star system is immense, approximately 40 <i>trillion</i> km  or roughly 4.2 light years (1 light year is the distance light travels in one year). Our fastest spacecraft to date, the Parker Solar Probe, has broken speed records as the fastest human object ever built; in 2025, Parker will be travelling at an astonishing 200 km/s as it hurtles toward another close pass of the Sun. At this speed it could circumnavigate the Earth in less than 4 minutes, but it would still take over 6 millennia to reach our closest interstellar neighbor. To cover this immense distance we clearly need to go incredibly fast, and to go incredibly fast requires a lot of energy. To send an equivalent of the 720-kg Voyager probe on a fast fly-by of the Alpha Centauri system within 40 years (an average researcher’s professional lifetime), the amount of kinetic energy we would need to impart to the probe is around 3.2x1019 J, roughly 30% of the total annual energy consumption of the United States in 2022. </p>
<p></p>
<p>Providing kinetic energy to a spacecraft requires a force to act on the vehicle, which is provided by some form of propulsion; for the Parker Solar Probe and our twin Voyager ambassadors, the kinetic energy was provided by chemical rockets. Additional acceleration was provided using well timed gravity assist maneuvers, which increase the kinetic energy of a passing spacecraft at negligible expense to the orbital kinetic energy of the planet (or Sun). We’ll return to these types of trajectories later in this chapter, but for now let’s assume that the change in velocity provided to the probe comes from an on-board chemical propulsion system. How much propellant would be required to send a Voyager-type probe on a fast flight to the next star?</p>
<p></p>

<h1>Chemical Rockets</h1>
<p></p>
<p>All launch vehicles heading skyward to date have been powered by the chemical energy released when fuels meet oxidizers and chemical bonds are broken and reformed in exothermic reactions. While several fuel and oxidizer combinations are possible, in practice the most energetic reactions result from the use of liquid oxygen (LOX) and hydrogen (H2). Stored in cryogenically cooled tanks, the measured release and ignition of hydrogen and oxygen in a rocket combustion chamber creates enormous pressure that rapidly expels the heated chemical byproduct (primarily water, in the form of steam) to generate enough thrust to overcome the force of gravity. Large launch vehicles require several engines working in unison, often augmented with supplementary solid propellant rocket boosters, to achieve liftoff and attain orbit. Once in space, chemical propellants are commonly used to maintain spacecraft orbits and change flight trajectories. These engines have successfully propelled humans from the surface of the Earth to orbiting space stations and to the surface of the moon, and robotic spacecraft to the farthest reaches of our solar system. Chemical propellants currently remain our only means of lifting large payloads from the surface of the Earth and into space, and they have been the mainstay of rocketry from its earliest days. </p>
<p></p>
<p>So why isn’t chemical propulsion used to send humans or our robotic emissaries on fast interstellar journeys? The answer resides in the limited amount of energy available in chemical bonds. As noted, the most energetic chemical process created in current rocket propellants involves the controlled combustion of oxygen and hydrogen. The amount of energy released per unit mass during the combustion process is approximately 1.35x107 J/kg. Assuming all this energy is converted to kinetic energy, the maximum exhaust velocity imparted to 1-kg of propellant is approximately 5200 m/s, corresponding to an Isp of approximately 530 s. Due to engine inefficiencies the actual Isp for a liquid oxygen-hydrogen engine is closer to 450 s, and lower for other propellant combinations.</p>
<p></p>
<p>Assuming we want to send a probe on a 40-year trip to the nearest star, the required change in velocity imparted to the spacecraft would be approximately 3x107 m/s (approximately 10% the speed of light; 0.1 c).[footnoteRef:1] Using Eq. 2, achieving this change in velocity with a state-of-the-art chemical engine Isp of 450 s drives the final to initial spacecraft mass fraction to zero, requiring a nearly infinite amount of propellant to make the trip. If we instead impose a mass fraction of 10-9 (where just one billionth of the initial launch mass reaches the target star system), the achievable change in velocity is approximately 9.3x104 m/s for a total trip time of nearly 13,700 years. A 1-kg probe would require 109 kg of propellant to make even this long duration voyage, which for a Voyager-like probe would require millions of SLS or SpaceX Starship launches just to amass enough propellant in orbit to make the trip. Clearly the use of chemical propellants is unviable for any reasonable excursion to the stars. Even the use of more exotic atomic, gelled, and metallic propellants[endnoteRef:2],[endnoteRef:3] won’t change the disappointing calculus of the impossibly large amounts of chemical propellant needed to achieve less than millennia interstellar trip times. Increasing specific impulse using these advanced propellants can significantly improve the mass fraction for flights to orbit or within the solar system but provides negligible improvement to the incredibly long trip times and near-infinite propellant requirements associated with chemically powered journeys between the stars. </p>
<p></p>
<h1>Getting an Assist from Gravity</h1>
<p></p>
<p>As a spacecraft approaches a planetary body it’s speed increases due to gravitational attraction and decreases again as it climbs back out of the planet’s gravity well. When a spacecraft approaches the planet in the same orbital direction (relative to the Sun) and departs in the opposite direction, it can increase its velocity at the expense of the planet’s orbital kinetic energy; approaching the planet in a retrograde direction decreases the spacecraft velocity and adds kinetic energy to the planet’s orbital motion. In each case kinetic energy is conserved, and the change in the planet’s orbital energy is negligible; however, significant changes in spacecraft velocities and trajectories can be achieved without the additional use of propellant. Such passive gravity assists, first theorized in the early 1920’s, have been used by the Parker Solar Probe, the Voyagers, and multiple spacecraft that came before them. At launch, the Parker probe was traveling at approximately 17.6 km/s before performing gravity assist maneuvers at Venus, which positioned it to fall rapidly toward the Sun. Following the latest gravity assist, the probe is expected to reach nearly 200 km/s as it slingshots around the Sun in 2025. However, Parker will also lose a significant amount of this energy as it climbs back out of the deep solar gravity well, reducing its velocity by roughly an order of magnitude as it heads out toward yet another encounter with Venus. The New Horizons Probe that flew past Pluto in 2015 and is now exploring the Kuiper Belt used a similar Jupiter gravity assist to increase its speed by 4 km/s, clocking over 23 km/s on its path toward Pluto. Such passive (non-propulsive) assists can save significant propellant mass over the life of a mission and, in the case of the Voyagers, provide a well-choreographed tour of the giant planets before a final gravity assist leading out of the solar system. </p>
<p></p>
<p>While passive gravity assists have been used to great effect, as early as 1929 rocket pioneer Hermann Oberth also envisioned the use of propulsive gravity assists to increase spacecraft speeds passing near planets or the Sun.[endnoteRef:4] Rather than simply swinging around the Sun or planet, a powered gravity assist fires the spacecraft engine at periapsis to add additional kinetic energy to the spacecraft. Because the spacecraft is already traveling at high speed, the additional increase in speed generates more kinetic energy than could be attained by the same maneuver far from the Sun. For example, a 100-kg spacecraft traveling at 1 m/s has an initial kinetic energy of 50-J; firing its engine to add 1 m/s will increase its kinetic energy by 150-J. The same spacecraft traveling at 100 m/s and firing its engine to add 1 m/s will increase its kinetic energy by 1000-J.  As Oberth showed, the energy gained by this propulsive maneuver close to the Sun, when the spacecraft speed is at a maximum, can be significantly higher than attained by passive gravity assists, at a small expenditure of propellant. [4:  Blanco, Philip &amp; Mungan, Carl. (2019). Rocket Propulsion, Classical Relativity, and the Oberth Effect. The Physics Teacher, Vol. 57, pp. 439-441; 10.1119/1.5126818.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>The caveat is the spacecraft must travel in close proximity to the Sun, typically within a few solar radii for maximum effect, which introduces significant thermal issues. In 2018 the Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory (JHU/APL) initiated a NASA-funded study to evaluate the feasibility of performing an interstellar mission that could be launched by 2030 using existing or soon to be matured technologies.[endnoteRef:5] Through a series of workshops and reports, this large, international team of experts investigated the mission designs, technologies, and science opportunities delivered by an interstellar probe that could operate for 50 years and travel to 1000 AU (1000 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, or around 1.5x1011 km). While not an attempt to reach the next star, this distance is significantly further than the current Voyager locations, and the probe would be specifically designed to measure the heliosphere, the circumsolar dust cloud, the local interstellar medium, and targets of opportunity within the Kuiper Belt. The study evaluated multiple mission scenarios using a notional probe based on the New Horizons spacecraft; several launch vehicle and upper stage combinations were studied, which either alone or together with gravity assists would satisfy the mission design guidelines. In addition to passive Jupiter gravity assists, the team studied Oberth maneuvers at Jupiter and at the Sun to increase the probe velocity. Using launch vehicle and upper stage configurations available in the 2030-time frame and trading distances of closest approach at periapsis, a passive Jupiter gravity assist could reasonably accelerate the probe to a speed of 7 AU/yr (33 km/s) while a powered Jupiter gravity assist could increase this speed to 9 AU/yr (43 km/s). Both values are lower than the approximately 20 AU/yr required to get the probe to 1000 AU in 50 years, leading the study team to perform an extensive analysis of solar Oberth maneuvers. An estimate of the minimum ∆V required to provide an escape velocity of 20 km/s can be obtained by setting perihelion equal to one solar radius, yielding a minimum propulsive ∆V requirement of 7.3 km/s. The thermal challenges of skimming through the solar photosphere are of course immense, and the spacecraft will also need to carry a thermal shield whose mass will increase as the perihelion distance decreases. <p></p>
<p>Optimizing the perihelion distance to maximize escape velocity while minimizing thermal shield and propellant mass is a trade with myriad variables and presents a daunting challenge for mission designers. However, recasting the harsh solar environment as a potential benefit for a close Oberth maneuver, a 2022 study led by Benkoski[endnoteRef:6] investigated the design of a solar thermal propulsion system in which the thermal shield used to protect the spacecraft is also used to heat the propellant to a high exhaust velocity, while simultaneously cooling the shield below its melting temperature. The study evaluated a solar thermal propulsion system operated with hydrogen, lithium, lithium hydride, and ammonia propellants at perihelion distances ranging from 3 to 10 solar radii and total spacecraft masses of 1000-kg to 5000-kg. Maximum surface temperatures were limited to 2700K, and both passive emissivity coatings and active propellant cooling through channels in the shield/heat exchanger were employed. The shield design was modeled after the Parker Solar Probe heat shield, with carbon composite panels layered over a carbon foam insulator. Detailed analysis of this multivariate problem identified curves of escape velocity as a function of perihelion distance for the various propellants, with the highest escape velocity of 12.3 AU/year achieved using lithium hydride propellant at a perihelion of 4.5 Rs due to the less complex propellant storage and handling systems and relatively low propellant molar mass. While a significant improvement over the prior estimates of 9 AU/year achievable with a solar Oberth maneuver, this velocity is still too low to reach significantly beyond our stellar neighborhood.  [6:  Benkoski, J., “Combined Heat Shield and Solar Thermal Propulsion System for an Oberth Maneuver,” Phase I Final Report, NASA Institute for Advanced Concepts, February 22, 2023.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>As previously noted, a 40-year mission to Alpha Centauri would require traveling at 10% light speed. Although such velocities are unattainable using passive or powered gravity assists within our solar system, concepts have been proposed in which more exotic celestial objects could anchor the relativistic Oberth maneuvers needed for fast interstellar travel. A detailed examination of one such scenario is included in the JHU/APL Interstellar Study report, which discussed the idealized case of a probe falling from rest toward an uncharged, non-spinning black hole.[endnoteRef:7] Using twice the Schwarzschild radius of the black hole as the distance of closest approach, the authors evaluated how an Oberth maneuver would be modified in a strong gravitational field. As it reaches this distance of closest approach the probe is already travelling at 70.7% the speed of light; without an Oberth maneuver the probe would slingshot around the black hole and recede back toward its initial zero velocity state far from the black hole. Instead, by firing its engine to execute an Oberth maneuver at the point of closest approach, the probe can attain a residual asymptotic velocity approaching 2000 km/s as it hurtles back toward interstellar space. While impressively higher than the current Voyager speeds of 17 km/s, it would however still take the probe over 630 years to cover the distance between the Sun and its nearest neighbor. To achieve truly relativistic speeds, Dyson[endnoteRef:8] analyzed a powered flyby of a binary white dwarf star system, later modified to a rapidly orbiting binary neutron star system. Tapping the rotational energy of the white dwarf pair could yield asymptotic velocities approaching 1% the speed of light, while the tighter, faster orbital motion of the neutron star system could generate speeds up to 27% the speed of light (81,000 km/s). At this speed the distance between our star and its neighbor could be covered in a little over 15 years.  Recent modifications to the “Dyson slingshot” concept include the use of a binary black hole system[endnoteRef:9] in which a light beam sent by a spacecraft a safe distance away from the system would gain energy by travelling around one of the orbiting binary black holes, blue shifting toward more energetic photons that would then provide momentum to the craft that sent the beam. The spacecraft gains energy with each pass of the beam through the system, eventually reaching a terminal velocity approaching 133% of the black hole orbital velocity; for rapidly orbiting black hole pairs, the final spacecraft velocity can be highly relativistic. [7:  McNutt, R. et al., Interstellar Probe Study 2019 Report, Appendices: Background and Beginnings, Oberth Maneuver, and Escaping the Solar System, NASA Contract NNN06AA01C, Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics laboratory, 2019.]  [8:  Dyson, F. J., Gravitational Machines, in Interstellar Communication (A.G.W. Cameron, ed.), W.A. Benjamin, New York, 1963, pp. 115–120. Available at: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.10470.pdf ]  [9:  Kipping, D., “The Halo Drive: Fuel-Free Relativistic Propulsion of Large Masses via Recycled Boomerang Photons,” arXiv:1903.03423 [gr-qc], 2019.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>While fascinating for the physics they uncover, the lack of a convenient binary neutron star or black hole pair make such relativistic Oberth maneuvers well beyond our current capabilities. With the comparatively limited performance provided by chemical engines and powered solar gravity assists, it appears that Oberth maneuvers within our solar system won’t provide the requisite speeds for trips much beyond our local star. To go faster, we clearly need more energy.</p>
<p></p>
<h1>Nuclear Fission Propulsion Systems</h1>
<p></p>
<p>As noted earlier, the energy released in the chemical reactions that power state of the art LOX-H2 engines is around 1.35x107 J/kg, sufficient to provide the high thrust to weight values required to launch and send spacecraft on trajectories throughout the solar system. However, given the limited Isp of these engines, the mass fraction rapidly drops to zero for an interstellar mission. Increasing the exhaust velocity requires increasing the propellant energy, and nuclear reactions provide orders of magnitude more energy per unit mass compared to chemical propellants. If a 1-kg mass of 235U (a fissile isotope of uranium) underwent complete fission, the energy released would be 8x1013 J, nearly 6 million times the energy density contained in LOX-H2 propellants. In reality only a fraction of the uranium will undergo fission, but even so the amount of energy released is significantly greater than the energy available through chemical combustion. Is this energy sufficient to propel a spacecraft on a rapid interstellar journey?</p>
<p></p>
<h2>Fission Process</h2>
<p></p>
<p>When a neutron is captured by a fissile atom it causes the atom to split (fission) into daughter products that separate at high velocity, producing additional neutrons in the process. Nearby fissile nuclei can in turn absorb the additional neutrons, subsequently splitting into energetic daughter products and additional neutrons, etc., leading to a significant release of energy. These reactions are controlled in a properly designed chamber (reactor), providing a robust thermal source to heat propellants to higher kinetic energies than can be achieved with chemical rockets. </p>
<p></p>
<p><a name="_Hlk146896540"></a><a name="_Hlk146899022"></a>The most common fission reactions involve the use of 235U, a fissile isotope that makes up 0.72% of natural uranium. To be useful as a power source natural uranium is processed (enriched) to increase the fraction of fissile isotopes; uranium blends composed of less than 20% 235U are referred to as low enriched uranium (LEU), while higher ratios of fissile to natural uranium are deemed highly enriched uranium (HEU). Commercial power reactors are operated with low enriched fuel, typically 3%-5%, while weapon-grade blends contain greater than 85% 235U.  Prior designs for space-based fission reactors for power or propulsion typically used highly enriched uranium to minimize reactor mass and volume; more recent designs are moving toward the use of high-assay low enriched uranium (HALEU) with up to 20% 235U, which mitigates the proliferation risk of weapons grade uranium but still maintains reasonable reactor sizes for in-space applications. Fission reactors are discussed in more detail in a separate chapter by Bennett; below we focus on the applications of nuclear fission for space propulsion, and it's potential for powering interstellar travel.</p>
<p></p>

<p></p>

The fission fragments are formed in very short-lived excited states which rapidly decay through the release of gamma and beta radiation, and in some decay processes the delayed emission of additional neutrons, into stable nuclei. The asymmetric distribution of product masses is most evident for fission induced by thermal (low energy, sub-eV) neutrons, becoming less so as the incident neutron energy increases. </p>

<p>Except for the energy carried away by the non-interacting neutrinos, the energy contained in the fission fragments and remaining fission products is recoverable, making nuclear fission an efficient energy source for terrestrial and space applications. Various options for using this energy for spacecraft propulsion are briefly discussed below. </p>
<p></p>
<h2>Nuclear Thermal Propulsion</h2>
<p></p>
<p>Perhaps the most straightforward application of nuclear fission for propulsion is to absorb the energy of the reaction products to heat a material, then transfer that heat to a propellant to generate thrust. This is the basic principle of nuclear thermal propulsion (NTP), which channels low molecular weight propellants (typically hydrogen) through a hot fission reactor to raise the propellant temperature before expelling it through a nozzle to provide thrust (Fig 2). </p>
<p></p>
<p><img src="embedded:image3.png" alt=""></p>
<p class="caption">Figure 2. Nuclear Thermal Propulsion (NTP) Engine Assembly (NASA Glenn Research Center)</p>
<p>Nuclear thermal engines have a long development history in both the United States and former Soviet Union, an engaging history of which is provided by Dewar.[endnoteRef:13] Both the US (Rover/NERVA) and USSR (RD-0410) programs ended with successful ground demonstrations in the 1970s and early 1980s, respectively, before succumbing to budget pressures and changing priorities. However, significant advancements were made under both programs, and numerous design studies have since been conducted to improve the performance demonstrated by these earlier engines. NASA, the U.S. Department of Energy, the Defense Advanced Concepts Research Agency (DARPA), and industry partners are developing the nuclear Demonstration Rocket for Agile Cislunar Operations (DRACO), anticipated to launch into high Earth orbit by 2027,[endnoteRef:14] and programs are underway to design and develop NTP engines as part of a robust architecture for crewed Mars exploration.[endnoteRef:15] [13:  Dewar, J., To the End of the Solar System, The University Press of Kentucky, 2004.]  [14:  “Nonreimbursable Interagency Agreement Between the National Aeronautic sand Space Administration and Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency for Demonstration Rocket for Agile Cislunar Operations Program” darpa-nasa_draco_nonreimbursable_interagency_agreement_final_2023-01-11_.pdf, 2023.]  [15:  Space Nuclear Propulsion - NASA] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>NTP reactors incorporate HEU or HALEU uranium fuel rods, neutron moderators to reduce the energy of fission neutrons and increase their probability of inducing another fission event, reflectors to scatter neutrons back into the reactor core to minimize neutron loss and the amount of fissile fuel needed, and neutron absorbers that capture neutrons to help control the reaction process. Flow channels embedded within the core allow the flow of a low molecular weight propellant (typically hydrogen), which cools the reactor while raising the propellant temperature; the heated propellant is then exhausted through a converging-diverging expansion nozzle. The amount of heat that can be transferred to the propellant is ultimately limited by the material properties of the reactor and flow channels, which will melt above a certain temperature. Refractory metals such as molybdenum and tungsten are used in reactor designs to maximize material temperatures, reaching operating temperatures on the order of 3000K. <p>NTP engines developed and tested under the NERVA program ran at hydrogen mass flow rates exceeding 30 kg/s, achieving thrust values above 250 kN. The combination of high thrust and high specific impulse can provide significant benefits for deep space human and robotic missions over state-of-the-art chemical engines in terms of propellant savings and reduced trip times. However, even a factor of two increase in specific impulse won’t noticeably improve the vehicle mass fraction for a trip to the stars, as it still requires a near-infinite amount of propellant to make the trip in decades vs millennia. </p>
<p></p>
<h2>Advanced Fission Propulsion Concepts</h2>
<p></p>
<p>The specific impulse achievable with nuclear thermal propulsion is constrained by material thermal properties; above a certain temperature material components will soften and melt, limiting the amount of heat that can be transferred to the propellant. The performance of nuclear fission systems could potentially be improved by removing these material constraints, which has led to several concepts for advanced fission propulsion concepts. While not an exhaustive list, several representative concepts are briefly discussed below.</p>
<p></p>
<h3>Liquid core nuclear engine</h3>
<p></p>
<p>The thermal constraints limiting solid core NTP engine performance can be relaxed if the uranium fuel is instead kept in a high temperature liquid state. Extensively studied in the early days of nuclear propulsion,[endnoteRef:17] these liquid core reactor concepts are again being evaluated as alternatives to solid core systems. In most liquid core designs the high temperature liquid fuel is spun to compress it in a thin layer against the inner surface of a porous moderator cylinder. In bubble-through reactors, hydrogen gas is diffused through the cylinder and bubbles through the fuel layer, raising the gas temperature to the temperature of the liquid core before being expelled through the engine nozzle. An alternative design flows hydrogen propellant down the center of rotating liquid fuel tubes, transferring thermal energy to the propellant through radiative heating and surface convection, and other variations use the continuous cycling of liquid fuel droplets within the core to heat the hydrogen. Modern centrifugal reactor designs[endnoteRef:18] flow the hydrogen propellant through the moderators and other engine structures to maintain material temperatures below 800K, then diffuse the gas radially inward through the porous cylinder walls and into the liquid uranium fuel. The liquid uranium near the inner cylinder wall is maintained near 1500K by the propellant inflow, while the temperature near the center of the rotating cylinder can reach as high as 5500K. The high temperature liquid uranium layer only contacts the propellant, keeping the material structures well below their melting temperatures. From Eqn. 2, these temperatures can provide engine specific impulse values approaching 1800 s for dissociated hydrogen, a significant improvement over chemical and solid core nuclear thermal engines, but still insufficient for an interstellar mission.  [17:  Barrett Jr., W. L., “Liquid Core Nuclear Rocket,” AIAA 64-541, AIAA Aerospace Propulsion Meeting, May 4-6, 1963, Cleveland, OH.]  [18:  Thomas, D., Houts, M., Walters, W., Hollingsworth, K., Frederick, R., and Cassibry, J., “Establishing the Feasibility of the Centrifugal Nuclear Thermal Rocket,” 10.2514/6.2021-3604, AIAA Propulsion and Energy Forum, August 9-11, 2021 (Virtual Event).] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h3>Gas Core Nuclear Engines</h3>
<p></p>
<p>To achieve even higher propellant temperatures, concepts have been proposed that move from solid and liquid uranium cores to high temperature uranium gas (plasma) cores, which can achieve order of magnitude increases in propellant temperature.[endnoteRef:19],[endnoteRef:20] In a gas core reactor, the uranium plasma is maintained at sufficiently high pressures and densities to keep the neutron and fission particle mean free paths within the fuel volume, maintaining a high gas core temperature and fission criticality. In an open cycle gas core reactor, hydrogen propellant seeded with microscopic particles of graphite or tungsten is injected through a porous wall and flows around the core of uranium gas to create a relatively stable, hydrodynamically confined central fuel region. As the seeded propellant flows around the gas core it is heated through thermal radiation, with the seed particles enhancing the radiative absorption and reducing the radiant heat flux to the pressure chamber walls. The achievable temperatures in open cycle gas core reactors can reach 104 – 105 K; transferring this heat to the hydrogen propellant can provide specific impulse values approaching 7000 seconds with thrust values of several 100-kN. A significant challenge with this engine is the continual loss of uranium gas through entrainment with the propellant flow, which requires a continual replenishment of uranium into the system. A closed-cycle version of the gas core reactor, often called the nuclear light bulb, confines the uranium gas in transparent containers, transmitting the radiant energy through the container walls to the seeded hydrogen propellant flow. An injected buffer gas flows in a vortex pattern around the high temperature uranium to keep it away from the transparent material walls, which must still be actively cooled to remove absorbed radiant heat from the uranium plasma. Preliminary designs of closed cycle systems estimated specific impulse values up to 3000 s, not quite matching the performance of the open cycle gas core systems. [19:  Kammash, T. and Galbraith, D. “Fuel Confinement and Stability in the Gas Core Nuclear Propulsion Concept,” AlAA 92-3010, AIAA/SAE/ASME/ASEE 28th Joint Propulsion Conference and Exhibit, July 6-8, 1992, Nashville, TN.]  [20:  Emrich, W., Principles of Nuclear Rocket Propulsion, Elsevier, 2016, pp. 273-304.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>While these next-step fission core engines offer significant performance improvements over solid and liquid core designs, even several thousand seconds of specific impulse isn’t adequate for a realistic transit to the next star.  A limiting factor with each of the nuclear concepts considered so far is that the fission energy is transferred to a propellant, which is then exhausted to provide thrust; what if the energetic fission fragments could instead be directly used for thrust?</p>
<p></p>
<h3>Fission Fragment Engines</h3>
<p></p>
<p>In 1988 Chapline et al.[endnoteRef:21] proposed the direct use of reactor fission fragments for propulsion, envisioning the high specific impulse (approaching 106 s) produced by these engines could enable trip times to Alpha Centauri on the order of a century, as well as high speed journeys across the solar system. The conceptual design consists of fissile material coated onto thin fibers which are in turn grouped into thin layers in a reactor core. Current carrying elements within a surrounding moderator material create magnetic fields between the layers, which insulates the moderator and guides the fission fragments out of the core. The separation distance between the layers is determined by the escape probability of the fragments and the average density of material within the core, typically resulting in a few to several centimeter layer separations. Neutron reflectors are used to minimize the fuel requirements and reduce the core size to a diameter less than a few meters. The magnetic field strengths required to guide the fission fragments are found to be modest, on the order of a Tesla or less. Alternate fission fuels were evaluated to maximize engine performance, including highly fissile metastable 242Am and 245Cm, which both require less critical mass for sustained reactions than 235U.  [21:  Chapline, G., Dickson, P. and Schnitzler, B., “Fission Fragment Rockets – A Potential Breakthrough,” OSTI  6868318, International Reactor Physics Conference, September 18, 1988, Jackson Hole, WY.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p><img src="embedded:image4.png" alt=""><img src="embedded:image5.emf" alt=""><p>(b)</p>
<p>(a)</p>
</p>
<p class="caption">Figure 3. (a) Original fission fragment engine design and (b) core design showing conductors for magnetic fields, fuel fibers and reflectors. [Chapline ref]</p>
<p><a name="_Hlk147140993"></a>Heat rejection in the fission fragment engine design is enhanced due to the small diameter of the fuel wires, which corresponds to a large fuel surface area to help radiate away heat. Assuming 3-μm diameter carbon fibers coated with 0.4-μm thick americium fuel, Chapline calculates that 103 kg of fuel wires will have a surface area of 2x105 m2, sufficient to radiate 20-GW of power at 1100k. However, the fuel wires within the active core region will see very high temperatures, requiring them to be quickly circulated through the core to prevent them from melting. To do this the fuel wires are strung out on a large diameter wheel that rotates the fuel layers through the core at a velocity of around 1 km/sec. This in turn limits the wheel diameter to ensure the wire tensile strength is not exceeded, resulting in wheel diameters on the order of 200 m. In addition, some fraction of the reactor energy will be absorbed in the moderator material, which will require active cooling. </p>
<p></p>
<p><a name="_Hlk147142964"></a>To demonstrate the potential performance of the engine, Chapline calculates that for an americium fueled rocket operating at 10-GW, and an escape probability of 50% for the fission fragments, that 6 tonnes of mass can be delivered to Alpha Centauri on a fly-by trajectory in about 100 years; 15 tonnes could be delivered in 121 years, and 30 tonnes in 148 years. Increasing the fission fragment escape fraction to 70% would send 10 tonnes in 87 years, and up to 30 tonnes in 113 years. </p>
<p></p>
<p>A variation on the fission fragment concept developed by Chapline was proposed by Clark and Sheldon[endnoteRef:22] in which the fuel consists of nanoparticle dust (&lt; 100 nm diameter) composed of fissile material. The fuel particles and the fission fragments in the reactor core form a dusty plasma cloud. The dusty plasma fuel elements are electrostatically or magnetically confined within the core, while the higher mass, high velocity reaction products are collimated and channeled out of the core using a magnetic field. Using various fissile isotopes, the dusty fission fragment engine could similarly achieve specific impulse values on the order of 106 s. Various mission scenarios were developed to evaluate the potential performance of the engine; for a 10-year mission to the solar gravity lens (550 AU), the required velocity change is about 2% the speed of light. For a rocket Isp of 1.5x106 s, the amount of fuel required is about 3% of the total rocket mass. The authors calculate that a 10-year mission to the solar gravitational lens would require approximately 180 kg of nuclear fuel, corresponding to a 350 MW reactor power; a 30-year trip to the Oort cloud ½-light year away would require a 5.6 GW reactor, and a 50-year trip to Alpha Centauri would require a 208 GW reactor consuming 240 tonnes of fission fuel.  [22:  Clark, R. Sheldon, R., “Dusty Plasma Based Fission Fragment Nuclear Reactor,” 41st AIAA/ASME/SAE/ASEE Joint Propulsion Conference &amp; Exhibit, July 10-13, 2005, Tucson, AZ] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>Clearly a significant amount of analysis remains to be performed to better evaluate the feasibility of fission fragment rockets for interstellar missions, and the initial performance estimates outlined above are no doubt highly optimistic. In addition to the reactor and engine design challenges, considerable infrastructure would be required to generate and handle the very large quantities of highly radioactive materials required for these systems. The need to electromagnetically channel the fission fragments out of the engine may limit the space charge density that can be achieved in these systems, which in turn may limit the achievable thrust.[endnoteRef:23] Nevertheless, the direct exhaust of highly energetic fission fragments may offer a viable path for high velocity missions, if not to the next star, then perhaps within the solar system and our local interstellar environs. [23:  Frisbee. R., “Limits of Interstellar Flight Technology”, Chapter 2, Frontiers of Propulsion Physics, ed. M. Millis and E. Davis, Progress in Aeronautics and Astronautics, Vol 227, American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics, Inc., 2009, pp. 67-69.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h3>Nuclear Pulse Engines</h3>
<p></p>
<p>We round out this section with one of the more dramatic uses of nuclear fission for propulsion, in the form of ejected nuclear explosives that detonate behind the spacecraft to push the vehicle forward. The most detailed version of this concept was Project Orion,[endnoteRef:24],[endnoteRef:25] based on concepts originally devised in the 1940s and refined into the 1960s. In a pulsed nuclear engine, the nuclear device is jettisoned aft and the resulting explosion produces prompt x-rays followed by the blast of an expanding plasma fireball that consists of high temperature ionized gas and debris, which impinge on a pusher plate attached to the vehicle. The x-rays ablate a layer of the plate’s surface, forming a shock wave that propagates into the plate and rebounds back to the plate surface, adding high velocity spall material to the ablated material leaving the plate to provide thrust. The blast wave impacting the plate provides additional momentum to the vehicle, with the transient pulse smoothed out by a spring-like shock absorber. Following the blast the plate returns to its initial position in time to absorb the next impulse. The explosives are ejected and detonated at a frequency causing harmonic oscillation of the pusher plate to minimize dissipative losses and maximize the coupling between the plate and vehicle. The interaction between the plate material and plasma blast occurs in less than 10-4 s, minimizing thermal transport and heating of the plate. Magnetic shielding may also be used to help reflect charged plasma particles and improve plate lifetimes at very high explosive yields. Variations on the pusher plate design include internal pulse designs, in which the detonation occurs within a pressure chamber to harness more of the explosive energy to heat and expel a hydrogen propellant; however, the internal pulse approach is more massive than the external plate approach and suffers from additional radiation heating of the chamber walls.  [24:  Emrich, W., Principles of Nuclear Rocket Propulsion, Elsevier, 2016, pp. 259-272.]  [25:  Schmidt, G., Bonometti, J., and Irvine, C., “Project Orion and Future Prospects for Nuclear Pulse Propulsion,” J. Propulsion and Power, Vol. 18, No. 3, May–June 2002, pp. 497-504.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>Originally considered as a launch vehicle to low earth orbit, the Orion vehicle would have an initial mass of 10,000 tonnes and initially operate with 0.1-kton yield pulse units ejected once per second, increasing to 20-kton yield explosives every 10 seconds as the vehicle accelerated. Recognizing the potential issues with repetitive nuclear detonations to power a launch vehicle, the designs moved toward in-space applications for rapid planetary missions. Various vehicle concepts were developed to provide a range of thrust and Isp values, with typical values of 10-20x106 N and 3000-4000 s, respectively. For interstellar missions the explosive yield was raised from sub-kton to megaton values, and the vehicle size increased dramatically to a total mass of 4x105 tonnes with a dry mass of 1x105 tonnes (mass fraction of 25%), carrying 300,000 1-MT nuclear devices. The devices were detonated at a rate of 1 every 2.9 seconds, providing an acceleration of 1.2-g to reach a velocity of 105 km/s (3.3% light speed) in just 10 days. At this speed a trip to Alpha Centauri would take approximately 130 years, carrying a significant scientific payload.[endnoteRef:26] While this version of Orion was meant primarily as an existence proof that such trips were possible, the engineering requirements are clearly staggering as are the political ramifications regarding the repetitive detonation of megaton nuclear devices in space. Development of the Orion concept effectively ended in the 1960s with the signing of the nuclear test ban treaty that prohibited nuclear explosions above ground and in-space, but not before the concept was demonstrated on a small scale using chemical explosives to propel a small vehicle over 100 m into the air off the coast of California.[endnoteRef:27] Although restricted by current treaties, modern versions of Orion that employ smaller nuclear fusion devices continue to receive serious attention for interstellar travel, and are discussed later in this chapter. [26:  Frisbee. R., “Limits of Interstellar Flight Technology”, in Frontiers of Propulsion Physics, ed. M. Millis and E. Davis, Progress in Aeronautics and Astronautics, Vol 227, American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics, Inc., 2009, pp. 66-67.]  [27:  Propulsion Test Vehicle, Project Orion | National Air and Space Museum] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h2>Nuclear Electric Propulsion</h2>
<p></p>
<p>In addition to using reactors to heat a propellant or to use the fission byproducts directly for thrust, fission reactors can also be used to generate power for electric propulsion systems. A variety of solar powered thrusters have been used for decades for commercial satellite applications and inner solar system robotic science missions; nuclear powered electric propulsion systems are also being designed for missions farther out into the solar system.[endnoteRef:28]  Electric (plasma) thrusters generate higher specific impulse but significantly lower thrust than chemical and nuclear thermal engines. Ion thrusters ionize a heavy ion propellant such as xenon and accelerate the ions through high potential electrostatic grids to generate specific impulse values between 2000 s and 10,000 s. Hall-effect thrusters use magnetically confined electrons near the thruster exit plane in place of electrostatic grids to set up a potential within the thruster that accelerates the ions; these devices achieve slightly lower values of specific impulse but mitigate wear issues associated with material accelerator grids. Thrust levels for both types of devices are well below 1-N for the kW-class devices flown to date, but 100-kW class laboratory models have achieved up to a few N of thrust.[endnoteRef:29] Magnetoplasmadynamic (MPD) thrusters use electromagnetic fields to accelerate a plasma using Lorentz forces; a strong radial current driven between coaxial electrodes ionizes a low molecular weight gas, and the return current through the center electrode creates an azimuthal magnetic field that, in combination with the radial current, axially accelerates the plasma out of the thruster. Additional applied magnetic fields can help stabilize the plasma and allow operation at higher currents, with laboratory devices operating at steady-state power levels of above 250-kW.[endnoteRef:30] At MW-class power levels MPD thrusters appear capable of generating several N of thrust with Isp values approaching 10,000 s using hydrogen and lithium propellants. Other high power propulsion engines rely on radiofrequency heating and acceleration; the VASIMR engine[endnoteRef:31] employs helicon waves to ionize a propellant followed by ion cyclotron heating of the plasma to high temperature before exhausting through a magnetic nozzle. Thrust values of several N at specific impulses up to 10,000 s may be achievable, depending on propellant type and mission application.  [28:  Cassidy, J. et al., “Recent advances in nuclear powered electric propulsion for space exploration,” Energy Conversion and Management, Vol. 49, Issue 3, March 2008, pp. 412-435.]  [29:  Mason, L., Oleson, S., Jacobson, D., Schmitz, P., Qualls, L., Smith, M., Ade, B., and Navarro, J., “Nuclear Power Concepts and Development Strategies for High-Power Electric Propulsion Missions to Mars,” Nuclear Technology, 208:sup1, S52-S66, DOI: 10.1080/00295450.2022.2045180.]  [30:  LaPointe, M., Strzempkowski, E., and Pencil. E., “High Power MPD Thruster Performance Measurements,” NASA/TM—2004-213226, September 2004.]  [31:  Giambusso, M., Chang Díaz, F., Corrigan, A., Dean, L., Warrayat, M., and Stoehr, M., “Steady-state Testing in the VASIMR VX-200SS Project,” AIAA 2021-4150, ASCEND 2021, November 15-17, 2021, Las Vegas, Nevada] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>The challenge with using nuclear electric propulsion for interstellar travel again lies in the enormous amount of propellant that must be carried to reach <i>interstellar</i> mission velocities.  While Isp values of 10,000 s provide significant advantages for deep space robotic exploration and for efficiently pushing large amounts of mass around the solar system, the achievable specific impulse is still well below the requirement for reasonable interstellar travel times, where mission velocities are fractions of the speed of light.</p>
<p></p>
<h2>Summary: Fission Propulsion for an Interstellar Mission</h2>
<p></p>
<p>Fission propulsion can offer significantly improved performance over chemical engines for planetary and deep space missions, although even these energetic engines are limited in capability for rapid interstellar flight. Transferring the fission energy to a propellant generates significant thrust but limits the specific impulse; directly using the fission byproducts provides high specific impulse, but limited thrust and acceleration. Of the fission concepts discussed in this section, those most suitable for interstellar travel appear to be those that use the fission products directly, such as the fission fragment engine and the pulsed fission engines embodied in the Orion concept. As noted, both types of engines face enormous engineering and, in the case of Orion, societal challenges; they do however serve as existence proofs that such extraordinary missions may be possible, even if currently far from practical.</p>
<h1>Nuclear Fusion Propulsion</h1>
<p></p>
<p>Nuclear fusion is the energy source that powers the Sun and the stars we wish to reach, releasing over 25 million times the amount of energy per unit mass available in chemical combustion and several times the energy released in nuclear fission. While the quest continues to control fusion for terrestrial power applications, several studies have investigated designs that, once fusion is achieved, could be used for spacecraft propulsion and power. A detailed discussion doing justice to all the proposed concepts is well beyond the scope of this chapter, and the reader is referred to the literature to more fully investigate the variety of innovative concepts proposed to harness these highly energetic reactions for propulsion. Instead, we provide a brief discussion on the potential benefits of fusion energy for propulsion, outline various types of fusion systems that have been proposed for in-space propulsion applications, and summarize their potential applicability for an interstellar mission.</p>
<p></p>

<h2>Approaches to Fusion Propulsion</h2>
<p></p>
<p>A brief review of the approaches used in the worldwide quest to harness fusion power is provided below, together with a short description of several types of fusion propulsion concepts based on their terrestrial counterparts. A succinct history of fusion propulsion is provided by Cassibry et al.[endnoteRef:36], and following Cassibry, the various engine concepts are categorized below in terms of the approaches used to confine and heat the plasma. [36:  Cassibry, J., Cortez, R., Stanic, M., Seidler, W., Adams, R., Statham, G., and Fabisinski, L., “The Case and Development Path for Fusion Propulsion,” J. <p></p>
<h3>Magnetic Confinement Fusion (MCF)</h3>
<p></p>
<p>One of the earliest confinement methods studied, MCF uses strong magnetic fields to contain the high temperature ionized plasma; design variations include tokomaks,[endnoteRef:37] spheromaks,[endnoteRef:38] stellerators,[endnoteRef:39] gas dynamic mirrors,[endnoteRef:40] reverse field plasma devices,[endnoteRef:41] and other devices that confine the plasma in a sufficiently large volume for the reaction products to transfer their energy via collisions to maintain the requisite fusion reaction conditions. Auxiliary heating may be provided by external (typically radiofrequency) power sources that inject additional energy into the plasma, and plasma losses are replenished through either continual or intermittent injection of the constituent fusion fuels. Several MCF reactors performing basic fusion research are in operation around the world, and an international consortium is building the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor (ITER)[endnoteRef:42] to study the reactor systems and materials required to generate net energy gain in a terrestrial tokomak fusion reactor. [37:  Stacey Jr., W., Fusion: An Introduction to the Physics and Technology of Magnetic Confinement Fusion, J. Wiley and Sons, 1984.]  [38:  Hoooper, E., et al., “Sustained Spheromak Physics Experiment (SSPX): Design and Physics Results,” Plasma Physics and Controlled Fusion, vol. 54, 113001, 2012; 10.1088/0741-3335/54/11/113001.]  [39:  Gross, R., Fusion Energy, J. Wiley and Sons, 1984, pp. 267-274.]  [40:  Kammash, T. and Tang, R., “The Gasdynamic Mirror Fusion Propulsion System – Revisited,” AIP Conf. Proc. 746, 2005, pp. 529–535.]  [41:  Marrelli, L. et al., “The reversed field pinch,” Nucl. Fusion, vol. 61 (2), 2021, 023001]  [42:  ITER Organization, “ITER Research Plan within the Staged Approach (Level III – Provisional Version),” Report No. ITER-18-003, September 17, 2018.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>Adapting a spherical tokamak design, Williams et al.[endnoteRef:43] developed a detailed engineering analysis of a compact spheromak propulsion system designed for fast, piloted outer solar system mission. D-3He was used as the fusion fuel, and hydrogen was mixed with the fusion reactor exhaust to increase thrust and reduce specific impulse. The reactor power was designed for 7.9-GW, with approximately 6-GW of power in the charged exhaust products. Trip times to Jupiter were on the order of 4 months with thrust and Isp values of 27-kN and 35,400 s, respectively; a similar crewed mission to Saturn required 7 months, with an engine thrust of 21-kN and an Isp of 47,000 s. In each case the mass fraction was kept at approximately 10%, with a total initial mass of 1690 tonnes. While not an interstellar mission, the study serves as a primer for the considerations that must go into any fusion powered mission design and demonstrates the significant capabilities of fusion power compared to chemical and fission engines. [43:  Williams, C., “Nuclear Fusion Space Propulsion Research, Experimentation, Theory Development, and Systems Analysis Efforts Led by the NASA Glenn Research Center (1994-2004),” 10.2514/6.2020-4084, ASCEND 2020, November 16-18, 2020 (virtual event).] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>A magnetic confinement fusion concept that has been explored for planetary and interstellar travel is the direct fusion drive (DFD) rocket.[endnoteRef:44],[endnoteRef:45],[endnoteRef:46] The DFD uses a field-reverse configuration (FRC) to generate a stable, magnetically confined plasma of D-3He, which is radiofrequency heated to fusion temperatures. Deuterium propellant is tangentially injected to flow around the central reactor region, where it is heated by the reactor products and expelled at high temperature through a magnetic nozzle. The DFD operates at MW-class power levels, and is projected to provide thrust up to 10 N per MW at specific impulse values exceeding 20,000 s.  Approximately 35% of the fusion power goes to thrust, 30% is used for electric power, station keeping and communication, roughly 25% is lost to heat, and the remaining 10% is used for radiofrequency heating of the plasma. Analysis of a 1-MW class DFD estimated the engine could deliver a 1 tonne payload to Pluto orbit in under 4 years, a considerable improvement over the 10 years it took for New Horizons to reach Pluto on a flyby mission. A mission to the solar gravitational lens at 550-AU estimated that a 1.7-MW DFD engine operating at 4-N and an Isp of 52,000 s could carry a 300-kg payload this distance in approximately 13 years, at a reasonable mass fraction of 6%. As an interesting option, the same spacecraft could be placed into a 550-AU solar orbit in about 18 years, a unique capability of this propulsion system. For an interstellar mission to Alpha Centauri a higher engine power of 160-MW operating at a specific power of 100 kW/kg could reach the star system in approximately 350 years; operating 100 DFD units at 1-MW would reduce the specific power to a more reasonable 5-kW/kg per engine for the same trip time, with the added benefit of reducing mass during transit by jettisoning used DFD engines and spent fuel tanks. For the interstellar mission the DFD engines operate at constant thrust of 4-N and an Isp of 2.4x106 s, and both fast flyby and rendezvous missions can be achieved with this concept. While not quite the 40-year mission envisioned at the start of this chapter, the DFD does offer transit times of centuries vs the multiple millennia required by other propulsion systems. It is a step in the right direction. <h3></h3>
<h3>Interstellar Ramjet</h3>
<p></p>
<p>Perhaps the best-known magnetic confinement fusion propulsion system is the Bussard ramjet,[endnoteRef:47] which replenishes its fuel by scooping up hydrogen from the interstellar medium, ingesting and compressing it to maintain a continual fusion reaction. The benefit of the engine is that it cheats the rocket equation by not carrying its fuel, which as previously noted can rapidly drive the mass fraction to zero for an interstellar mission. A significant challenge, one of many with the concept, is the need to collect a sufficient number of hydrogen ions from the near vacuum of interstellar space to maintain the reactor. Bussard proposed the use of an extended electric or magnetic field that could channel any ions encountered along the path into the reaction chamber, and Fishback[endnoteRef:48] later expanded on this concept with a detailed design for the axial component of the magnetic scoop. Schattschneider and Jackson[endnoteRef:49] developed an adiabatic analysis of the charged particle dynamics for Fishback’s magnetic scoop design, and demonstrated that ionized interstellar hydrogen can indeed be scooped and guided to the fusion reactor using magnetic field strengths of 3 − 50 T. However, the engineering requirements are formidable. The magnetic field scoop radius is on the order of 2000 km for a reactor inlet radius of 10 m and is assumed to be generated by a superconducting solenoid coil. The faster the ship travels, the higher the magnetic field strength required to channel the ionized hydrogen (protons) into the reactor. The higher fields induce large mechanical stresses due the Lorentz force on the current carrying coils, which in turn increases the mechanical supports and adds mass. For a given thrust, higher mass leads to a lower spacecraft acceleration; as such, the ramjet can only accelerate up to a certain speed, after which the acceleration must be reduced, or the magnetic supports will be stressed beyond their breaking point. Nevertheless, the Bussard ramjet is a truly relativistic engine, capable of providing MN of thrust with accelerations approaching 1-g. Such capabilities make travel to the next star system within a few years’ time imaginable, if impractical due to the immense engineering challenges that are currently well beyond our capabilities. [47:  Bussard, Robert W. "Galactic matter and interstellar flight." Astronautica Acta 6.4 (1960): 179-194.]  [48:  Fishback, J. F., “Relativistic interstellar spaceflight”, Astronautica Acta (1969), vol. 15, pp. 25–35.]  [49:  Schattschneider, P., and Jackson, A., “The Fishback ramjet revisited,” Acta Astronautica, Vol. 191, 2022, pp. 227–234.] 
</p>
<h4></h4>
<h3>Inertial Confinement Fusion (ICF)</h3>
<p></p>
<p>ICF uses high-powered lasers or heavy-ion beams to rapidly compress and heat a small spherical fuel target. The target structure is typically composed of a hollow shell consisting of various hydrocarbon layers surrounding an inner core of solid D-T ice. The beams may directly impinge on the target, ablating the outer shell material to drive the remaining shell material inward at high velocity to compress the fuel, or use an indirect method, in which the fuel target is placed within a small metal cylinder (hohlraum) and the impinging ion or laser energy excites the hohlraum material to generate a uniform distribution of x-rays. As with a direct drive method, the x-rays ablate the shell material and, somewhat appropriately, this “rocket effect” of the outward moving shell material implodes the fuel target inward at high velocity. As the fuel compresses radially inward it stagnates and generates a “hot spot” which, for proper density and temperature conditions (Lawson criteria), ignites a burn wave that propagates outward into the compressed D-T fuel. Energy deposition from the 3.5-MeV 4He (α) products created during D-T fusion sustains the fusion burn until the rebounding target radially expands and cools below the conditions required to sustain fusion. The entire process happens literally in the blink of an eye; typical radial implosion velocities are on the order of 350 km/s, compressing the fuel to several hundred GBar and temperatures of several keV within tens of nanoseconds.</p>
<p></p>
<p>One of the first ICF propulsion concepts was VISTA, the Vehicle for Interplanetary Space Transport Application Powered by Inertial Confinement Fusion, developed by the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory.[endnoteRef:50],[endnoteRef:51] Designed as a single-stage vehicle for interplanetary missions, the propulsion system consisted of a diode-pumped solid-state laser used to drive a spherical array of lasers to uniformly compress D-T fuel pellets at a rate up to 30 pellets/sec. The fuel pellets would implode, fuse, and rapidly expand within a magnetic nozzle to provide thrust. A detailed system analysis estimated that a 6,000 tonne vehicle could produce 2x106 N of thrust at a specific impulse of 27,000 s, capable of performing a round-trip crewed Mars mission in 150 days. [50:  Orth, C., Klein, G., Sercel, J., Hoffman, N., Murray, K., and Chang-Diaz, F., “The VISTA spacecraft–Advantages of ICF for Interplanetary Fusion Propulsion Applications,” Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Report UCRL-96676, October 2, 1987.]  [51:  Orth, C., “VISTA -- A Vehicle for Interplanetary Space Transport Application Powered by Inertial Confinement Fusion,” Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, Report UCRL- TR-110500, March 31, 2005.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>From 1973 to 1977, the British Interplanetary Society sponsored Project Daedalus to design an uncrewed interstellar spacecraft that could reach Barnard’s Star (5.9 light years away) within 50 years.[endnoteRef:52] The designers conducted a detailed engineering design study of a pulsed ICF engine using D-3He pellets compressed by energetic electron beams, channeling the resulting fusion products using a magnetic nozzle to provide thrust. The two-stage spacecraft would be constructed in Earth orbit with an initial mass of 54,000 tonnes, including 50,000 tonnes of fuel (7% mass fraction). The first stage would operate for 2 years and reach 7.1% the speed of light, after which it was jettisoned, and the second stage fired for approximately 1.8 years to attain a final velocity of around 12% light speed. The craft would then cruise for 46 years until it quickly flew by its destination. Detonating 250 fuel targets/sec, the first stage engine produced 7.5x106 N of thrust at an Isp of 1.08x106 s, and the second stage produced 6.63x105 N with an Isp of 9.4x106 s. Due to the scarcity of terrestrial 3He, the fuel would be mined over decades from the moon or from the atmosphere of Jupiter and transported to Earth orbit to fuel the spacecraft.  [52:  Project Daedalus: demonstrating the engineering feasibility of interstellar travel, K. Long and P. Galea (Ed), British Interplanetary Society, London, England, SBN/ISSN/EAN: 5800056982849, 2015.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>In 2009 an all-volunteer study team revisited the Daedalus concept with Project Icarus, evaluating several alternative pulsed fusion concepts to propel a robotic science mission to Alpha Centauri. Propulsion concepts considered in the Icarus study included a z-pinch variant using D-D fusion (Icarus Firefly); neutron pumped laser ICF with D-D fuels (Icarus Ghost); a laser ICF system using D-3He fuels (Icarus Endeavor); and additional, less well-developed alternatives. Of these, the Firefly concept received the most engineering analysis, resulting in a spacecraft that was 750 m long with an initial total mass of 23,550 tonnes (compared to the Daedalus 54,000 tonnes) and propellant mass of 21,350 tonnes (mass fraction of 9%). The engine would produce a thrust of 6x105 N at an Isp of 1.2x106 s, reaching a maximum cruise velocity of just under 5% light speed in 10 years, then coasting for 85 years before decelerating for 5 years to enter the Alpha Centauri system. The spacecraft would eject probes to investigate Alpha Centauri B and enter orbit around Alpha Centauri A for detailed scientific analysis. Significant engineering work remains to be completed on the feasibility of this architecture, but as with the other fusion concepts considered in this chapter, this work shows that interstellar travel in reasonable trip times may be possible, overwhelming engineering challenges notwithstanding. </p>
<p></p>
<p>This section closes with a bright note on the road to ICF that occurred on December 5, 2022, when the U. S. National Ignition Facility (NIF) conducted the first controlled fusion experiment in history to reach scientific energy breakeven,[endnoteRef:53] where more energy was produced from fusion (3.15 MJ) than laser energy delivered to the target (2.05 MJ). Although NIF is primarily intended to underpin research for the national nuclear stockpile, the successful demonstration of scientific breakeven is a key milestone in understanding the physics of ICF for the design of future commercial fusion power plants and, by extrapolation, potentially more capable in-space propulsion systems for planetary and deep space exploration.  [53:  Zylstra A., et al., “Burning plasma achieved in inertial fusion,” Nature, Vol 601, January 27, 2022, pp. 542-548.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h3>Magneto-Inertial Fusion (MIF)</h3>
<p></p>
<p>The ICF approaches discussed above rely on the rapid spherical implosion of a fusion target by surface ablation to compress the fuel to the pressures and temperatures required for fusion. Magneto-inertial fusion (MIF), also known as magnetized target fusion (MTF), uses magnetic pressure to drive the target implosion.[endnoteRef:54] Typically, a conductive liner material surrounds the cylindrical fuel target; an axial magnetic field is applied between the target and liner, and the fuel is preheated prior to sending a high current pulse through the liner. The axial current surging through the outside of the conductive liner generates a strong azimuthal magnetic field which, combined with the axial current, creates an inward Lorentz force on the liner material. As the liner implodes inward it compresses the internal magnetic field to several thousand Tesla and the fuel to the densities and temperatures required to ignite a fusion burn; the strong internal field decreases radial thermal conduction losses and constrains the motion of the charged fusion products (high energy α particles) to improve the fuel heating. Reducing the energy losses and increasing the energetic particle heating allows a reduction in the required implosion velocity to achieve the necessary fusion conditions, in general making this a more efficient approach than ICF alone.[endnoteRef:55] Various versions of MIF have been developed and tested, including z-pinch devices[endnoteRef:56] and solid, liquid and plasma liner implosions of magnetized target plasmas.[endnoteRef:57] Significant work continues in this area as a promising alternative to MCF and ICF fusion concepts for terrestrial power generation, including experimental programs at several national laboratories.[endnoteRef:58] [54:  Siemon, R., Lindemuth, I., and Schoenberg, K., “Why Magnetized Target Fusion Offers a Low-Cost Development Path for Fusion Energy,” Plasma Physics and Controlled Fusion, Vol. 18. (6), 1999, pp. 363-386.]  [55:  Walsh, C., et al., “Magnetized ICF implosions: Scaling of temperature and yield enhancement,” Phys. Plasmas, Vol. 29, 042701, 2022; doi: 10.1063/5.0081915]  [56:  Liberman, M., De Groot, J., Toor, A., and Spielman, R., Physics of High-Density Z-Pinch Plasmas, Springer-Verlag, NY, 1999.]  [57:  Thio, Y., “Status of the US program in magneto-inertial fusion,” J. Phys.: Conf. Ser. 112, 042084, 2008.]  [58:  Wurden, G. et al., “Magneto-Inertial Fusion,” J. Fusion Energy, Vol. 35, 2016, pp. 69–77.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>Adapting terrestrial approaches, several concepts have been proposed that use various forms of MIF for propulsion. The pulsed z-pinch concept employs a high current pulse sent along a cylindrical target to create a strong azimuthal current in the outer layer, driving the outer layers inward at high velocity to compress and heat the fuel to fusion temperatures.[endnoteRef:59] Shear flows may be used to stabilize the plasma and improve confinement and burn times.[endnoteRef:60] Plasma jets may also be used to generate a compressive liner to implode a spherical fusion target,[endnoteRef:61] while other devices use rapidly accelerating solid liners or pulsed axial magnetic fields to compress and heat FRC plasmoids to fusion energies.[endnoteRef:62],[endnoteRef:63] Variations on MTF designs include firing fusion pellets into a high gradient magnetic field to induce strong liner currents and a compressive radial Lorentz force,[endnoteRef:64] as well as devices that boost target fusion by using the fusion neutrons to initiate additional fission reactions in a fissile liner material, further heating the plasma before exhausting the byproducts through a magnetic nozzle for thrust.[endnoteRef:65],[endnoteRef:66] Typical Isp and thrust values appear comparable to the ICF designs, making these concepts highly competitive for rapid planetary and deep space missions; additional analysis remains to be performed regarding their suitability for interstellar missions. [59:  Miernik, J., et al., “Z-Pinch Fusion-Based Nuclear Propulsion,” Acta Astronautica, Vol. 82, Issue 2, February 2013, pp. 173-182.]  [60:  Shumlak, U., “Z-pinch fusion,” J. Appl. Phys., Vol. 127, 200901, 2020; doi: 10.1063/5.0004228]  [61:  Thio, Y., Knapp, C., Kirkpatrick, R., Siemon, R., and Turchi, P., “A Physics Exploratory Experiment on Plasma Liner Formation,” NASA Technical Report Server Document ID 20020066592, January 1, 2002.]  [62:  Slough, J., Votroubek, G., and Phil, C., “Creation of a high-temperature plasma through merging and compression of supersonic field reversed configuration plasmoids,” Nucl. <h3>Inertial Electrostatic Confinement (IEC)</h3>
<p></p>
<p>Not to be confused with ICF, IEC devices use recirculating ion beams in a potential well to electrostatically confine electrons and create a confined, quasi-steady plasma.[endnoteRef:67] Gridded IEC devices use a spherical material mesh grid; in operation, fusion fuel is fed into the grid, and the negative voltage on the grid is raised to 10’s of kV to create a plasma discharge between the grid and a grounded outer wall. The large negative voltage extracts the plasma ions from the plasm and accelerates them toward the center of the negatively biased grid at high energy. As they traverse the inner grid region they have a chance of fusing, else they proceed to the opposite side of the grid where they slow, stop, and turn back to accelerate once again toward the center of the grid. The average ion energy set by the grid voltage can approach 100-keV, and ions focused at the center of the grid can in principle achieve densities of 1022 m-3, improving the chances for a fusion vs scattering collision to occur. In addition to scattering losses, ions can be lost by collisions with the grid and outer wall material, creating material degradation issues. An alternative to the spherical grid design is the polywell design by Bussard, in which a multipole cusp magnetic field is used to create a virtual cathode, removing the grid to mitigate electrode collisions and material melting concerns.[endnoteRef:68] IEC devices have demonstrated D-T fusion (though so far well below breakeven) and, due to their compact size and inherent simplicity, are being developed as robust neutron sources in addition to continued development toward terrestrial power applications using advanced fusion fuels such as p-11B. An interesting aspect of IEC devices for propulsion is that by introducing an asymmetry into the grid, a jet mode develops in which a well-collimated beam of fast ions exits the system. The high (multi-keV) potentials used in IEC devices can provide significantly higher ion velocities than other electric propulsion systems, and as such variations of these devices are also being explored for use in satellite and deep space mission applications.[endnoteRef:69] Given the high kinetic energy of the extracted ion beams, specific impulse values well in excess of 10,000 s appear feasible with such systems. However, as with the electric propulsion systems discussed earlier, the extracted ion current and corresponding thrust may be too low for reasonable interstellar trip times. [67:  Miley, G., and Krupakar Murali, S., Inertial Electrostatic Confinement (IEC) Fusion: Fundamentals and Applications, Springer, 2014.]  [68:  Bowden-Reid, R., and Khacha, J., “Electric potential in a magnetically confined virtual cathode fusion device,” Phys. Plasmas, Vol. 28, 042702, doi: 10.1063/5.0040792, 2021.]  [69:  Miley, G., Bromley, B., and Gu, Y., “A novel IEC propulsion unit for satellite applications,” AIP Conf. Proc. 361, March 1, 1996, pp. 1435–1440] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h2><a name="_Hlk147322299"></a>Summary: Fusion Propulsion for an Interstellar Mission</h2>
<p></p>
<p>Several types of fusion drives have been proposed that take advantage of the significant development work underway in terrestrial fusion power sources. As with the fission concepts discussed earlier, the direct exhaust of the energetic fusion byproducts appears to offer better potential performance for rapid interstellar transport than concepts that transfer heat to a propellant. The pulsed Icarus design, and the constant thrust, high specific impulse operation offered by the Direct Fusion Drive engine, offer possible initial steps on the path toward interstellar flight. The Bussard ramjet, while attractive in its use of interstellar in situ propellants, faces daunting challenges that are beyond known engineering capabilities. IEC devices offer high specific impulse in jet mode, but the thrust appears too low for effective interstellar vehicle accelerations. Fusion concepts employing ICF and MTF require significant additional analysis to determine whether they can realistically be engineered to reach to the next star system.</p>
<h1>Antimatter Propulsion</h1>
<p></p>
<p><a name="_Hlk147611511"></a>Moving beyond fusion, the most energetic process currently known is the annihilation of matter with antimatter. Every particle has a corresponding antiparticle (photons, π0 and μ0 mesons are their own antiparticles). Compared to its matter counterpart, antimatter has the same mass and lifetime, opposite electrical charge, and opposite magnetic moment. Predicted by Dirac in 1928 as a symmetric solution to his relativistic electron wave equation,[endnoteRef:70] the antielectron (positron) was discovered in 1932,[endnoteRef:71] and the antiproton in 1955.[endnoteRef:72]  Just as elements are built up from particles, anti-elements can be built up from antiparticles. Antihydrogen consists of an antielectron (e+) orbiting an antiproton ; the first  atoms were fleetingly created at the CERN accelerator in 1995,[endnoteRef:73] and are now routinely trapped and stored for several thousand seconds.[endnoteRef:74] In 2011 anti-helium nuclei, each containing two anti-protons and two anti-neutrons, were created at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC).[endnoteRef:75] The process was exceedingly rare however, generating 18 anti-helium nuclei out of nearly a billion high energy collisions. Producing the next stable anti-element, anti-lithium, appears to be beyond the capabilities of today’s colliders, but there are no physical principles that prohibit such heavier anti-elements from existing.  [70:  Baggott, J., The Quantum Cookbook, Oxford University Press, 2020, pp. 179–202.]  [71:  Lindley, D., “Landmarks: The First Antiparticle,” Phys. Rev. Focus, 17, 5, February 10, 2006.]  [72:  Orrman-Rossiter, K., “Observation and Annihilation: The Discovery of the Antiproton,” Phys. Perspect. 23, 2021, pp. 3–24.]  [73:  Baur, G. et al., “Production of antihydrogen,” Physics Letters B, Vol. 368 (3), February 1, 1996, pp. 251-258.]  [74:  The ALPHA Collaboration, “Confinement of antihydrogen for 1,000 seconds,” Nature Phys, Vol. 7, 2011, pp. 558–564.]  [75:  Battersby, S., “Physicists create heaviest form of antimatter ever seen,” New Scientist, March 22, 2011.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>The amount of energy produced when matter meets antimatter is enormous. For one kilogram of combined matter and antimatter the energy released is approximately 9x1010 MJ/kg, which is over 250 times the specific energy released in nuclear fusion and over 8 orders of magnitude greater than the specific energy released in chemical combustion. Efficiently using this energy could potentially propel us to the distant destinations envisioned by countless science fiction authors, and antimatter propulsion has received serious attention since the first electron-positron engine was proposed in the 1950s. How this energy might be used for propulsion, and the extraordinary challenges that must be overcome to move these propulsion concepts closer to reality, are briefly discussed below.</p>
<p></p>

<p></p>
<p>The rest mass energy (moc2) of an electron or a positron is 0.511 MeV (8.2x10-14 J); when an electron and positron meet, they annihilate in a burst of gamma-radiation:</p>

<h3>Electron-Positron Propulsion</h3>
<p></p>
<p><a name="_Hlk147616591"></a><a name="_Hlk147617347"></a><a name="_Hlk147617491"></a><a name="_Hlk147618013"></a><a name="_Hlk147618088"></a><a name="_Hlk148027565"></a>The earliest antimatter rocket concept was proposed in 1953 by Sanger,[endnoteRef:76] who suggested redirecting the energetic γ-rays from the  reaction to produce thrust (antiprotons hadn’t been discovered yet). Unfortunately, there is no presently feasible method to reflect high energy -rays, resulting in a very low engine efficiency. In a later 2006 study, Smith[endnoteRef:77] evaluated three positron-based propulsion systems. The first was a solid-core concept that absorbs -rays from positron annihilation in a refractory core, which serves as a heat exchanger to transfer energy to a flowing hydrogen propellant. It was estimated that the engine could produce an Isp of 920 seconds with chamber temperatures at 3000 K, at a thrust and power that matched nuclear thermal fission propulsion systems. For a Mars mission, a spacecraft with three 72-kN solid-core positron engines would require approximately 6-9 mg of positrons, assuming 100% energy conversion efficiency. The second concept was a gas-core design, where the -rays directly heat a propellant through a one- or two-fluid process. It was found that the propellant in a one-fluid system did not efficiently absorb the -rays, causing the engine performance to drop below 10%. However, in the two-fluid system, a thrust of 130 kN with an Isp of 3200 sec appeared achievable with an engine efficiency of about 85%. A burn time of 30 minutes could produce a mission velocity of 3.7 km/s using just 25 mg of positrons with a total initial spacecraft mass of about 56000 kg and final mass of 50,000 kg at burnout. A third concept used a solid propellant ablating from a surface bombarded by pulses of high-energy photons down converted from the -rays. To improve coupling with the material, the -rays are shifted to lower wavelengths through an intermediate high-Z material. Initial performance estimates for a Mars mission with a total initial spacecraft mass of 60,000 kg included a propellant mass of 9300 kg and a positron mass of 34 mg to generate 85-kN of thrust, reaching a mission velocity of 3.7 km/s. Positron storage is typically accomplished using Penning-Malmberg traps, which confine positrons in electrostatic potential wells augmented with magnetic fields to help prevent the loss of positrons from the ends of the trap. Trapping and storage of 1010 positrons has been demonstrated,[endnoteRef:78] and trapping up to 1015 positrons may be possible in larger traps with higher magnetic fields.[endnoteRef:79] Even at this relatively high storage density the mass of positrons is only the order of 9x10-10 mg; to store milligram quantities of positrons would require an excessively large number of traps, adding significant mass and complexity to the system. [76:  Sanger, E., “Zur Theorie der Photoneraketen” (“The Theory of Photon Rockets”), Ingenieur-Archiv, Vol. 21, 1953, pp. 213-226.]  [77:  Smith, G., “Positron Propelled and Powered Space Transport Vehicle for Planetary Missions,” NIAC Phase I Final Report, Research Subaward No. 07605-003-048, April 19, 2006.]  [78:  Dupre’, P., “A new scheme to accumulate positrons in a Penning-Malmberg trap with a linac-based positron pulsed source,” AIP Conf. Proc. Vol. 1521, 2013, pp. 113–122.]  [79:  Meyer, K., et al., “Long-Term Confinement of Dense Positron Plasma,” AIP Conf. Proc. Vol. 692, 2003, pp. 184–192.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>Rather than store large numbers of positrons, Weed et al.[endnoteRef:80] investigated a concept for a radioisotope positron-catalyzed fusion engine that injects pulses of positrons into a substrate containing deuterium. As the positrons annihilate with substrate electrons the annihilation energy couples to lattice-trapped deuterons, giving them sufficient kinetic energy to fuse with other deuterons in the substrate. The D-D fusion reactions are exhausted to provide thrust, while the fusion neutrons are captured in a nozzle blanket to produce a positron-emitting radioisotope (79Kr), which is enriched and deposited onto a source layer to produce more positrons. This ‘breeder’ cycle avoids the need to store large amounts of positrons, greatly simplifying the overall system. Following a detailed cycle analysis, preliminary estimates indicted that a relatively compact engine design might achieve specific impulse and thrust values of 3x105 s and 0.2-N, respectively, with a total mission velocity of 60 km/s. [80:  Weed, R., Machacek, J., and Ramamurthy, B, “Radioisotope Positron Propulsion: NIAC Phase I Report”, Positron Dynamics, NASA award 80NSSC18K0910, 2019.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>While the positron-based engine concepts discussed above are not suitable for rapid interstellar transit, they do demonstrate the immense power available from the annihilation of small amounts of matter and antimatter. The challenge of trying to efficiently use the high energy -rays for propulsion has led to several concepts that instead use the charged energetic products from proton-antiproton annihilation, described in the following section.</p>
<p></p>
<h3>Proton-Antiproton Annihilation</h3>
<p></p>
<p><a name="_Hlk147611803"></a><a name="_Hlk147611672"></a><a name="_Hlk147613749"></a>The higher rest mass energies of the proton-antiproton (p) pair yield 1877 MeV per annihilation event, compared with the 1.02 MeV released by electron-positron annihilation.  Protons and antiprotons may undergo direct annihilation, or at low energies may form bound states of protonium (Pn) leading to subsequent annihilation. At low energies, p annihilation proceeds via rearrangement collisions in Protonium, while direct annihilation dominates at energies above roughly 10 eV. </p>
<p></p>
<p>Unlike electron-positron annihilation, proton-antiproton annihilation process proceeds through several stages as shown in Figure 6 below. The initial byproduct of p annihilation is the formation (on average) of three charged pions (), each with a rest mass energy of 139.6 MeV, and two neutral pions () each with a rest mass energy of 135.0 MeV; hence 689 MeV of the initial annihilation energy is contained in the rest mass of the newly formed pions. The average kinetic energy of each charged pion is approximately 250 MeV/pion; at this energy the charged pions are traveling at 93% the speed of light and their lifetimes are extended from 26 ns (at rest) to approximately 70 ns. Unless acted upon, each charged pion will travel almost 21 meters before it decays. The remainder of the annihilation energy resides in the kinetic energy of the neutral pions, roughly 220 MeV/pion. At this energy the neutral pion lifetime is extended to a still brief 2.2x10-16 seconds, and upon their decay each neutral pion produces two energetic -rays, each with energies between 130 and 300 MeV/.</p>

<p><a name="_Hlk147662762"></a>The decays into a positron, an electron-neutrino (νe) and an anti-muon-neutrino (), while the decays into an electron, a mu-neutrino () and an anti-electron-neutrino (). The total energy available from the decay of a charged muon traveling at 94% light speed is about 300 MeV/muon. Of this energy, 0.511 MeV goes into creating an electron or positron, about 100 MeV into the electron or positron kinetic energy, and the remainder to the noninteracting neutrinos. The decay of the three charged muons provides roughly 300 MeV in kinetic energy of the three electrons/positrons; these are stable against decay but can annihilate with one another to produce 0.511 MeV -rays, the ultimate end of the proton-antiproton annihilation process.</p>
<p> </p>
<h3>Proton-Antiproton Propulsion </h3>
<p></p>
<p><a name="_Hlk147665026"></a><a name="_Hlk148033166"></a>The variety of proposed proton-antiproton (p) engines typically fall into one of five categories: solid core, gas core, plasma core, beam core, and antiproton-catalyzed fission-fusion designs. Each of these is briefly described in the following sections. </p>
<p></p>
<h4>Solid-Core Engines</h4>
<p></p>
<p>Solid core p engine designs absorb the antiproton annihilation products in a bulk refractory metal core that acts as a heat exchanger, raising the temperature of a flowing propellant which is then ejected through a nozzle to provide thrust. Although the neutrinos will carry their energy out of the engine, stopping the charged pions and subsequent muons, electrons, positrons, and -rays with the core can capture a high fraction (typically above 70%) of the annihilation energy, providing thrust and specific impulse values comparable to nuclear thermal propulsion systems. In the solid core engine design analyzed by Howe and Metzger[endnoteRef:81], the annihilation of 100-mg of antiprotons provided sufficient energy to complete a round-trip, opposition class Mars mission with 3 times less launch mass than chemical engines and slightly less mass than a nuclear thermal rocket.  [81:  Howe, S. and Metzger, J., ”Antiproton-Based Propulsion Concepts and the Potential Impact on a Manned Mars Mission,” AIAA 87-2191, AIAA/SAE/ASME/ASEE 23rd Joint Propulsion Conference, San Diego, CA, June 29-July 2, 1987.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h4>Gas Core Engines</h4>
<p></p>
<p>Similar to the concept of gas core nuclear thermal engines, gas core antimatter concepts raise the specific impulse by removing the temperature constraints imposed by material walls. This type of engine was also analyzed by Howe and Metzger to perform the crewed opposition-class Mars mission. The engine design assumed a 100-atm hydrogen gas in a central pressure chamber surrounded by high current solenoids producing axial magnetic fields up to 50-T to radially confine the charged annihilation products away from the chamber walls. For antiproton flow rates comparable to the solid core annihilation engine, the gas core engine produced higher specific impulse values of 1000-1500 s with thrust values of 440-kN and 300-kN, respectively. The engine only operated at an efficiency of 8% due to the less efficient transfer of annihilation energy to the propellant gas vs. the sold core, wasting a significant amount of the annihilation energy. Cassenti[endnoteRef:82] considered the use of a gas-core annihilation engine for a less demanding orbital transfer vehicle (OTV) application, moving a 10-tonne payload from low Earth orbit to geosynchronous orbit and back again. The engine design consisted of a central combustion chamber 2 m in diameter, operating at 100 atm with a hydrogen propellant. A 5-T magnetic field in the chamber radially confined the charged annihilation byproducts, while 50-T fields at the ends of the chamber reflect the products for improved axial confinement. A hydrogen propellant flow rate of 28.7 kg/s and antiproton flow rate of 5.77 μg/s produced an engine thrust of 9.8-kN at a modest specific impulse of 340-s, sufficient for the proposed OTV application but far short of the Isp values of interest for interstellar propulsion. A total of 40 tonnes of hydrogen and 8-mg of antiprotons were used for the mission, with an engine conversion efficiency of around 35%. [82:  Cassenti, B., “Antimatter Propulsion for OTV Applications,” AIAA 84-1485, AIAA/ASME/SAE 20th Joint Propulsion Conference, Cincinnati, OH, June 11-13 1984.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h4>Plasma Core Engine</h4>
<p></p>
<p>Similar to the gas core design, the plasma core engine utilizes magnetic mirror fields to contain the charged annihilation products, but unlike the gas core uses an ionized (versus high pressure neutral) gas propellant. Morgan[endnoteRef:83] sketched a preliminary design for a pulsed heavy-ion plasma engine, in which antiprotons annihilate with nucleons in a heavy atom nucleus to produce energetic charged nuclides and particles. The charged byproducts are confined by external magnetic field coils and collisionally transfer their energy to the remaining heavy atoms, forming a magnetically confined plasma. The magnetic mirror field is then relaxed at one end of the system, and the plasma escapes to provide thrust. The antiproton and propellant injection rates can be tailored to provide a variety of thrust and specific impulse values with a single engine. However, the efficiency of the pulsed heavy-ion plasma engine is [83:  Morgan Jr., D., “Concepts for the Design of an Antimatter Annihilation Rocket,” J. British Interplanetary Society, Vol. 35 (9), 1982, pp. 405-412.] 
</p>
<p>limited by the amount of energy residing in the kinetic energy of the heavy charged annihilation byproducts, estimated to be less than 20% of the initial annihilation energy; only a fraction of this available energy will be transferred by collisions to the plasma, further reducing the total engine efficiency. </p>
<p></p>
<p>If the charged byproducts produced by antiproton annihilation in hydrogen were used instead, a variable thrust, variable specific impulse engine might be designed without the disadvantages of heavy atom annihilation. LaPointe[endnoteRef:84] performed a parametric analysis of an antiproton annihilation plasma engine that uses hydrogen propellant ionized and confined in a strong magnetic field. Injected antiprotons annihilate with the initially neutral hydrogen gas to produce relativistic, charged pions. The pions traverse the hydrogen and transfer some amount of energy before decaying into charged, relativistic muons. The energetic muons interact with the hydrogen over microsecond time scales before decaying into relativistic electrons and positrons. The electrons and positrons transfer energy to the hydrogen over a time scale corresponding to the plasma confinement time, typically a few milliseconds. The electrons and positrons are stable, although positrons may be lost due to annihilation with plasma electrons. Strong (10-20 T) magnetic fields are used for confinement and stability, but even so particles will be continually lost from the system.  [84:  LaPointe, M., “Antiproton Powered Propulsion with Magnetically Confined Plasma Engines,” J. Propulsion, Vol. 7 (5), Sept-Oct 1991, pp. 749-759.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p><a name="_Hlk147683435"></a>The results of the parametric modeling indicated that the hydrogen plasma core engine suffers from extremely low energy transfer efficiencies at low hydrogen number densities (1020 m-3) and from excessive plasma bremsstrahlung at high hydrogen number densities (1024 m-3). Optimum performance was obtained with a hydrogen number density of 1022 m-3 and antiproton number densities between 1016 m-3 and 1018 m-3. Thrust values of several hundred kN could be generated over a wide range of specific impulse values (4600 s to 60,000 s) with 5-ms plasma confinement times and realistic magnetic mirror strengths. However, the annihilation energy transfer efficiencies were generally below 1%, with relativistic electrons and positrons providing most of the plasma heating. Higher thrust could be produced using higher hydrogen number densities, but the plasma was shown to radiatively cool and recombine if not expelled within a few hundred microseconds. Preliminary analysis also indicated that substantial mass will be required to shield the superconducting coils from the high energy gamma radiation emitted in the p annihilation process. Shielding problems are compounded by plasma bremsstrahlung radiation, which will contribute to shield surface ablation and degradation. The total mass of the radiation shield could dominate the system mass, limiting the achievable spacecraft acceleration. Combined with the poor annihilation energy conversion efficiencies, the plasma core engine does not appear to be a viable means for interstellar propulsion.</p>
<p></p>
<h4>Beam Core Engines</h4>
<p></p>
<p><a name="_Hlk147685122"></a><a name="_Hlk147783333"></a>The solid, gas and plasma core engine concepts outlined above use the energetic p annihilation products to heat a propellant, with varying degrees of efficiency. Transferring the annihilation energy to a propellant increases the thrust but lowers the specific impulse that might achieved if the relativistic annihilation byproducts were used directly. This is beneficial for planetary and deep space missions, but detrimental for missions requiring relativistic exhaust velocities. To achieve these high exhaust velocities, the beam core engine uses strong magnetic fields to guide the relativistic charged pions created in p annihilation directly out of the engine. Morgan83 proposed the concept of a relativistic beam core engine in which antiprotons are injected axially into a strong conical magnetic nozzle, and an equal amount of neutral hydrogen atoms are injected perpendicular to the magnetic field, colliding with the antiproton beam to create the high energy pions, as shown in Fig. 8: </p>
<p><img src="embedded:image10.png" alt=""></p>
<p class="caption">Figure 7. Antiproton beam core engine design.83</p>
<p>With proper engine design nearly 100% of the antiprotons can be annihilated. The neutral pions will decay almost immediately into high energy -rays, but most of the charged pions will be redirected by the magnetic field to exit the engine; about 10% will escape radially across the field lines, and 5% will exit the upstream part of the engine. Depending on the length of the pion path, some will decay into charge muons and neutrinos before exiting the engine, reducing the available energy for thrust. Morgan estimates that 40%-50% of the annihilation energy appears in the kinetic energy of the relativistic charged products leaving the engine; thus an antiproton flow rate of 2x1020 /sec combined with an equal flow of hydrogen atoms will produce about 70-N of thrust at a blazingly high specific impulse of 2.9x107s. The spacecraft mass was estimated to be 7000 kg, including 500 kg of hydrogen and an equal mass of antiprotons; at 70-N the engine would produce an acceleration of around 0.01 m/s2, and could reach Alpha Centauri in a few hundred years. In a later analysis Frisbee[endnoteRef:85] developed scaling equations and performed detailed parametric sensitivity analyses on several beam core engine technologies, including the magnetic nozzle, radiation shielding, thermal control systems and radiators, propellant storage and feed systems, electric power systems, payloads, dust shields (as protection for relativistic travel), and miscellaneous spacecraft subsystems. The “stretch” mission analyzed by Frisbee was to send a 100-tonne science payload to a distance of 40 light years at a cruise velocity of 50% the speed of light, which required several million <i>tonnes</i> of antiprotons. An additional analysis by Frisbee[endnoteRef:86] for a 70-year rendezvous mission to Alpha Centauri reduced the amount of antiprotons required to around 1600 tonnes, and an Alpha Centauri flyby mission to 354 tonnes. As discussed below this amount of antimatter far exceeds our current production capabilities (measured in nanograms per year), but the studies are a <i>tour de force</i> exposition of the technology and supporting infrastructure requirements associated with using antimatter for truly rapid interstellar travel. [85:  Frisbee, R., “How to Build and Antimatter Rocket for Interstellar Missions – Systems Level Considerations in Designing Advanced Technology Vehicles,” AIAA 2003-4676, 39th AIAA/ASME/SAE/ASEE Joint Propulsion Conference and Exhibit, Huntsville, Al, July 20-23, 2003.]  [86:  Frisbee, “Limits of Interstellar Flight Technology”, in Frontiers of Propulsion Physics, ed. M. Millis and E. Davis, Progress in Aeronautics and Astronautics, Vol 227, American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics, Inc., 2009, pp.84-92.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h4>Antimatter-Catalyzed Fission and Fusion</h4>
<p></p>
<p>A review of various concepts proposed to use small amounts of antiprotons to initiate fusion or fission-fusion events in the core of a small target is provided by Cassenti et al.[endnoteRef:87]  These include the use of antiprotons to create fission in heavy nuclei, with the highly energetic byproducts initiating fusion within the target; antiproton heating to directly ignite a fusion reaction in a target; and using the muons created during antiproton annihilation for muon catalyzed fusion. [87:  Cassenti, B., Kammash, T. and Galbraith, D., “Antiproton Catalyzed Fusion Propulsion for Interplanetary Missions,” J. Propulsion and Power, Vol. 13 (3), May – June 1997, pp. 428-434.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>In the first method, when antiprotons annihilate with fissile nuclei they fission the nucleus and generate high energy nuclides, neutrons, and other particles, with over 20% of the reaction energy contained in the kinetic energy of the nuclides (the basis of Morgan’s plasma core engine discussed earlier). In this approach, a fuel target consisting of D-T and fissile material is initially compressed with ion beams or lasers (as in ICF), and a pulse of antiprotons is injected. The antiprotons annihilate with the fissile nuclei, and the energy of resulting nuclides is deposited in the fuel to ignite a fusion reaction. The fission neutrons generate additional fission reactions in the surrounding fissile material, boosting the fusion fuel temperature. The result is an expanding high temperature plasma that can be directed by a magnetic nozzle to produce thrust. An initial analysis of an engine based on this process (ICAN-II) was developed by Gaidos et al.[endnoteRef:88] They estimated that a 345 tonne vehicle dry mass with 421 tonnes of propellant could produce an Isp of 13,500 s and a thrust of 100-kN, sufficient to accelerate the vehicle to a velocity of 25 km/sec in 3 days using just 30 ng of antiprotons.  [88:  Gaidos, G., et al., “Antiproton-Catalyzed Microfission/Fusion Propulsion Systems for Exploration of the Outer Solar System and Beyond,” CP420, Space Technology and Applications International Forum-1998, edited by Mohamed S. El-Genk, American Institute of Physics, 1998.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>The other two approaches appear to have fundamental issues that may preclude their use. In the second method, antiprotons are used to directly ignite a fusion reaction in a D-D or similar fuel target. In this case the annihilation generates mainly neutral and charged pions, with the latter immediately decaying into high energy -rays. The highly relativistic charged pions must be absorbed within the fuel for significant energy transfer before they decay into muons and neutrinos (ref Figure 6). The muons can also add energy to the fuel before they decay, and the subsequent electrons and positrons will add energy as well. Strong magnetic fields can be used to confine the charged byproducts and enhance the energy transfer. However, as modeled by Morgan et al.[endnoteRef:89], each antiproton might only catalyze one fusion reaction; because the energy required to make an antiproton is significantly greater than the energy released in fusion, this may not be a viable means for power or propulsion. The final approach uses muons created in the antiproton annihilation chain to sustain muon-catalyzed fusion of D-T fuel. The antiprotons provide a more compact source to generate the muons compared to an accelerator and would be more suitable for in-space propulsion. However, as pointed out by Cassenti et al., muon-catalyzed fusion is only effective in a narrow temperature range centered around 1200 K, to support a molecular resonance between the tritium and deuterium atoms. These low temperatures would be difficult to maintain, making this a challenging approach. [89:  Morgan Jr., D., Perkins, L., and Haney, S., “Antiproton Catalyzed Fusion,” UCRL-JC-120149, Lawrence Berkely National Laboratory, May 15, 1995.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>Building on these results, Cassenti et al.87 developed a model for a pulsed ICF engine with a fission-fusion burn triggered by antiprotons. The ICF pellet consists of an inner core of D-T fuel. The fuel is coated on one hemisphere with fissionable material, and the core is surrounded by an outer shell consisting of a heavy metal. The inside of the shell consists of neutron reflecting material. A pulse of antiprotons and positrons is injected through a hole in the shell, causing annihilation to occur at the surface of the fissile hemisphere. The resulting annihilation products ionize the fuel, creating a plasma within the shell. Electrons created during the annihilation develop a current flow, generating a strong, transient magnetic field within the shell; the magnetic field traps the charged particles (fission fragments, fuel ions, pions, muons, etc.) which heat the core to fusion temperatures. The neutron reflecting material reflects neutrons back into the fuel to boost the fission reactions, and the heavy metal shell provides inertia to contain the plasma long enough for fusion to occur. It was estimated that a pulsed engine based on this design could produce an Isp of 6x105 s for complete fusion burn, or about 2x105 s for 10% burn, assuming a 3.5-g fuel pellet injected with 2x1013 antiprotons. The pellet design parameters were used to estimate planetary mission trip times, with trips to Mars occurring in days and trips to the asteroid belt in weeks using microgram quantities of antimatter. </p>
<p></p>
<p></p>
<p>Each of the antiproton annihilation engine concepts outlined in this section rely on the availability of antiprotons, from the tens of nanogram quantities used in ICAN-II for small robotic planetary exploration to the tonnes required for Frisbee’s stretch mission of sending a 100-tonne payload to Alpha Centauri in under a century. How much antimatter can we currently produce and store in our quest to make these trips a reality?</p>
<p></p>
<h3>Antimatter Production and Storage</h3>
<p></p>
<p>Antielectrons (positrons) are produced when a radionuclide decays into a neutron, positron, and electron-neutrino. Cyclotrons are routinely used to create short-lived isotopes such as 11C, 13N, 18F and others that decay by positron emission, which are commonly used in PET (positron emission tomography) scans for medical diagnostics. Natural positron emitters are more rare; for example, only 0.001% of 40K decays into a 40Ar nucleus via positron emission. An interesting side note, bananas, a rich source of potassium, produce a positron approximately once every 75 minutes, a rate insufficient to fuel a starship, but a potentially fun fact for the supermarket. Positron-electron pairs can also be created through energetic processes that focus the equivalent rest mass energy of the pair (2m0c2) in a very small volume; this can be accomplished in particle accelerators that collide energetic particle beams with targets; pair creation from the vacuum by energetic photons (laser; γ-rays); and cosmic rays that collide with nuclei in the atmosphere, forming charged muons that decay into electrons and positrons. Positrons can readily be stored in Penning-Malmberg traps, which as noted earlier confine the positrons in electrostatic potential wells with magnetic fields to help prevent the loss of positrons from the ends of the trap.</p>
<p></p>
<p>Antiproton production is not as easy. Given their larger mass, the minimum amount of energy required to create a proton-antiproton pair is 1.876x103 MeV, compared to 1.02 MeV for electron-positron pair creation. At present, the only method for us to create proton-antiproton pairs is with high energy particle accelerators, such as those found at the Conseil Européen pour la Recherche Nucléaire (CERN) on the French-Swiss border in Geneva, the Deutsches Elektronen-Synchrotron (DESY) in Hamburg, Germany, and the U.S. Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory (FNAL) in Chicago, Illinois. </p>
<p></p>
<p>To produce antiprotons, a high energy proton beam is collided with a target to produce copious amounts of particles, some fraction of which are antiprotons. FNAL[endnoteRef:90] used an incident beam of 120-GeV protons to impact a heavy metal target; typically, less than 5% of the resulting particles spraying out of the target were antiprotons. A magnetic lens downstream of the target was used to focus 10%-30% of the antiprotons, and of these about 1% had the correct energy to be collected and cooled. Once collected, the antiprotons were sent to a decelerator to further reduce their energy, and from there to low energy antiproton accumulator ring, where they could be circulated and stored for several days. About 1 antiproton was collected for every 105 protons incident on the target, and approximately 1012 antiprotons could be stored in the accumulator. Between 1985 and 2011, FNAL produced about 17 x10-9 g (17 nanograms) of antiprotons, accounting for over 90% of the total world production of antimatter. In comparison, CERN has produced a total of about 2 nanograms, and DESY about 1 nanogram. If annihilated, the total amount of antiprotons produced to date would generate 3.2×106 J of energy, enough to boil about 1 liter of water.  [90:  Nagaitsev, S., “Fermilab Antiproton Source, Recycler Ring, and Main Injector,” Report number: FERMILAB-FN-0957-AD (2014); arXiv:1408.0759 [physics.acc-ph]] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p><a name="_Hlk148032193"></a>Including the cost of facility operation, producing antiprotons is not cheap; Schmidt et al.[endnoteRef:91] calculated the FNAL production cost at approximately $6.4x1012 per milligram of antiprotons. In fairness, the world’s high energy accelerators were not optimized for antiproton production, and they are not dedicated solely to the production of antimatter. The dedicated operation of FNAL for antiproton production would be able to produce nanograms of antiprotons/year. Improvements to the facilities, such as using thinner targets to better focus the antiproton particles, improving the antiproton collection and storage methods, and optimizing the proton acceleration energy and duty cycle, could increase production rates even further, to micrograms/year. Alternatively, a dedicated facility specifically designed for ground-based antiproton production could be constructed; a 1998 Rand Corporation study[endnoteRef:92] evaluated the cost of a dedicated facility to produce several mg/year of antiprotons. They estimated the facility cost (in 1988 dollars) at between $3 x109 and 10x109 to build, and including operating expenses the total antiproton production cost could be brought down to around $6.4x107/milligram, a more reasonable cost for several of the antiproton engine concepts discussed above. [91:  Schmidt, G. et al., “Antimatter Requirements and Energy Costs for Near Term Propulsion Applications,” J. Power and Propulsion, Vol. 16 (5), Sep-Oct 2000; pp 923-928.]  [92:  Proceedings of the Rand Workshop on Antiproton Science and Technology, World Scientific, Singapore, June 1988] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>In addition to production, the storage of large quantities of antiprotons presents a unique challenge for propulsion systems. The density of antiprotons that can be stored is limited by space charge effects. As noted, the accumulator ring at FNAL could hold on the order of 1012 antiprotons (1.7x10-12 g) for long periods of time. Between 2000 and 2003, a portable Penning-Malmberg trap was designed and built at the NASA Marshall Space Flight Center to hold 1012 antiprotons for several days to perform ground-based propulsion experiments;[endnoteRef:93] though constructed, the project was cancelled before it was used for its intended purpose. The challenge with using electrostatic traps to provide nanograms of antiprotons is that thousands of such traps would be required, adding significant mass and complexity to a vehicle. To circumvent the limits of these space charge storage approaches, concepts have been proposed that use antihydrogen atoms stored as ice in place of charged antiprotons, leading to significantly higher storage densities. As noted earlier, antihydrogen atoms are now routinely made and trapped for long periods of time; Forward[endnoteRef:94] suggested that it may be possible to nucleate the antihydrogen to form solid antihydrogen ice or microcrystals that could be more readily stored. Unlike the nucleation of regular hydrogen, antihydrogen can’t be allowed to touch the chamber walls and would need to be directly nucleated from cold, trapped antihydrogen atoms. It may be possible to laser cool the antihydrogen atoms to form and trap antihydrogen molecules, which could then condense to form microcrystals of antihydrogen ice. Alternatively, it may be possible to cluster antihydrogen atoms around a single charged antiproton, adding antihydrogen atoms to grow the cluster into microcrystals. While these approaches remain speculative, developing such techniques for high density antihydrogen storage is critical if antiproton engines are to someday become a reality. [93:  Martin, J., Lewis, R., Chakrabarti, S., Sims, W., Pearson, J. and Fant, W., “Ion Dynamic Capture Experiments with the High Performance Antiproton Trap (HiPAT),” NASA Technology Report Server (NTRS) DOI: 10.1063/ 1.1541340, February 2002.]  [94:  Forward, R., and Davis, J., Mirror Matter: Pioneering Antimatter Physics , Wiley Science, 1998.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>Fortunately, once solid antihydrogen is formed there are feasible options to store and transport it.94 Because antihydrogen is diamagnetic, when it is placed in a magnetic field a weak dipole moment is induced within the antihydrogen opposite to the direction of the applied field. As such, solid antihydrogen could be passively trapped in a magnetic “bottle” formed by current-carrying rings. Alternatively, the antihydrogen could be levitated between two charged electrodes; a weak ultraviolet light source could be used to liberate positrons from the surface of the ice, leaving behind a negative charge that responds to the confining electric fields. To transport antiprotons from storage to the engine, an ultraviolet laser could be used to drive positrons from a spot on the surface, and an intense electric field could then be used to pull off the charged antiprotons; the antiprotons could then be electromagnetically channeled to the annihilation region of the engine. If suspended in the form of a frozen cloud of microcrystals, an ultraviolet light could be used to drive positrons from the surface of a selected microcrystal, leaving a negative charge which would allow an electric field to pull the microcrystal from the cloud. The charged antihydrogen microcrystal could then be electromagnetically channeled to the engine.</p>
<p></p>
<h3>Summary: Antimatter Propulsion for an Interstellar Mission</h3>
<p></p>
<p>Clearly there are several challenges associated with use of antimatter for propulsion; in particular, the production rates of antiprotons are woefully inadequate to generate the amount of antimatter fuel needed for an interstellar voyage. Production costs in current facilities are also prohibitive, requiring the construction of a dedicated antiproton factory to reduce production costs and increase production rates. Once generated, the antiprotons would need to be converted to antihydrogen and condensed to form clouds of microcrystals or solid balls of frozen antihydrogen, through methods that have yet to be demonstrated. Once formed there are potentially viable methods for its storage and transport, though scaling these to produce the antiproton beam currents required for interstellar missions remains to be explored. While sub-microgram amounts of antiprotons might be used for rapid travel within the solar system, the amount of antiprotons required for interstellar trip times less than a century borders on hundreds of kilograms to several tonnes, and as noted by Frisbee,85 generating this much antimatter would require a civilization-defining development program.</p>
<p></p>
<h2>Propulsion Without Propellant</h2>
<p></p>
<p>The propulsion approaches considered so far in this chapter have carried their source of propulsive energy with them: chemicals, fission and fusion fuels, antimatter. Most have also required a significant amount of propellant to be carried as well, the mass of which dramatically reduces their applicability for interstellar missions. Several concepts have been proposed that move the propulsion system off the vehicle, relying on an externally delivered force to accelerate the craft and thereby circumvent the constraints of the rocket equation. Employing energetic beams of photons, particles, and projectiles, these concepts are discussed below.</p>
<p></p>

<p>To increase the amount of acceleration, the surface area should be as large as possible, the total mass as small as possible, and the material as reflective as possible. This drives the design toward large, lightweight, highly reflective planar sheets, reminiscent of the ship sails that capture the wind for propulsion, giving rise to the equally enchanting vision of solar sailing. First envisioned by Tsiolkovsky in the 1920s and given a firm theoretical footing in the 1950s,95 the advancement of resilient thin film materials and support structures combined with highly capable, low mass science payloads have ushered in a new era of interest in solar sails for heliophysics research and solar system exploration. Similar to ship sails tacking in the wind, orienting the surface of a solar sail at an angle to the incident sunlight can provide translational motion in addition to the radial motion away from the sun; canting the sail’s surface in one direction can increase its tangential velocity and raise its heliocentric orbit, while an opposite orientation can reduce the tangential velocity and move the sail closer to the Sun. In the extreme case of a sail oriented parallel to the Sun’s rays, the solar pressure drops to zero and the sail will be pulled inward by the Sun’s gravity. Although providing typically low acceleration, the capability to perform such maneuvers without the use of onboard propellant makes solar sailing an attractive option for several types of missions. As discussed by Johnson et al.,[endnoteRef:96] solar sails can perform Heliophysics measurements from unique vantage points out of the ecliptic plane that can’t be readily reached with conventional propulsion systems; monitor the Sun to improve space weather predictions and provide timely hazard warnings for crewed space missions; provide sustained measurements of the Earth’s magnetotail as well as polar sitting and polar viewing observations; and advanced missions including rapid travel to near-interstellar space. </p>
<p></p>
<p>Solar sail demonstration missions have been flown, starting in 2010 with the IKAROS (Interplanetary Kite-craft Accelerated by Radiation Of the Sun) sail flown by the Japanese Aerospace Exploration Agency.[endnoteRef:97] Configured as a square with an area of 196 m2 and a polyimide resin membrane 7.5 microns thick, IKAROS was deployed on an interplanetary trajectory, demonstrating successful acceleration using solar pressure and attitude control using liquid crystal panels located at the edges of the sail that could change reflectivity through application of a small electric current. Also in 2010, NASA flew the NanoSail-D2 mission,[endnoteRef:98] deploying a 10 m2, 7.5-micron thick polymer sail from a 3U CubeSat into a 650-km low Earth orbit.[footnoteRef:2] The mission successfully demonstrated sail deployment and the effects of solar pressure on deceleration, reentering the atmosphere after 240 days. In 2016 the Planetary Society flew LightSail 1, a 32 m2, 4.5-micron thick sail followed in 2019 with the launch and deployment of the twin LightSail 2.[endnoteRef:99] Both sails successfully demonstrated CubeSat deployments and acceleration by solar radiation pressure, countering atmospheric drag for an extended period before eventually deorbiting; LightSat 2 also demonstrated sail control, changing its orientation up to 90o with respect to the Sun. In 2016 Canada flew the CanX-7 (Canadian Advanced Nanospace eXperiment-7) CubeSat[endnoteRef:100] that deployed four small drag sails (each about 1 m2 in area) to demonstrate accelerated orbital decay through atmospheric drag; though not a solar sail demonstration mission per se, the satellite successfully deployed the sail array and demonstrated accelerated orbital decay, reentering the atmosphere in April 2022. The NASA Near Earth Asteroid Scout (NEAScout) spacecraft, designed to use an 86 m2 solar sail to propel a 6U CubeSat on an asteroid flyby mission, was launched in 2022 but unfortunately communication with the spacecraft was lost following launch and the mission was terminated.[endnoteRef:101] NASA’s Solar Cruiser mission[endnoteRef:102] was designed to fly a 1653 m2, 2.5-micron thick polymide sail containing embedded photovoltaic cells for power generation and reflectivity controls for sail maneuvering to demonstrate station-keeping at the Sun-Earth L1 Lagrange point as well as changing the heliocentric inclination of the spacecraft; although successfully passing several reviews, the mission was not selected for further development and will be closed out in 2024 after testing to demonstrate the technology is sufficiently mature to be proposed for future space science missions. The NASA Advanced Composite Solar Sail System (ACS3)[endnoteRef:103] is scheduled to launch to low Earth orbit in 2024, with the key objectives of demonstrating the first deployment of a lightweight composite boom to support the 74 m2 sail and to evaluate sail performance using solar radiation to change its orbit. [97:  Tsuda, Y. et al., “Achievement of IKAROS — Japanese deep space solar sail demonstration mission,” Acta Astronautica Vol 82 (2), February 2013, pp. 183-188.]  [98:  Heaton, A., Faller, B, and Katan, C., “NanoSail – D Orbital and Attitude Dynamics,” https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140000644/downloads/20140000644.pdf. ]  [2:  The first NanoSail-D was lost in an unsuccessful Falcon 9 launch in 2008.]  [99:  The Planetary Society, “LightSail, a Planetary Society solar sail spacecraft;” https://www.planetary.org/sci-tech/lightsail. ]  [100:  CanX-7 (Canadian Advanced Nanospace eXperiment-7): https://www.eoportal.org/satellite-missions/canx-7#mission-status. ]  [101:  National Aeronautics and Space Administration, “NEAScout,” https://science.nasa.gov/mission/nea-scout/. ]  [102:  National Aeronautics and Space Administration, “Solar Cruiser: Enabling new vistas for Heliophysics Science,” https://science.nasa.gov/heliophysics/programs/technology/solar-cruiser/ ]  [103:  National Aeronautics and Space Administration, “NASA Facts: Advanced Composite Solar Sail System,” FS-2019-07-01-ARC, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190028916/downloads/20190028916.pdf. ] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>In addition to reflective sails, researchers are investigating diffractive sail concepts that offer potential benefits in reduced sail size and maneuvering efficiency. To perform orbit changes, reflective sails are canted with respect to the incident sunlight to produce a tangential force; however, the amount of sunlight falling on the sail is reduced by the cosine of the angle between the normal to the sail face and the Sun (0o when the sail is perpendicular to the Sun, 90o when oriented parallel to the Sun’s rays). As such the radiation pressure is equally diminished during these maneuvers, requiring large sail areas to maintain a sensible amount of acceleration. In contrast, a diffractive sail can preferentially change the diffraction angle of sunlight incident on the sail, maintaining the sail face perpendicular to the Sun while developing a net force with radial and tangential components. This configuration allows maximum use of the incident sunlight, potentially reducing the required sail area for a given acceleration and allowing for more efficient maneuvering through the use of addressable diffractive sail elements. Underpinned by detailed theoretical models, experimental work is currently underway to develop ultrathin diffractive films with surface structures composed of refractive index materials and birefringent polymeric liquid crystals to evaluate the performance of these new sail materials.[endnoteRef:104] [104:  Swartzlander Jr., G., Johnson, C., Kim, S., and Tabiryan, N., “Diffractive Lightsails,” NASA Innovative Advanced Concepts (NIAC) Phase II Final Report, NASA Grant No. 80NSSC19K0975, June 2022.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>At the distance of the Earth from the Sun, the pressure of sunlight pushing on a purely reflective sail is (Eqn. 8) approximately 9.1x10-6 N/m2; using the 86 m2 NEAScout sail as an example, the corresponding force would be around 0.8 mN, and for the total NEAScout vehicle mass of 14 kg, the resulting acceleration would be 5.6x10-5 m/s2.  Because the solar intensity drops as the inverse square of the distance from the Sun, the solar pressure and corresponding sail acceleration could be significantly increased if the sail were deployed closer to the Sun. Similar to the powered Oberth maneuvers discussed previously, the sail can be maneuvered such that the solar gravitational force overcomes the radiation pressure, and the sail falls inward to execute a close flyby around the Sun. At perihelion, the sail is maneuvered for maximum tangential acceleration, boosting its speed along its trajectory. This solar-powered gravitational assist can significantly boost the vehicle’s velocity at it travels away from the Sun. Known as “sun divers”, such maneuvers have been evaluated for rapid transits to the outer planets and near-interstellar space.[endnoteRef:105],[endnoteRef:106]  To complete such maneuvers requires significant advancements in sail materials to withstand the intense thermal environment encountered within a few solar radii; such metamaterials, including metallized high temperature substrates and metasurface dielectric films, are the subject of active research.[endnoteRef:107] With the potential to reach speeds of 300 km/s, these advanced sun diver sails can significantly reduce travel times for a number of exciting heliophysics and deep space missions, including flights to the 550 AU solar gravitational lens in less than a decade. However, even with such extreme speeds a trip to the nearest star would still take over 4 millennia. The challenge of providing an even higher acceleration to shorten the trip time leads to the consideration of beamed energy propulsion, where high power lasers or microwaves are used to rapidly accelerate a vehicle to fractions of the speed of light. These concepts are briefly discussed in the section below. [105:  Bailer-Jones, C., “The sun diver: Combining solar sails with the Oberth effect,” Am. J. Phys. 89, 235–243 (2021).]  [106:  Ancona, E.,  Kezerashvilib, R., and Matloff, G., “Exploring the Kuiper Belt with sun-diving solar sails,” Acta Astronautica, 160 601–605 (2019).]  [107:  Davoyan, A., “Extreme solar sailing for breakthrough space exploration,” NASA Innovative Advanced Concepts (NIAC) Phase II Annual Report, NASA Grant Number 80NSSC21K0954, July 2023.]

<p>As a simple example, assume the NEAScout vehicle is accelerated by a laser with a wavelength of 1064 nm at a power level of 100 MW. Assuming the laser is sent through a beam expander with a diameter of 1 m2, the range over which the laser completely fills the sail area of 86 m2 is roughly 3.3x107 m. The mass of the NEAScout is 14 kg, and from Eqn. 12 the acceleration produced by the laser incident on the sail is 0.048 m/s2, over 8500 times the acceleration provided by incident sunlight at 1 AU. The time it takes to traverse this distance at a constant acceleration of 0.048 m/s2 is roughly 3.7 x 104 sec, providing a sail velocity of approximately 1.8 km/s in a period of just over 10 hours. To increase the sail velocity the power of the laser could be increased, the sail diameter increased, or a combination of both; this scaling has led to the conceptual development of extremely high-power beam arrays on the ground and in space, with large gossamer space structures reflecting the incident beam momentum to provide the high accelerations required to traverse interstellar distances. </p>
<p></p>
<p></p>
<p>One of the first to provide a detailed analysis of laser and microwave power beaming for interstellar travel was Forward, who considered a microwave-powered flyby science mission to Alpha Centauri as well as a laser-powered roundtrip crewed mission to the nearest stars.[endnoteRef:110],[endnoteRef:111] The microwave powered Starwisp consists of a 1-km diameter aluminum wire mesh sail with a mass of just 16 grams carrying a 4-gram communications payload of microcircuits and optical detectors. A 10-GW microwave beam incident on the wire mesh accelerates the craft in a period of 10 hours to 20% light speed, reaching the star in 21.5 years, at which point the wire mesh serves as an antenna powered by the faint microwave energy of the distant transmitter, sending data back to Earth. To accomplish the mission the solar powered microwave transmitter is built as a Fresnel lens with a diameter of 50,000 km, roughly 4 times the diameter of the Earth. Increasing the transmitter power to 1-TW allows higher accelerations, and for a sail diameter of 30 km and a 9000 km diameter transmitter the acceleration period increases to approximately 18 hours and provides a relativistic speed of 0.5 c for the larger, 14-kg sail. The crewed round-trip laser light sail mission is even more daunting, requiring 43,000-TW of laser power beamed through a 1000-km diameter segmented ring lens, capable of sending a collimated beam of light roughly 44 lightyears before the beam begins to noticeably spread. The sail itself would be built in 3 sections; an inner payload sail 100 km in diameter, surrounded by a second ring shaped sail 320 km in diameter with a 100-km central hole, and surrounding that a third ring 1000 km in diameter with a 320 km inner hole. The whole structure is accelerated at 30% Earth gravity for a period of 1.6 years, reaching 50% the speed of light. The intended destination is the Epsilon Eridani star system at a distance of 10.5 light years, which would be reached in about 20 years Earth time (17 years crew time due to relativistic effects). At a distance of 0.4 lightyears from the star, the outer ring sail would separate, and the inner rings would turn to face the separated outer ring. Laser light (still collimated at this distance) would reflect from the outer ring onto the inner ring sails, slowing them down to place them into orbit within the Epsilon Eridani system. To return to Earth the second ring sail separates from the inner sail and reflects the laser beam onto the inner payload ring, accelerating the payload back toward Earth on a 30-year journey (due to the smaller sail size). Once in the vicinity of the solar system the laser system brings the sail to a halt, completing the roughly 51-year mission (46 years in elapsed crew time).  [110:  Forward, R., “Roundtrip Interstellar Travel Using Laser-Pushed Lightsails,” J. Spacecraft, Vol. 21 (2), March-April 1984, pp. 187-195.]  [111:  Forward, R., “Starwisp: An Ultra-Light Interstellar Probe,” J. Spacecraft, Vol. 22 (3), May-June 1985, pp. 345-350.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>Forward also considered requirements for a flyby mission of Alpha Centauri by a smaller science probe with a total mass of 1000 kg (1/3 of which is payload) and sail diameter of 3.6 km, powered by a 65-GW laser. As before, the laser is beamed through a large, 1000-km diameter lens to maintains full power on the sail over the acceleration distance, which is calculated to be approximately 0.17 lightyears. Maintaining an acceleration of the probe for a period of 3 years over this distance provides a final probe velocity of around 0.11 c, allowing the probe to complete the trip to Alpha Centauri. These are still enormous structures and power levels, but as winkingly noted by Forward,[endnoteRef:112] the basic physical principles are known and all that is required is “a lot of engineering and a lot of money.” The latter remaining a perpetual challenge, recent research has moved toward potentially more realistic if still daunting beamed propulsion concepts. Landis[endnoteRef:113] reevaluated Forward’s interstellar probe concept and determined that the use of thin dielectric films in place of the metallized aluminum sails proposed by Forward would allow higher sail temperatures and correspondingly higher power densities, providing higher accelerations and smaller sail sizes.  Using dielectric materials the acceleration can be increased to approximately 43 times the acceleration of gravity, nearly 1200 times higher than the acceleration achieved by the metallized sail; at this acceleration, the sail would reach 0.1 c in a little over a week. Keeping the same lens diameter would allow a significant decrease in the sail size by a factor of 1200, or conversely, keeping the sail size the same would reduce the size of the focusing lens by this amount. For the smaller sail size (assuming the payload is again 1/3 of the total spacecraft mass), the laser power would be reduced to around 448 MW, a significant improvement over the 65-GW required in the original concept. [112:  Forward, R., “Beamed Power Propulsion to the Stars,” in Advanced Space Propulsion Study, AFRL TR-87-070, Grant F04611-86-C-0039, Air Force Astronautics Laboratory, Edwards Air Force Base, CA, 1987.]  [113:  Landis, G., “Advanced Solar- and Laser-pushed Lightsail Concepts,” NASA Institute for Advanced Concepts, Phase I Final Report, May 1999.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>Building on current laser capabilities, Lubin[endnoteRef:114] provides a detailed study of both relativistic and non-relativistic directed energy missions assuming a square diffraction-limited phased laser array illuminating a square dielectric light sail and develops scaling relations for several key performance parameters. The comprehensive analysis includes considerations of payload imaging systems, communications, attitude control, dust impacts, and other aspects associated with an interstellar mission. Options for ground-based vs space-based deployment of the laser system are discussed, as well as the use of microwave and millimeter beams vs lasers. For an assumed laser array 10-km on a side beaming a power of 70-GW, Lubin evaluates several mission parameters for various payload sizes. Assuming the sail constitutes half the total mass, he finds that for a 1-g wafer-scale spacecraft with a 1-g, 0.85-m dielectric reflector, the wafer-craft will reach 0.14 c over a distance of 4x106 km in just over 3 minutes, with a whopping acceleration exceeding 104 times the acceleration of gravity. At this speed, a flyby mission to Alpha Centauri would take approximately 30 years. Under continuous illumination, allowing the beam to spread beyond the sail area, the spacecraft would achieve a final velocity of 20% light speed, reaching Alpha Centauri in approximately 20 years. For increasing values of payload and sail area, the corresponding velocities are reduced and travel times increased; a 100-g payload carried by a 100-g, 8.5-m sail would achieve 0.046 c in roughly 1.6 hours (0.064 c under continuous illumination), while a 10-kg probe propelled by a 10-kg, 85-m reflector would reach 0.014 c in roughly 6 months (0.02 c under continuous illumination). For crewed missions, assuming the same laser system, a 104 kg vehicle attached to a 104 kg, 2.7 km reflecting sail would have a small but steady acceleration over roughly 6 years, achieving a velocity of 0.0026 c for a trip time to Alpha Centauri of around 1,650 years (or 1,200 years under continuous illumination). Clearly the latter is too long for a crewed mission, but such journeys could become feasible with substantial increases in laser array sizes. Lubin concludes his study with a roadmap for technology development for wafer-class interstellar probe missions; this roadmap has been used to help inform the Breakthrough StarShot Initiative,[endnoteRef:115],[endnoteRef:116] a privately funded endeavor to send several small gram-size probes (“StarChips”) to Alpha Centauri within a generation using a 100-GW laser power station to sequentially illuminate the 4-m sail on each StarChip for short period of time, accelerating them to 0.2 c for a 20-year trip to the next star. During a flythrough of the Alpha Centauri system, the small but capable craft will survey the planet Proxima b orbiting Proxima Centauri, returning enough data to determine the planet’s atmospheric makeup and perhaps image large scale features in sufficient detail to determine oceans and large-scale continental features. Heller et al.[endnoteRef:117] evaluate options for slowing down at the target star using a combination of solar radiation pressure and gravitational assists, which increase the total mission time but offers the opportunity for a more leisurely evaluation of the star system; they estimate that probe velocities as high as 5.7% the speed of light can be slowed and captured by their proposed method, requiring a 75-year trip time but allowing more detailed analysis to be performed upon arrival. Finally, Lubin and Cohen[endnoteRef:118] have recently investigated the economics of interstellar flight, deriving an analytical cost model driven by the physics and scalability of the directed energy system. They note that the combination of modular and scalable designs, combined with the exponential growth in the capability of photonics, allows for the economic scalability of near-future systems; a promising result as we look to send our robotic emissaries out the stars. [114:  Lubin, P., “A Roadmap to Interstellar Flight,” NASA Innovative Advanced Concepts (NIAC) Program Phase I Final Report, NASA Grant NNX16AL32G S007, January 2016. See also NASA Technical Report Server, Document 20190001185, 2019.]  [115:  Breakthrough StarShot website: https://breakthroughinitiatives.org/initiative/3 ]  [116:  Popkin, G., “What it would take to reach the stars,” Nature Vol. 542, 20–22 (2017);  https://doi.org/10.1038/542020a]  [117:  Heller, R., Hippke, M., and Kervella, P., “ Optimized Trajectories to the Nearest Stars Using Lightweight High-velocity Photon Sails,” The Astronomical Journal, 154, pp. 115-126, September 2017.]  [118:  Lubin, P. and Cohen, A., “The Economics of Interstellar Flight,” arXiv:2112.13911 [econ.GN], https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2112.13911. ] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h3>Particle and Pellet Beams</h3>
<p></p>
<p>We close this chapter with a consideration of directed particle and pellet beams to provide momentum to a target spacecraft. As previously noted, photons carry momentum that can be imparted to a sail to provide thrust; however, generating sufficiently high velocities requires large sails, significant amounts of directed energy, or both. Replacing the solar or laser photons with beams of energetic particles or pellets can also impart a substantial momentum, and several such concepts have been proposed and studied for rapid deep space and interstellar travel. These are briefly discussed below.</p>
<p></p>
<h4>Magnetic Sails</h4>
<p></p>
<p>A concept reminiscent of the solar sails discussed above is the Magnetic Sail (MagSail) proposed by Andrews and Zubrin,[endnoteRef:119] that uses the magnetic field created by a large, current-carrying superconducting coil to deflect charged solar wind particles (protons and electrons) to produce thrust. The average solar wind proton number density and speed at 1 AU are approximately 7x106/m3 and 450 km/s, respectively; although the momentum per unit area is less than that produced by solar photons, the complexities of fabricating and deploying large, lightweight sails are replaced by the conceptually simpler current loop whose magnetic field can extend well beyond the coil area. The amount and direction of thrust produced depends on the angle of the MagSail relative to the solar wind, the area of the superconducting loop, and the strength of the magnetic field produced by the current in the coil. Although the energy of the solar wind particles remains fairly constant as they move away from the Sun, the particle density falls off as the inverse square of the distance and, as with solar sails, the force acting on the MagSail will be commensurately reduced as it moves away from the Sun. In their original paper, Andrews and Zubrin calculate that for a 500-tonne, 100-km radius loop operating at 1-AU, with a peak central magnetic field of 10-5 T, the interaction with the solar wind will generate a force of 1980 N on the coil, producing an acceleration of around 0.004 m/s2. Over the period of a year, the coil can achieve a velocity approaching 126 km/s, which is useful for interplanetary missions but too low for reasonable interstellar trip times. Similar to solar sails, the performance of the magnetic sail could be improved by energizing the sail during a close perihelion maneuver, although the thermal issues flying a superconducting coil close to the Sun would need to be addressed. Even still, as with solar sails the resulting velocity would be too low for rapid interstellar transport. As noted by Andrews and Zubrin, an option is to instead use the MagSail for braking on approach to the star, using the stellar wind to slow the craft for a rendezvous vs. flyby mission. Additional, detailed mathematical models describing the interactions between the magnetic field and solar wind for single loop MagSails have since been developed, to which the interested reader is referred.[endnoteRef:120]  [119:  Andrews, D. and Zubrin, R., “Magnetic Sails and Interstellar Travel”, Journal of The British Interplanetary Society, Vol 43, pp. 265-272,1990]  [120:  Bassettoa, M., Perakis, N., Quarta, A., and Mengali, G., “Refined MagSail thrust model for preliminary mission design and trajectory optimization,” Aerospace Science and Technology, 133(2023) 108113; https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ast.2023.108113  ] 
</p>
<p></p>
<p>As discussed above, the acceleration and final velocity of solar sails can be dramatically increased using high power microwave and laser systems. Similarly, the performance of the MagSail could be improved using a high energy particle beam directed at the sail. As noted by Landis,[endnoteRef:121] particle beam sails have several potential advantages over solar sails for interstellar travel. In addition to the potentially lower mass of the loop versus large diameter solar sail, the magnetic field itself creates a large target that makes aiming the beam and issues with beam divergence easier to solve. Physical sails have material temperature limitations that in turn limit the incident power of the incoming laser light; there are no such restrictions for the magnetic field, allowing significantly higher beam power densities than can be achieved with physical sails. This in turn increases the acceleration, with the sail reaching the required velocity over a shorter distance (again benefiting beam divergence and stabilization concerns). A particle beam carries significantly more momentum per particle than does a photon beam, transferring more force to the sail at higher efficiency. Finally, unlike laser beams, particle beams are not diffraction limited, allowing potentially smaller sail sizes. Particle beams would however need to be neutralized to avoid electrostatic expansion, and thermal spread would need to be accounted for over large distances. <p>To complete the discussion of the magnetic sail concept, it is noted that the charged, highly energetic particles created during a nuclear explosion could also be used to push against the MagSail, similar to the nuclear Orion concept discussed earlier. This concept, termed MagOrion,[endnoteRef:124] uses small kT-class nuclear devices detonates approximately 2-km behind the spacecraft to create a rapidly expanding plasma, about half of which can be turned into useful thrust. While high specific impulse values of 15,000 – 45,000 s could be achieved, the challenge of launching and transporting multiple nuclear explosives, combined with the engineering challenges of material survivability over a long duration mission, poses similar issues for the MagOrion as for the original Orion concept. A variation on this concept, dubbed Mini-MagOrion, instead beams particles consisting of fissionable material with a deuterium/tritium core to the spacecraft, where they are rapidly compressed by a z-pinch to ignite and generate a highly energetic charged plasma to push against the magnetic field.[endnoteRef:125] Though faced with daunting engineering challenges in terms of beam propagation, capture and compression, this approach circumvents the issues associated with launching nuclear explosives into space and offers a path toward achieving the high velocities necessary for interstellar travel. [124:  Andrews, D. and Zubrin, R., “Nuclear Device Pushed Magnetic Sails (MagOrion)” 33rd Joint Propulsion Conference and Exhibit, Seattle WA, July 6-9, 1997; https://doi.org/10.2514/6.1997-3072. ]  [125:  Lenard, R. and Andrews, D., “Use of Mini-Mag Orion and superconducting coils for near-term interstellar transportation,” Acta Astronautica 61 (2007) 450 – 458.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h4>Pellet Streams</h4>
<p></p>
<p>Similar to particle beams, streams of more massive pellets can also be used to rapidly accelerate a payload. Singer[endnoteRef:126] proposed the use of an electromagnetic launcher, roughly 105 km long, located in space; superconducting pellets with masses of a few grams up to 100 grams would be launched at high acceleration (106 times the acceleration of gravity) to impact a target vehicle. While high, these accelerations have been achieved in terrestrial rail guns over much shorter distances. Singer’s proposed mission was to send a 450 tonne payload to Barnard’s Star, a distance of 5.6 light years, in 50 years (comparable to the Daedalus fusion powered mission discussed earlier in this chapter). Magnetic fields on the target vehicle would be used to reflect the superconducting pellets, providing momentum to the spacecraft. To maintain a collimated beam, the accelerator would be built in multiple sections with trim coils to fine tune the pellet launch direction. Several collimating stations would be placed along the beam path (potentially deployed by the target vehicle along its journey) to monitor and correct the pellet trajectories using electric or magnetic fields. The accelerator power required for the mission was 15-TW (averaged over a firing interval), launching a 2.8-g pellet every 0.5 seconds at an exit velocity of 0.25 c, for a period of 3 years. Less stringent mission requirements showed that a 10-tonne mass could be sent to Alpha Centauri in 130 years using less than a GW of continuous beam power.  [126:  Singer, C., “Interstellar Propulsion Using a Pellet Stream for Momentum Transfer,” Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory Report PPPL-1583, October 1979; also, J British Interplanetary Society, Vol. 33, 1980, pp.107-116.] 
</p>
<p></p>
<h4>SailBeam</h4>
<p></p>
<p>Bridging the gap between laser lightsails, MagSails and pellet propulsion, the SailBeam concept uses multi-GW laser beams to push small dielectric lightsails (typically 10-cm in diameter with a mass of a few milligrams) to speeds approaching 0.1 c. Millions of these small sails impact the target vehicle, transferring momentum to the vehicle until it asymptotically reaches the sail velocity. The target vehicle uses an onboard laser to vaporize the incoming sails, creating a high velocity plasma that pushes against the vehicle’s MagSail to provide thrust; the MagSail is also used to a brake against the stellar wind when the vehicle reaches its destination star. A detailed analysis of the concept developed by Kare[endnoteRef:127]  considers multiple factors in the design of the system, including the constitution of the dielectric sails, tensile and material limits, sail stability and guidance, MagSail designs, and laser power and optics requirements. A parametric analysis was performed for a nominal sail mission, assuming a 2000-kg payload mass, 2000-kg MagSail mass, and a final vehicle velocity of 0.1 c. The analysis assumed 8.77x107 sails, each with a mass of 23-milligrams, would be accelerated to a velocity of 0.12 c using a high-power laser, with power levels ranging from 35-GW to 180-GW depending on sail material, areal density, etc. The laser beam would be collimated by focusing optics, providing an acceleration length of 64,800 km and an illumination time per sail of around 3.6 s to provide a sail acceleration of 1x107 m/s2. For these parameters a 0.38-m diameter, two-layered diamond film sail with 98.9% reflectivity offered the best sail performance and required the lowest laser power (35-GW) to meet the mission parameters. With this performance the vehicle would reach Alpha Centauri in just over 40 years, where it would use its MagSail to slow and capture into the system. Options considered by Kare include the use of multiple lower power lasers with multiple focusing optics to reduce the development demands on the beam system. Kare concludes his study with a technology roadmap, defining the key system elements to be developed as well as near-term experiments that could be performed to advance the readiness of the constituent technologies. [127:  Kare, J., “High-acceleration Micro-scale Laser Sails for Interstellar Propulsion,” NASA Innovative Advanced Concepts Phase I Final Report, NIAC Grant #07600-070, December 2001 (Revised Feb. 2002).] 
</p>
<h1>Concluding Remarks</h1>
<p></p>
<p>Reaching the next star system in decades or even centuries vs. millennia precludes the use of conventional and near-term chemical and nuclear propulsion systems; the mass penalty associated with carrying onboard propellant is too severe to make these systems a feasible option. Fusion ramjets that take advantage of the sparse hydrogen density diffused within the interstellar medium appear to be well beyond our engineering capabilities, and the amount of antimatter required to make an interstellar journey would require a dedicated large-scale production effort which is prohibitively expensive using current approaches. Concepts that move the propulsion system off the vehicle, such as laser, microwave, and particle/pellet beams, can rapidly accelerate small payloads to the velocities required for reasonable interstellar trip times, at the expense of significant infrastructure on the ground or in space. As noted at the start of this chapter, getting to the next star is incredibly hard given the enormous distance and the amount of energy that must be provided to traverse that distance in relevant time scales. Our Voyager probes will cover an equivalent distance in approximately 75,000 years, and dedicated researchers will continue to design methods to get us there faster by pushing toward evermore energetic propulsion concepts. The enduring quest to reach the stars embodies the ultimate human spirit of exploration; it is ours to make this bold venture a reality.</p>
