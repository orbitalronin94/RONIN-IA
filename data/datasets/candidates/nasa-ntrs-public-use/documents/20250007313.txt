<p>Artificial Neural Networks for Solving Forward and
Inverse Kinematics on a 7 Degree-of-Freedom
</p>
<p>Robotic Manipulator
Aanchal L. Save
</p>
<p>Amentum Space Exploration Division, Huntsville, Alabama
Prepared for George C. Marshall Space Flight Center, National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>Under Contract 80MSFC18C0011 Task EC-EV43-10-01-MTID-AIG-01.
</p>
<p>Abstract—Traditionally, forward or direct kinematics refers
to computing the end-effector pose of a robotic arm given its
joint angles, while conversely, inverse kinematics is used to find
the joint angles needed to reach a desired end-effector pose.
Unlike forward kinematics, inverse kinematics lacks a general
deterministic solution and becomes increasingly complex as the
number of degrees of freedom (DoF) grows. While Artificial
Neural Networks (ANNs) have shown promise in solving inverse
kinematics for low-DoF systems, their accuracy diminishes in
higher-DoF configurations. This study presents a neural network-
based approach for both forward and inverse kinematics on a
7-DoF robotic arm, detailing the data generation process, model
architecture, and training methodology. The forward kinematics
model is used to iteratively validate the inverse kinematics model,
a unique solution that allows the model to achieve a median
accuracy of 9.95 centimeters.
</p>
<p>I. INTRODUCTION
</p>
<p>Solving forward and inverse kinematics for robotics systems
is integral for spacial recognition for a robotic manipulator.
Forward kinematics refers to computing the pose of the end-
effector of the arm given its joint angles, and is always
deterministic, though increasing the number of joints can make
it more complex [1]. Conversely, inverse kinematics is a fun-
damental problem in robotics, involving the determination of
joint angles required to achieve a desired end-effector pose [2].
While traditional methods for solving inverse kinematics are
well-established [3], they become increasingly complex and
computationally intensive as the number of degrees of freedom
(DoF) increases. High-DoF manipulators, such as those with
7 DoF, present significant challenges due to their redundancy
and the non-linear nature of their kinematics. With 7 degrees
of freedom, there are an infinite number of ways to achieve
a desired pose, which relates to position and orientation. This
causes the inverse kinematics problem to become increasingly
complex [4]. The existing inverse kinematics solutions for
these advanced manipulators are computationally intense and
require complex calculations for real-time use [5].
</p>
<p>Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) have been explored as a
potential solution for inverse kinematics, particularly for low-
DoF systems, [6] [7] and are proving to have strong ability to
approximate the solution in systems with six DoF and lower.
However, there are few solutions noting the application of
ANNs to high-DoF manipulators and these solutions can be
improved upon for real-time use.
</p>
<p>This study aims to address these challenges by develop-
ing a neural network-based approach for both forward and
inverse kinematics on a 7-DoF robotic arm. The proposed
method includes a data generation process, model architecture
design, and a training methodology. By leveraging the forward
kinematics model to iteratively validate the inverse kinematics
model, this unique approach has achieved a median accuracy
of 9.95 centimeters.
</p>
<p>II. METHODS
</p>
<p>A. KINOVA® Gen3 Ultra lightweight robot- 7-DoF
</p>
<p>The Kinova Gen 3 robotic arm used for this analysis is a
7 degree-of-freedom manipulator, with a 902 mm maximum
reach. The repeatability for the robot is 1 millimeter with
an uncertainty of 2 standard deviations [8]. The actuators
each possess an 360 degree range of rotation, allowing the
manipulator to reach a wide variety of positions. The seven
DoF provide redundancy in the case of a kinematic singularity,
which is a specific configuration of the manipulator, where due
to the alignment of its joints, the robot loses a DoF [9].
</p>
<p>B. MATLAB Setup
</p>
<p>To collect data from a simulated model of the manipulator,
the MATLAB robotics system toolbox was utilized, loading
the Version 12 model of the Kinova Gen3 robotic arm and the
corresponding URDF file, as shown in Fig. 1. The gravitational
constant for the simulation was set to -9.81m/s2.
</p>
<p>Fig. 1: Kinova Gen3 Manipulator in MATLAB using Robotics
System Toolbox</p>
<p></p>
</div>

linear patterns between data inputs and outputs. They are
universal approximators and can approximate virtually any
function of interest. ANNs consist of multiple interconnected
neurons (nodes) organized in layers [10].
</p>
<p>In this study, multilayer-feedforward regression neural net-
works were developed and used in PyTorch using supervised
learning to gain approximations of our desired outputs.
</p>

<p>III. APPROACH
</p>
<p>A. Forward Kinematics
</p>
<p>1) Data Collection: The data collection for forward kine-
matics, entailed generating seven pseudo-random numbers in
the range of [0, 360] degrees using the rand() funtion in MAT-
LAB. This emulated the current joint angles of the robotic arm.
Then, using the robotics system toolbox in MATLAB to obtain
the transformation matrix between the base of the manipulator
and the end-effector. This matrix was then converted into a
pose (x, y, z, θx, θy, θz), with the last three angles representing
</p>
<p>the orientation of the end effector. The seven joint angle values
were the inputs to the model, and the pose was the output.
In order to train the model to the desired accuracy, 500,000
datapoints were collected. Fig. 2 shows the data collection
pipeline and how it will interface with the neural network.
</p>
<p>Fig. 2: Forward Kinematics Data Collection and Neural Net-
work Architecture
</p>
<p>2) Data Processing: Because the data was scaled differ-
ently: input angular data ranged from 0 to 360 degrees, output
position data ranged from about -2 to 2 meters, and output
angular data ranged from -180 to 180, the model would place
higher emphasis on errors in the larger scaled features, and the
training would be imbalanced. To counteract this, a min-max
scaler was used to scale the inputs to [0, 1] and the outputs to
[-1,1]. These were scaled differently because the inputs were
solely positive values, while the negative values in the output
denoted direction and were important. The following is the
equation used to scale the inputs to [0,1].
</p>
<p>X ′ =

</p>

</p>

minimum and maximum values of the feature, and X ′ is the
scaled value.
</p>
<p>3) Model Architecture: The architecture when the forward
kinematics model training began was a network with 2 fully
connected hidden layers, with 16 and 12 neurons respectively.
It was soon discovered that the data was extremely complex,
and the number of inputs and outputs were extremely large, so
more neurons and layers were needed to effectively learn the
relationships between all of the features. After iteration, the
network consisted of 1 input layer with 7 neurons, 4 hidden
layers with 2048, 1024, 512, and 256 neurons, and 1 output
layer with 6 neurons.
</p>
<p>Initially, the hyperbolic tangent, or Tanh, activation func-
tion was implemented between the layers, which is used to
introduce non-linearity into the network and allows it to learn
relationships between data that are not strictly linear. The Tanh
formula is:
</p>
<p>Tanh(x) =

</p>

</p>
<p>This is better for this application than the commonly used
logistic activation function because it allows for both negative
and positive inputs. As training began, the MSE value would
plateau at around 0.119. This issue was because of vanishing</p>

<p>gradients, a common occurrence with the Tanh function due

activation function was then switched to the Leaky Rectified
Linear Unit function, or LeakyReLU, which doesn’t have the
same asymptotic behavior, and still allows for the propagation
of negative values from one layer to another [12], as seen in
Fig. 3.
</p>

<p>4) Model Training: Once the architecture was finalized, the
model was trained using batch, or offline learning, for 2000
epochs. The train-validate-test structure at 90% -8.3% - 1.7%
was used to split the data, and the model was trained on a
Nvidia RTX A4000 Graphics Processing Unit (GPU). During
training, the training MSE converged to 0.008, while the test
MSE was 0.019. The graph of validation MSE vs Epoch is
seen in Fig. 4.
</p>
<p>Fig. 4: Forward kinematics model training graph
</p>
<p>5) Results and Accuracy: When testing, the forward kine-
matics neural network that was developed proved to correctly
predict the end-effector position based on the joint angles with
an median error of 2.68 centimeters when run in MATLAB,
proving to be a computationally effective way to produce the
transformation matrices and for localizing the end-effector.
Fig. 5 shows an example of the results in simulation. The
joint angles shown for the arm in that configuration were
inputted into the model, and the point in the figure is what
the model predicted as the location of the end effector. The
orientation predictions are also accurate, as the model can
</p>
<p>correctly predict the orientation of the end-effector given the
current joint angles.
</p>

</p>
<p>Fig. 6 shows the positional error in centimeters of the
predicted end-effector pose from the actual position of the
end-effector in that configuration. The median error is 2.68
cm after 600 trials.
</p>

</p>
<p>B. Inverse Kinematics
</p>
<p>1) Original Trial: To solve the inverse kinematics problem
using artificial neural networks, it was initially attempted to
simply reverse the data collected earlier, using the six outputs
for position as the model’s inputs, and using the seven inputs
for joint angles as the model’s outputs. However, because there
are a greater number of outputs to the model than inputs, there
are an infinite number of solutions for joint angles to achieve
each goal position. This caused the model to repeatedly</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>predict extremely close to the mean, so the resulting values
were all close to 180 degrees, resulting in almost identical
inaccurate solutions for various desired output positions. Given
the outcome of this methodology, an alternative approach was
needed to achieve the desired results.
</p>
<p>2) Data Collection: Rather than the inputs to the model
simply being the six measurements for the goal position,
the inputs were expanded to include the current joint angle
configuration of the robot as well. This mitigated the greater
number of outputs than inputs that occurred in the previous
method, and allows for the most optimal joint angles based on
the current configuration of the manipulator.
</p>
<p>To collect data, the seven measurements for current joint
angles were pseudo-randomly generated to be in the range
of [0, 360] degrees, as they were for the forward-kinematics
solution. The goal positions for the model were all positions
that were found to be within the reach of the robot from the
data that was used for the previous forward kinematics model.
</p>
<p>Given this starting configuration and the goal pose inputs,
the MATLAB Robotics System Toolbox Inverse Kinematics
solver was used generate data for the angle measurements
required to reach the target pose. This solver used the Broyden-
Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) Gradient Projection for-
mula, which is the default Inverse Kinematics solver in
MATLAB. The initial standard weights for the solver were
[1 1 1 0.8 0.8 0.8], and the initial guess was the random
current joint angles. The BFGS Optimization Algorithm is a
Quasi-Newtonian method, approximating the inverse of the
Hessian matrix using second derivative information, such as
the gradients of the cost function from past iterations [13].
The Gradient Projection method is used to deal with limits on
joint angle limits and so that the direction of search is valid
[14].
</p>
<p>For accurate training, 100,000 data points were collected,
with the inputs being seven current joint angle measurements,
six measurements for the target pose of the arm, and the
outputs being the seven joint angles that would allow the
robot to reach the target position and orientation. Fig. 7 shows
the data collection pipeline and how it will interface with the
neural network.
</p>
<p>Fig. 7: Forward Kinematics Data Collection and Neural Net-
work Architecture
</p>
<p>3) Data Processing: The data was scaled in a similar
fashion as it was for the forward kinematics problem. The min-
max scaler was utilized with the positive data being scaled to
the range of [0, 1] and the negative data being scaled to [-1,
1].
</p>
<p>4) Model Architecture: The architecture that was used for
the forward kinematics problem was found to be greatly
overfitting the data, learning the training data extremely well,
but performing poorly on the test data. The number of hidden
layers and neurons were decreased until overfitting stopped,
resulting in a network with an input layer of 13 neurons, 3
hidden layers with 512, 1024, and 256 neurons respectively,
and an output layer of 7 neurons.
</p>
<p>Another method to decrease overfitting was one dimensional
batch normalization, forcing the model to normalize the out-
puts of each activation before the next layer, allowing for better
convergence while training [15].
</p>
<p>Dropout layers were added with a dropout parameter of 0.5
to further decrease overfitting. This randomly deactivates 50%
of neurons each pass, reducing the dependence of the model
on specific neurons in training, and distributing the weights
more evenly [16].
</p>
<p>5) Model Training: The inverse kinematics model was
trained on the same GPU and with the same offline learning
structure as the forward kinematics solution, as well as the
same train-validate-test split. This model was trained for 1000
epochs, and the MSE converged to 0.12, which is not as
low as would be ideal, however the accuracy is improved in
comparison to other studies which converged to 0.32 [17]. The
graph of validation MSE vs Epoch is seen in Fig. 8.
</p>
<p>Fig. 8: Inverse kinematics model training graph
</p>
<p>6) Results and Accuracy: When tested in simulation, the
inverse kinematics solution did not perform well enough on
the test inputs to be used in a real world setting. Fig. 9 shows
an example of the results in simulation. The point shown in
the figure is the target position for the end-effector, and the
configuration of the arm shown in the figure set to is the angles
that the model outputted. In some cases, the model predicts a
close solution, or else one joint is inaccurate while the others
are able to give the correct solution.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Fig. 9: Inverse kinematics results in simulation
</p>
<p>Fig. 10 shows the positional error in centimeters of the end-
effector at the predicted joint angles from the goal position of
the end-effector. The median error is 71.68 cm after 400 trials.
This error isn’t at all low enough for this solution to be used
in a production setting.
</p>
<p>Fig. 10: Inverse kinematics error scatter plot
</p>
<p>C. Combination for Iterative Validation
</p>
<p>1) Architecture: To improve the accuracy of the ANN-
based inverse-kinematics solution, it is combined with the ac-
curate forward-kinematics neural network that was developed
for iterative validation of its output.
</p>
<p>Fig. 11: Architecture for combining forward and inverse
kinematics for iterative validation
</p>
<p>As shown in Fig. 11, the goal pose and the starting position
are inputted into the inverse kinematics model, and the output
is validated against the goal using the forward kinematics
model. If it is under a five centimeter error tolerance, that
solution is valid and returned, else those joint angles are fed
back in as the starting position for the inverse-kinematics
model, and this is iterated. Inputting different starting angles
each iteration allows for the inverse kinematics model to give
different outputs and converge towards the closest solution by
outputting increasingly accurate starting joint angle values for
its next iteration. If the model is unable to find a solution
within ten seconds, which was an arbitrary amount of time
for this test, the closest solution to the goal pose is returned.
</p>
<p>D. Results and Accuracy
</p>
<p>In simulation, this was found to be extremely accurate. The
validation from the forward kinematics model allowed the
inverse kinematics to predict the joint angles needed to reach
the desired pose with an median error of 9.95 centimeters. Fig.
12 shows an example of the results in simulation. The point
shown in the figure is the target position for the end-effector,
and the configuration of the arm shown in the figure is set to
the angles that the model outputted.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Fig. 12: Combination results in simulation
</p>
<p>Fig. 13 shows the positional error in centimeters of the end-
effector at the predicted joint angles from the goal position of
the end-effector. The median error is 9.95 centimeters after
600 trials.
</p>
<p>Fig. 13: Inverse kinematics error scatter plot with forward
kinematics validation
</p>
<p>IV. RESULTS
</p>
<p>The developed neural networks to solve forward and in-
verse kinematics problems, and then the combination of the
two models for validation of the control allows for accurate
solutions to these problems. The forward kinematics network
had a median positional error of 2.68 centimeters, and when
combined with the inverse kinematics model, it was able to
solve complex inverse kinematics problems with a median
error of 9.95 centimeters. This solution allows for more
computationally efficient inverse kinematics solutions, without
the need for real-time error and gradient calculations, as the
model is already trained on specific architecture and data from
</p>
<p>the arm, and therefore simply needs to iterate to validate
its calculations. This decreases the number of computations
that would be needed in a real-world application of inverse
kinematics.
</p>
<p>V. CONCLUSION
</p>
<p>The complex problem of solving inverse kinematics for a
high DoF manipulator, in this case seven DoF, was addressed.
The approach used deep neural networks to solve both the
forward and inverse kinematics problem, and combined the
two models for iterative validation, achieving an accuracy of
9.95 centimeters. This significantly reduces the computations
needed to solve inverse kinematics problems, allowing it
to be suitable for applications with limited computational
allocations.
</p>
<p>The results demonstrate that the neural network-based ap-
proach provides a viable solution for high-DoF manipula-
tors, offering improved accuracy and efficiency. However, the
model’s performance could be further enhanced by increasing
the dataset size and exploring alternative neural network
architectures.
</p>
<p>Future work could involve exploring various other network
architectures, improving simulation data collection methods,
and focusing on model efficiency. Overall, this study con-
tributes to the advancement of neural network applications
in robotics, paving the way for more efficient and accurate
kinematic solutions.
</p>
