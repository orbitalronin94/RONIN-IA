<p>Paper for Space Cryogenics Workshop 2023 
</p>
<p>Two-dimensional network flow modeling of no-vent tank filling of a cryogenic 
</p>
<p>tank with thermo-dynamic vent system assisted injector 
</p>
<p>Alok Majumdar, Andre LeClair 
</p>
<p>NASA Marshall Space Center, Huntsville, AL 35812 
</p>
<p>Jason Hartwig 
</p>
<p>NASA/Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 44135 
</p>
<p> S. Mostafa Ghiaasiaan 
</p>
<p>Georgia Institute of Technology; Atlanta, GA 30332-0405 
</p>
<p>ABSTRACT 
</p>
<p>A finite volume-based network flow modeling tool, Generalized Fluid System Simulation Program 
</p>
<p>(GFSSP), was used to simulate the no-vent filling of a cryogenic tank with a thermodynamic vent 
</p>
<p>system assisted injector. The tank was discretized into nodes in the radial and axial directions to 
</p>
<p>calculate two-dimensional axisymmetric flow with heat transfer between the solid wall and fluid. 
</p>
<p>The nodes are connected by branches where momentum equations are solved to calculate the 
</p>
<p>flowrates. The mass and energy conservation equations and the equation of state are solved to 
</p>
<p>calculate pressure, temperature, and resident mass at the nodes. The energy conservation equations 
</p>
<p>at the solid nodes were solved to calculate the temperature of the solid wall. The system of 
</p>
<p>equations was solved by a combination of successive substitution and simultaneous Newton-
</p>
<p>Raphson method. The numerical model accounts for a) different regimes of pool boiling heat 
</p>
<p>transfer, b) condensation of vapor around the spray droplets, c) condensation of vapor at the 
</p>
<p>interface of the cooled injector and ullage, and d) condensation at the liquid-vapor interphase. The 
</p>
<p>numerical predictions of tank pressure, filling rate, and wall temperatures were compared with the 
</p>
<p>test data. The paper also presents the effect of nodal discretization by comparing the results of the 
</p>
<p>1D and 2D models and examines the effect of the droplet diameter on tank filling.    
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>1. Introduction 
</p>
<p>On orbit transfer of cryogenic liquids is necessary for many future space missions. Filling a tank 
</p>
<p>with cryogenic fluid is more challenging than filling a tank with water or any other fluid that is in 
</p>
<p>liquid state at atmospheric conditions. Filling a tank with cryogenic fluid is a two-step process. 
</p>
<p>First the tank and the transfer line must be chilled. Liquid cryogens start flowing into the tank at a 
</p>
<p>significant rate only after the tank and transfer lines are chilled to the fluid saturation temperature. 
</p>
<p>In normal gravity, cryogenic tanks are usually filled from the bottom at nearly atmospheric 
</p>
<p>pressure. The vapor generated due to heat transfer from the warm tank walls is allowed to vent 
</p>
<p>from the top of the tank while the tank is being filled. Filling a cryogenic tank in the absence of 
</p>
<p>gravity is more challenging because in a non-stratified environment, liquid propellant may not 
</p>
<p>settle at the tank bottom as it does on earth. There is a strong possibility that liquid propellant may </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>exit through the vent valve, which is typically located at the top of the tank to vent propellant 
</p>
<p>vapor. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>To reduce the loss of precious propellant during tank filling, a “charge-hold-vent” (CHV) method 
</p>
<p>[1] was developed. CHV is a cyclic process where the tank wall temperature is reduced to the 
</p>
<p>saturation temperature of cryogenic propellant by extracting energy from the wall by repeated 
</p>
<p>injections of the liquid propellant followed by a hold and vent process. Once the tank wall reaches 
</p>
<p>the saturation temperature, the tank is filled with a closed vent valve. A simpler method is that of 
</p>
<p>Vented-Chill / No-Vent-Fill (VCNVF) [2].  In this method, the tank’s vent valve is kept open for 
</p>
<p>an initial period while the tank walls are chilled with a spray of cryogenic liquid that boils to vapor 
</p>
<p>and exits through the vent.  When the tank walls have been sufficiently chilled, the vent valve is 
</p>
<p>closed, and the tank is filled with liquid.  Care must be taken with the timing of the vent closure.  
</p>
<p>If the vent is closed too early, residual heat in the walls may drive sufficient boil-off to raise the 
</p>
<p>tank pressure high enough to stall the inlet flow.  If the vent is closed too late, some liquid 
</p>
<p>propellant may be lost out the vent. Compared to the CHV method, VCNVF is less mass efficient; 
</p>
<p>the gas being vented during the chill may not be extracting the maximum possible thermal energy 
</p>
<p>from the tank wall.  There is always the risk that some of the precious liquid propellant will be 
</p>
<p>vented.  However, VCNVF is much simpler from an operational standpoint.  The transfer lines 
</p>
<p>need to be chilled down only once and there is less cycling of inlet and vent valves.  Since there is 
</p>
<p>no hold period, overall loading time is likely to be shorter. 
</p>
<p>The limitation of the VCNVF method to find an optimum target temperature was overcome by the 
</p>
<p>No Vent Fill (NVF) method [2] where a TVS (Thermodynamic Vent System) augmented injector 
</p>
<p>was used with the vent valve closed during the entire filling process. In a TVS-augmented injector, 
</p>
<p>the liquid flow splits into two streams:  the minor stream is routed to a Joule-Thomson (J-T) orifice 
</p>
<p>where the flow immediately flashes from liquid to vapor or two phase mixture, because the 
</p>
<p>downstream of the J-T leg is maintained at vacuum level; the primary stream is injected into the 
</p>
<p>tank after being cooled by the cold vapor of the J-T leg in a heat exchanger. The flow through the 
</p>
<p>J-T leg is also used to cool the outer metal matrix of the injector which in turn cools the vapor in 
</p>
<p>the tank ullage. The cooling of vapor in the ullage by the cold injector surface reduces the ullage 
</p>
<p>pressure, allowing liquid to enter and fill the tank. 
</p>
<p>An accurate simulation of no vent tank chill and fill is necessary to estimate the amount of 
</p>
<p>propellant that is needed to chill the tank, determine the maximum sustainable flowrate to fill the 
</p>
<p>tank without stalling, and establish a timeline for the filling process. Numerical modeling of filling 
</p>
<p>a cryogenic tank is complex because the flow is unsteady two phase and the process involves both 
</p>
<p>boiling and condensation with heat transfer between solid and fluid. In recent years, progress has 
</p>
<p>been made to develop a numerical modeling technique to simulate CHV [3], VCNVF [4] and NVF 
</p>
<p>[5,6] methods of tank filling and verifying the numerical predictions by comparing with the test 
</p>
<p>data of Chato et al [1] and Hartwig et al [2]. The numerical technique was incorporated into 
</p>
<p>NASA’s Generalized Fluid System Simulation Program (GFSSP) [7]. 
</p>
<p>GFSSP was used [3] to model CHV process by discretizing the tank into multiple nodes with 
</p>
<p>conjugate heat transfer between tank wall and fluid nodes. The predicted propellant consumption </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>and wall temperature compared well with the test data measured at K-Site at NASA/GRC [1]. The 
</p>
<p>VCNVF test carried out at NASA/MSFC [2] using CRYOTE tank was also modeled [4] using 
</p>
<p>GFSSP’s multi-node modeling approach. The pool boiling curve was introduced in the model 
</p>
<p>along with a simplified condensation model at droplet-ullage interphase. The predicted tank 
</p>
<p>pressure, filling rate and wall temperatures compared well with the test data. The multi-node model 
</p>
<p>of VCNVF test was extended to model NVF test with TVS assisted injector. In this modeling effort 
</p>
<p>[5], two additional condensation models were introduced: condensation at injector-wall interface 
</p>
<p>and condensation at liquid-vapor interphase. The numerical predictions of pressure and tank filling 
</p>
<p>time compare with the measured data within 11% and 5% respectively. 
</p>
<p>The purpose of this paper is to investigate the effect of discretization on the performance of No 
</p>
<p>Vent Tank Filling model. In reference 4 and 5, the tank was discretized into 10 axial nodes. The 
</p>
<p>variation in the radial direction was not considered. In the present model, the tank was also 
</p>
<p>discretized in the radial direction to account for a recirculating flow that could develop in the tank 
</p>
<p>due to natural convection. The intent was to compare the numerical predictions of 2D 
</p>
<p>axisymmetric model with 1D model results and test data. A working progress of 2D model 
</p>
<p>development was presented in reference 6. A plausible numerical solution was obtained without 
</p>
<p>introducing pressure work and condensation at liquid vapor interface. Model predictions compared 
</p>
<p>favorably with test data and 1D solution. 
</p>

<p>3. Mathematical Modeling 
</p>
<p>Numerical modeling of filling a tank at ambient temperature with liquid cryogen is highly 
</p>
<p>complex. The physical processes are complex because the flow is unsteady, two-phase, and the 
</p>
<p>process involves both boiling and condensation with heat transfer between solid and fluid. The 
</p>
<p>objectives of a numerical model of filling a cryogenic tank are to predict the chilldown and 
</p>
<p>filling time and to estimate the amount of propellant consumption to chill down a tank. This 
</p>
<p>paper uses a nodal approach for numerical simulation using GFSSP. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>3.1 Introduction to GFSSP 
</p>
<p>GFSSP is a general-purpose computer program for analyzing fluid flow and heat transfer in a 
</p>
<p>complex network of fluid and solid systems.  It employs a pressure based, finite volume 
</p>
<p>algorithm [8] that solves the mass and energy conservation equations at the nodes and the 
</p>
<p>momentum conservation equation at branches connecting the nodes. The energy conservation 
</p>
<p>equations for solid are solved at solid nodes that are connected with fluid nodes to allow 
</p>
<p>conjugate heat transfer.  The mass and momentum conservation equations along with the 
</p>
<p>equation of state are solved simultaneously to calculate pressure, flowrate and resident mass 
</p>
<p>using the Newton-Raphson method. The energy conservation equations are solved by a 
</p>
<p>successive substitution method to calculate enthalpies at the nodes within the iteration loop of 
</p>
<p>Newton-Raphson scheme. This scheme known as “SAAS” (Simultaneous Adjustment with </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Successive Substitution) scheme [8] has been found to be robust and efficient for a non-linear 
</p>
<p>and coupled set of governing equations. 
</p>
<p>Thermodynamic properties are calculated for propellants using the computer program GASP [9], 
</p>
<p>which is integrated with GFSSP.  The fluid resistance library includes pipes, orifices, and 
</p>
<p>common fittings and valves.  GFSSP has three major parts.  The first part is the graphical user 
</p>
<p>interface MIG (Modeling Interface for GFSSP).  MIG allows users to create a flow circuit by a 
</p>
<p>“point and click” paradigm.  It creates the GFSSP input file after the completion of the model 
</p>
<p>building process.  It can also create a customized GFSSP executable by compiling and linking 
</p>
<p>User Subroutines with the solver module of the code.  The user can run GFSSP from MIG and 
</p>
<p>post process the results in the same environment.  The second major part of the program is the 
</p>
<p>Solver and Property Module.  This is the heart of the program, which reads the input data file and 
</p>
<p>generates the required conservation equations for fluid and solid nodes and branches with the 
</p>
<p>help of thermodynamic property programs.  It also interfaces with User Subroutines to receive 
</p>
<p>any specific inputs from users.  Finally, it creates output files for MIG to read and display results.  
</p>
<p>The User Subroutine is the third major part of the program.  This consists of several blank 
</p>
<p>subroutines that are called by the Solver Module.  These subroutines allow the users to 
</p>
<p>incorporate any new physical model, resistance option, fluid, etc. in the model. Further details of 
</p>
<p>the finite volume procedure and the computer program are described in references 7 and 8. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>3.2 Pool Boiling Model 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The chilldown of a propellant tank is primarily governed by boiling of liquid propellant at the 
</p>
<p>tank wall. Figure 5 shows different pool boiling regimes. During chilldown, the heat flux 
</p>
<p>temperature history of the solid surface moves from right to left (E - D - C – B – A). Initially, the 
</p>
<p>difference between the solid wall surface temperature and saturation temperature (wall 
</p>
<p>superheat) is typically several hundred degrees. Therefore, the boiling starts in the Film Boiling 
</p>
<p>regime and continues until it reaches the Leidenfrost (Minimum Film Boiling, MFB) point. After 
</p>
<p>reaching the Leidenfrost point, it switches to Transition Boiling and continues to Critical Heat 
</p>
<p>Flux. Nucleate Boiling starts after reaching Critical Heat Flux and, finally, heat transfer is 
</p>
<p>controlled by natural convection beyond the Onset of Nucleate Boiling (ONB) point. Pool 
</p>
<p>boiling correlations used in this paper are described in reference 7. </p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>

<p>3.3 Condensation Model 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Condensation on a droplet from liquid spray 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Some of the vapor in the tank ullage condenses during the process of filling the tank by spraying 
</p>
<p>liquid propellant in the tank. Condensation of vapor occurs when vapor comes in contact with 
</p>
<p>subcooled liquid spray. Condensation reduces the tank pressure and therefore must be accounted 
</p>
<p>for in the numerical model to predict the pressure and the resident liquid mass in the tank. A 
</p>
<p>simple condensation model was developed [11] for such analysis. It is assumed that the liquid 
</p>
<p>spray in the tank remains in the form of droplets of constant effective diameter. The number of 
</p>
<p>droplets in the tank was estimated from the resident mass. The method of estimating the 
</p>
<p>condensation rate was described in reference 10. 
</p>
<p>Condensation at Injector Ullage Interface 
</p>
<p>Figure 6 shows the schematic of the condensation model at Injector-Ullage interface. The 
</p>
<p>injector is cooled by the internal flow of cold vapor produced by the J-T valve. Ullage vapor is 
</p>
<p>condensed at this cold surface. Condensation occurs on a thin condensate liquid film where the 
</p>
<p>liquid film-ullage interface remains at saturation temperature corresponding to the ullage 
</p>
<p>pressure near the solid surface. The method of estimating condensation rate described in 
</p>
<p>reference 10 was also used in this paper. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
 
</p>
<p>Figure 6. Schematic of Injector – Ullage Condensation Model 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Condensation at the liquid pool-vapor interface 
</p>
<p> The interphase of liquid and vapor remains saturated with respect to the local vapor pressure. At 
</p>
<p>the liquid-vapor interphase, there is a possibility of either evaporation or condensation depending 
</p>
<p>upon the condition of ullage vapor and liquid. The phase change occurs as a result of imbalance 
</p>
<p>between sensible heat transfer rates on the vapor and liquid sides of the saturated interphase.  If 
</p>
<p>the heat transfer from the ullage to the interphase (QUI) is larger than the heat transfer from the 
</p>
<p>interphase to liquid (QIL), evaporation of liquid occurs  as shown in Figure 7. On the other hand, 
</p>
<p>if the heat transfer from the interphase to liquid (QIL) is larger than the heat transfer from the 
</p>
<p>ullage to the interphase (QUI), condensation of vapor occurs at the interphase. Further detail of 
</p>
<p>this analysis appears in Reference 10. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 7.  Evaporative mass transfer at liquid-vapor interface </p>

<p>4. 1D and 2D GFSSP Model of No Vent Tank Fill 
</p>
<p>Two GFSSP models of NVF Test with TVS augmented injector are described in this section. 
</p>
<p>Although the main purpose of this paper is to describe the axisymmetric two-dimensional 
</p>
<p>model, the 1D model [10] is also described for reference purpose. Both models start from the 
</p>
<p>inlet of the TVS augmented injector and include the J-T valve and the line leading to the 
</p>
<p>vacuum pump. The vent line of the tank was also included because the vent line was open for 
</p>
<p>a very short period during tank filling. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>4.1 1 D Model 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
Figure 8. 1D GFSSP Model of CRYOTE tank with TVS assisted injector 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The 1D GFSSP model of NVF experiment is shown in Figure 8. The model starts from the inlet 
</p>
<p>to the injector (Node 2). The fluid is distributed to two parallel paths. A smaller portion of the 
</p>
<p>flow goes through a J-T valve (Branch 5727) and exits through a vacuum pump represented by 
</p>
<p>Node 29. In the J-T valve, the liquid is expanded to go through a change of phase and becomes a 
</p>
<p>mixture of cold liquid and vapor. This cold mixture cools Solid Node 30 which represents the 
</p>
<p>injector surface (Figure 6) in contact with the ullage. The larger portion of the flow goes through 
</p>
<p>the injector represented by Node 32 and 9 restriction branches (321, 327, 328, 329, 3210, 3211, 
</p>
<p>3212, 3213, 3214). These branches all have identical resistances and therefore the injector 
</p>
<p>provides uniform flow to 9 Tank Nodes (1, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14). Note that the top tank 
</p>
<p>node 15 does not receive any spray. However, it is cooled by the injector surface (Node 30).  </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>In the 1D model, the spherical tank was modeled as 9 cylindrical segments with different 
</p>
<p>diameters. The combined volume of the 9 cylinders was equal to the tank volume. Those 9 
</p>

<p>5.1 Effect of Droplet Diameter 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Calculations have been performed with two different droplet diameters: 1.5 mm and 
</p>
<p>0.375 mm. It is observed that the predicted resident mass (Figure 12) with 0.375 mm 
</p>
<p>diameter (blue line) matches better with the test data. However, the pressures were under 
</p>
<p>predicted (Figure 11) during the 1800 seconds to 2400 seconds period, and they were 
</p>
<p>over predicted during the 2400 seconds to 3100 seconds period. It may be noted that three 
</p>
<p>modes of condensation influence the tank pressure during tank filling. During the 1700 to 
</p>
<p>1900 seconds period, condensation at injector-ullage interface helps to lower the tank 
</p>
<p>pressure and thereby avoids the stalling of the tank during no vent filling. The second 
</p>
<p>mode of condensation, i.e., condensation around droplets, becomes prevalent after 1900 
</p>
<p>seconds. This allows the tank pressure to drop at a faster rate. As the tank fills, 
</p>
<p>condensation around droplet reduces since fewer droplets are available for condensation. 
</p>
<p>However, the third mode of condensation starts at the liquid-vapor interphase as the tank 
</p>
<p>starts filling and thereby causing the tank pressure continue to fall. A detailed description 
</p>
<p>of all three condensation models incorporated in the 1D GFSSP model is provided in 
</p>
<p>Reference 10. Those condensation models with one additional modification was 
</p>
<p>incorporated in the 2D model described in this paper. In the modified condensation model 
</p>
<p>around spray droplets, the number of droplets is reduced as the tank fills.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The variation of droplet counts during tank filling is shown in Figure 13. The maximum 
</p>
<p>number of droplets for 1.5 mm and 0.375 mm are approximately 88,000 and 14,000 
</p>
<p>respectively. The larger number of droplets provides a higher rate of condensation as </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>shown in Figure 14. The droplet counts and condensation rate gradually decrease as the 
</p>
<p>tank fills. 
</p>

</p>
<p>“stalling”. Once the ullage temperature reaches saturation temperature, condensation at 
</p>
<p>ullage-injector interphase ceases and condensation around spray droplet starts as 
</p>
<p>discussed in the earlier section. The condensation at liquid-vapor interphase starts shortly

<p>5.4 Predicted Flow Pattern  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>One of the reasons of building a 2D model is to account for the effect of recirculating flow on the 
</p>
<p>thermodynamics and heat transfer processes that occur during tank filling. The 1D model shown 
</p>
<p>in Figure 8, does not have the capability to predict recirculating flow. On the other hand, a Navier-
</p>
<p>Stokes based CFD simulation of the entire tank filling process may also not be practicable for 
</p>
<p>scoping analysis. Therefore, a network based multi-dimensional flow calculation was attempted in 
</p>
<p>this paper. However, viscous effects were not modeled in any detail. In the momentum equation 
</p>
<p>only pressure and gravity terms were accounted for. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 16 shows the predicted stream traces and contours of vapor quality at 2000 seconds. The 
</p>
<p>X and Y co-ordinates are in inches. Stream traces are computed from radial and axial velocity 
</p>
<p>components at each point. It represents the path of “zero mass” particles when released at 
</p>
<p>multiple points in the flow domain. The 2D model predicts recirculating flows that are similar to 
</p>
<p>those observed in natural convection. An upward flow is observed near the wall which is warmer 
</p>
<p>than the fluid. This is due to gravitational effect in a flow with density variation. The vapor 
</p>
<p>contour plot shows that the fluid with lower vapor content (denser fluid) is settled at the bottom. 
</p>
<p>Overall, the predicted flow field and vapor quality contour plots appear plausible. 
</p>

<p>6. Conclusions 
Numerical modeling of No Vent Fill of a Cryogenic Tank is complex for several reasons. 
</p>
<p>The flow is two-phase with both boiling and condensation taking place during the 
</p>
<p>operation. In recent years, a nodal modeling tool, GFSSP [7] has been used [10,111] to 
</p>
<p>model tank filling. Due to unavailability of accurate correlations to model condensation, 
</p>
<p>“ad-hoc” condensation models were developed. Numerical prediction of system 
</p>
<p>parameters such as tank pressure and filling rate compared reasonably well with the test 
</p>
<p>data [2]. Typically, nodal modeling tools are used to model flow networks consisting of 
</p>
<p>pipes and various flow components where flow is considered one-dimensional. In no vent 
</p>
<p>fill models of cryogenic tank, the spherical tank was discretized into cylindrical section 
</p>
<p>and was thus represented by a series of pipes with variable diameters. There was no 
</p>
<p>attempt to predict recirculating flow in the tank caused due to natural convection. In this 
</p>
<p>paper an attempt was made to discretize the tank assuming the tank is two-dimensional 
</p>
<p>and axisymmetric. A two-dimensional nodal based model was able to predict 
</p>
<p>recirculating flow in the tank and provided some improvement over the 1D model. In the 
</p>
<p>2D model it was possible to use a more realistic effective droplet size in comparison to 
</p>
<p>the 1D model. However, more validation is necessary to justify the use of the 2D models 
</p>
<p>over the 1D model since the 2D model take more time to run. Both 1D and 2D modeling, 
</p>
<p>however, need more accurate correlations of condensation and evaporation heat transfer 
</p>
<p>to improve the accuracy of numerical predictions. Furthermore, the ability to model 
</p>
<p>separated two-phase flow will help to predict the ullage stratification more accurately. 
</p>
