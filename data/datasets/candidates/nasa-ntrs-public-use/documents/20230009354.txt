<p>HARDWARE VERIFICATION AND VALIDATION FOR A 
NAVIGATION SENSOR SOFTWARE MODEL IN SUPPORT OF 
</p>
<p>FLIGHT VEHICLE PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS  
</p>
<p>Evan J. Anzalone,* Nicholas Hoen,† Thomas Park‡ , and Charles Weyandt§ 
</p>
<p>… or, “It’s in the details, how to make complicated software perform like com-
plicated hardware.” In attempts to minimize development time and quickly build 
an operational vehicle, NASA’s Space Launch System (SLS) has had to be inten-
tional about integrated testing. Constraints on budget and schedule have required 
balance between testing needs and the desire for an integrated flight vehicle as 
soon as possible. To provide key insights early in design and analysis cycles, a 
large amount of effort has shifted into maturing and validating models at the com-
ponent level with integrated testing as a means to validate their integration. In 
terms of SLS Navigation, this, and the model-based design approach have pushed 
explicit requirements for sensor models to be validated against flight hardware to 
high precision. This paper covers the approach taken to verify and validate the 
models for the two key navigation sensors on the SLS vehicle, the Redundant 
Inertial Navigation Sensor and the Rate Gyro Assembly. These models are used 
in performance evaluation, fault detection, and operations development exten-
sively. Using a mix of data from hardware vendor documentation and testing re-
ports, limited in-house testing, and integration activities, these models were able 
to be validated against flight hardware at multiple levels, from the internal soft-
ware design to statistical behavior at the raw sensor and integrated box levels. The 
high level of insight into the hardware elements is instrumental to support flight 
certification activities and building confidence in SLS Navigation capability. Fo-
cused testing enabled additional insight and validation that proved invaluable and 
the resulting insights were used to focus and mature models. Additionally, of hav-
ing validated performance-based hardware models enables a wide breadth of ac-
tivities including detailed fault detection studies and integration into future vehi-
cle frameworks, such as an upper stage and provide a valuable asset to continued 
SLS analysis and design.   
</p>
<p>INTRODUCTION  
Inserting a payload accurately into a defined orbit is the major requirement of any launch vehicle system. 
</p>
<p>For large vehicles that take years to design, build and fly, verifying this requirement is very complex, espe-
cially when test flight opportunities are limited or are not available. For the Space Launch System (SLS), a 
large number of requirements are verified through a combinations of hardware and software testing ̶ both at 
the component level and at the integrated system level.  Hardware characterization is used to feed the devel-
opment of software models that are used in 6 degree of freedom simulation and hardware-in-the-loop testing. 
</p>

<p>These hardware and software models are used to demonstrate system capability across a wide variety of 
vehicle configurations, launch environments, as well as robustness to off-nominal conditions. Cross-compar-
ison between simulations at various level of hardware integration and with independent model implementa-
tions are used to provide the necessary evidence to ensure the models are a consistent and accurate represen-
tation of the system under design. 
</p>
<p>This approach is in contrast to that used on other programs, where the vehicle matures through cycles of 
increased capability, such as the Saturn program, and similar recent development programs, such as that of 
SpaceX*† or Blue Origin‡, which utilize extensive flight testing and demonstration as secondary missions to 
evolving integrated capability. Due to the vehicle complexity, schedule, and cost constraints, SLS is allocat-
ing one test flight, Artemis 1, to demonstrate integrated vehicle capability prior to human flight. As such, 
there is increased focus and attention to certification activities from individual hardware components up to 
an integrated stage, with testing focused on filling in analysis gaps, validating software models, and demon-
strating integration performance prior to integrated vehicle flight. 
</p>
<p>This paper documents the approach taken to verify and validate the navigation subsystem of the SLS 
Block 1 vehicle. The two key elements of this are the navigation hardware and performance models. A de-
scription of the data products, their source, and their integration are included to provide insight into the sys-
tem’s capability. Both verification activities, which ensures that the model of the system matches the speci-
fication, and validation activities, which involves comparisons of the model performance to flight hardware 
data, detailed in this paper. Together these provide insight into a recommended approach for the usage of 
models in navigation system design for critical flight systems.  
</p>
<p>SLS MODEL-BASED ENGINEERING APPROACH 
In order to streamline the development process and updates in computational models and process, the 
</p>
<p>SLS program shifted away from a paperwork- and requirement-centric approach to systems capability defi-
nitions and vehicle design. Lessons learned from the Constellation program led to the implementation of a 
model-centric approach to vehicle design and internal collaboration among disciplines. In this definition, 
models can capture multiple facets and include such artifacts as a 3D model, a table of data, or executable 
code representing some portion of the vehicle. This is in contrast to the use of written documentation to pass 
requirements between systems and capture design elements. The impact of this is not to eliminate documen-
tation, but to focus it and allow the models themselves to capture design elements and performance criteria. 
Similarly including traceability within models is important for documentation and later reference. Addition-
ally, with the capturing of this information into executable models, it is also possible to gain more insight to 
integrated systems due to the increased capability and performance of subsystem models.  
</p>
<p>For such a large vehicle as SLS, flight testing can be schedule- and cost-limited due to available budget 
and desired system capability on-dates. This does not reduce the need for confidence in the design being safe, 
reliable, and meeting performance capabilities prior to flight, particularly for a manned vehicle; instead, it 
places additional burden on pre-flight analysis to ensure a successful mission. These models thus become 
key anchors of vehicle integrated analysis and confidence in vehicle performance. The maturity of these grow 
throughout the program to match hardware design maturity and eventually are used in integrated vehicle 
simulation models with as much hardware as possible to generate evidence to demonstrate expected vehicle 
performance. Similarly, additional burden must be taken to ensure the validity and official approval of these 
models and their results. For SLS, this encompasses the Design Math Model (DMM) processes for model 
acceptance and release among program elements. A description of this process in detail with regards to the 
SLS GNC is provided in Emerson et al. 1 
</p>

<p>A major model used to assess the launch vehicle insertion capability, from a guidance and navigation 
perspective, is the Inertial Navigation System model. This software model mimics the inertial sensor behav-
ior, onboard software processing, and navigation algorithms that result in an integrated navigation state---
position, velocity, and attitude---that is used in closed-loop guidance and steering algorithms to meet vehicle-
level performance requirements.  The development, verification, and validation of the SLS INS model has 
been ongoing for several years.  This effort has recently completed in advance of Flight Readiness Analysis 
Cycle and Design Critical Review activities leading towards the Artemis-1 mission.   
</p>
<p>GNC MODEL DEVELOPMENT RESPONSIBILITIES  
The SLS Stages Element is responsible for integrating with the Prime Contractor, Boeing, for the design 
</p>
<p>and procurement of component-and system-level hardware based on NASA-provided requirements. The in-
ertial sensors used in the GN&amp;C architecture for the SLS Block 1 configuration are the Redundant Inertial 
Navigation Unit (RINU) and the Rate Gyro Assembly (RGA). The Stages Element also has the responsibility 
of developing any necessary models of the hardware and embedded software of these GN&amp;C sensors. These 
models would be of sufficient fidelity for integration into Hardware in the Loop (HWIL) laboratories as well 
into vehicle 6-DOF analysis simulations.   
</p>
<p>Vehicle Management (VM) is responsible for the vehicle Mission and Fault Management algorithms, 
GN&amp;C design, and integrated performance assessment. A major component of this performance assessment 
for GN&amp;C is based on vehicle 6-DOF simulation analysis.  It also is the responsibility of VM to integrate the 
GN&amp;C sensor models, provided by the Stages element, into any analysis tools—including 6-DOF simulation 
tools—used in the performance assessments of the GN&amp;C design.    
</p>
<p>Through the Stages Element, the hardware vendor provided to the SLS Navigation Team detailed docu-
mentation and insight on the design and functionality of the RINU and the RGA. This documentation in-
cluded information on the different sensor error parameters modelled in the Vendor’s own analysis tools as 
well as details on the contribution of each of these error sources on integrated navigation performance. The 
Navigation Team also had access to Vendor test data with which to anchor the sensor error models and the 
performance of the integrated navigation model.  The Vendor shared with the team information on the im-
plementation of algorithms used in the inertial measurement processing as well as in the navigation state 
estimation.  This insight into the internal software of the GN&amp;C sensors also including details on the fault-
detection capability of the hardware. Through these data deliveries, along with numerous technical data ex-
changes, the Navigation team gained a level of insight into the hardware and software design, internal and 
external data interfaces, system performance, and integrated testing of the avionics components allowed the 
Navigation Team to develop high-fidelity performance models of the navigation sensors. 
</p>
<p>The Redundant Inertial Navigation Unit (RINU) is a one-fault tolerant Inertial Navigation System that 
produces an integrated navigation state---position, velocity, and attitude---as well as angular rate and linear 
acceleration data for use in the vehicle control software.  To support the one-fault tolerance, the RINU con-
tains redundant accelerometers and gyroscopes as well as Fault Detection, Isolation, and Recovery (FDIR) 
software used to detect failed sensors and prevents data from failed sensors from being used for any output 
data.  To initialize the navigation system, the RINU performs Gyrocompass Alignment (GCA) to estimate 
its orientation with respect to an inertial frame. To support the navigation function, the RINU software con-
tains modules for performing high-rate compensation of the inertial data, computing estimates of gravita-
tional acceleration, and for integrating the compensated incremental angles and incremental velocities into 
an estimate of the vehicle position, velocity, and attitude.  The RINU also contains hardware and software 
necessary to support communications with the SLS Flight Computers over redundant MIL-STD-1553B data 
busses, receiving and responding to commands and data loads, including time synchronization to vehicle 
time performing hardware built-in tests. 
</p>
<p> One key part of the RINU performance model is the sensor error model. Since the RINU contains redun-
dant sensors, the Navigation team simulated each individually to reflect the geometry of the hardware sensor 
configuration.  Stages and the hardware vendor provided NASA with a detailed characterization of the sensor 
errors and their impacts on the RINU’s navigation performance.    These were used to identify key perfor-
mance metrics of the raw sensor capability and provided scope for primary drivers. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 4 
</p>
<p>The hardware supplier also provided detailed descriptions of the algorithms used in the RINU in form of 
Software Description Documents (SDD).  The level of detail in these software descriptions allowed the Nav-
igation Team to develop high-fidelity models of various modules in the RINU software.  Primarily, the team 
implemented models of the RINU’s Gyrocompass Alignment filter, models of the inertial navigation algo-
rithms, gravity model, and the high-rate compensations---coning and sculling---of the 𝑑𝑑𝑑𝑑s and 𝑑𝑑𝑑𝑑s used in 
the navigation algorithms.  Additionally, the team developed a detailed model of the FDIR algorithms used 
to detect and isolate failed sensors.  
</p>
<p>As a part of the RINU model, the Navigation Team developed a model of the RINU’s capability to detect 
and isolate failed sensors.  Specifically, the minimum number of sensors required to produce measurements 
in orthogonal axes is equal to the number of degrees of freedom for orthogonally arranged sensors; for sensors 
in a skewed configuration, the minimum is equal to the number of degrees of freedom plus one. The minimum 
number of sensors required to detect a dissimilar measurement in asymmetric (skewed) arrangement is equal 
to the number of degrees of freedom plus an additional sensor to resolve the measurement and another addi-
tional sensor to create an over-determined system capable of fault detection.  
</p>
<p>The RINU model is used in a variety of stand-alone and integrated 6-DOF tools for to develop several 
analysis products.  The RINU model is integrated into MAVERIC, the vehicle 6-DOF simulation used at 
MSFC.  Integrated GN&amp;C performance is assessed with Monte Carlo runs of MAVERIC.  For each vehicle 
configuration, the GN&amp;C ascent performance is documented in the various volumes of SLS-RPT-080, Space 
Launch System Program (SLSP) Integrated Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GN&amp;C) Performance As-
sessment. 
</p>
<p>Additionally, MAVERIC results are used to anchor our assessment of Hardware in the Loop (HWIL) test 
data. Operating as a stand-alone tool, the RINU model was used to perform an offline gyrocompassing anal-
ysis outside the MAVERIC simulation.  The results of this analysis are used to initialize the RINU navigation 
model in the vehicle 6-DOF.  When performing a large Monte Carlo set, a significant amount of computation 
time is saved by not having to wait on the RINU model to perform GCA in preparation for ascent flight.  The 
RINU model is also used in developing the flight-specific set of parameters used in the RINU FDIR algo-
rithms. Additionally, the model was used to determine the sensitivity analysis that was performed to deter-
mine the sensitivity of RINU FDIR thresholds and false positive failure probability due to increased sensor 
noise, flexible body dynamics and other un-modeled dynamics.   
</p>
<p>The Rate Gyroscope Assembly (RGA) is a set of three pairs of mutually orthogonal gyroscopes.  The 
RGA samples the gyroscopes internally at a high rate, filters the measurements, and outputs filtered angular 
rate data on MIL-STD-1553B busses.  While the RGA is internally redundant, each pair of rate gyros operates 
independently.  The consolidation or down-selection of the angular rate data across the three channels is 
handled in the SLS Flight Software on the FCs.  As such, the Navigation team chose to represent a single 
channel of RGA sensors and data processing in the model. Multiple instances of this single channel model 
were used to simulate outputs to match the hardware flight interface. 
</p>
<p>MODEL VERIFICATION APPROACH 
Verification of the RINU and RGA performance models (DMM-CS-0411 and DMM-CS-0410) was per-
</p>
<p>formed according to the V&amp;V requirements in Paragraphs 3.15 of NASA document SLS-RPT-080 Vol 3 
Revision A, "Space Launch System Program (SLSP) Design Model Delivery Standard"2, which states: 
</p>
<p>“Model Verification is the process of determining that a computational model accurately represents the 
underlying mathematical model and its solution from the perspective of the intended uses of the M&amp;S 
[Model and Simulation]. It includes a set of input/output model test cases that can be used to verify that 
a model has been implemented correctly, and that span the range of validity of the model. Verification 
measures the correctness of (a) the mathematical understanding of the underlying physics, (b) the trans-
lation (i.e., program code that does the computational effort) of the physics-to-mathematics into a model, 
and (c) the effects and sensitivities of dependent and independent variables on other variables of the 
model, so that results are indicative of the hardware/system performance.” </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 5 
</p>
<p>The Navigation Team developed several criteria in the verification of the RINU and RGA simulation 
models.  These criteria included: 
</p>
<p>1. Trace of stochastic error parameters and modeling to vendor-provided documentation 
2. Unit tests of individual error model implementations 
3. Trace of algorithms and software used to the vendor documentation 
4. Trace of isolator and latency characteristics to vendor-provided documentation 
5. Monte-Carlo sets with the simulation model to quantify performance.  For the RINU specifically, 
</p>
<p>these Monte Carlo sets included both failed and non-failed sensor configurations. 
6. Comparison with vendor analysis for RINU FDIR component of model. 
7. Comparison with vendor performance data from the simulation used by the vendor to verify accu-
</p>
<p>racy requirements. 
8. Verification of frequency domain behavior of model components which affect the sensors’ fre-
</p>
<p>quency domain. 
9. Comparison with vendor analysis from Software Flight Qualification Test (FQT). 
</p>
<p>While there was no requirement for the Navigation team to independently verify the RINU and RGA 
models, members of the team that performed the V&amp;V activity were not the same individuals who developed 
and implemented the model.  As the V&amp;V team discovered issues with the model, the Navigation Team peer-
reviewed the findings and decided on actions necessary to resolve the discrepancy.  The model developers 
would then implement the solution and present evidence to the V&amp;V team.  All V&amp;V issues, along with 
dispositions were documented in the Verification Report that is delivered with the models. 
</p>
<p>For the individual sensor models in the RINU and RGA component models, the Navigation Team per-
formed a code inspection to verify the correct implementation.  The code inspection was accompanied by 
low-level unit tests to confirm the expected behavior of the model.  For each function in the model, e.g., gyro 
and accel bias, scale factors, random walks, a test was developed to comparing the output of the component 
model to an analytically derived result. The data parameters used as inputs to the model were compared 
against vendor-provided to ensure that the error sources were modeled consistent with the values used by the 
vendor for verifying accuracy requirements.  For error parameters used in the RGA model, comparisons were 
made to Stages-levied requirements as well as to vendor-supplied capability estimates.  For the RINU, the 
error parameters were compared back to the vendor-developed Navigation Error Budget which documented 
the vendor’s analysis of the individual gyroscope and accelerometer error terms. 
</p>
<p>The Navigation Team performed a thorough inspection of the RINU model code compared to the vendor 
software descriptions.  The Navigation V&amp;V team annotated the model code with references to the vendor 
documentation.  This allowed the team to trace, line-by-line, the functionality of the model code to RINU 
software documentation.  Any discrepancies in the model were reported to the Navigation team for review 
and disposition.  The model developers implemented the required changes.  All modeling discrepancies were 
documented in the model verification report that is delivered along with the model. 
</p>
<p>To verify the navigation performance of the RINU model, the Navigation team performed several Monte 
Carlo sets, flying the RINU along a specified reference trajectory.  These simulation runs were performed 
with and without sensor failures.  The 1-sigma navigation errors from these analyses were compared to the 
capability estimates of the RINU navigation performance listed in the Navigation Error Budget.  Addition-
ally, the vendor supplied NASA with addition sets of Monte Carlo data used during Formal Qualification 
Test (FQT) of the RINU software.  Comparisons of the one sigma navigation errors showed favorable results.  
In addition to verifying the performance of the RINU model by comparison to vendor test data, several ana-
lytical tests were performed by the Navigation team.  Since heading error can be analytically predicted, the 
RINU model was run with a simulated East angular velocity bias.  The RINU model’s heading error tracked 
reasonably well with theoretical predictions. Assuming perfect sensors, the RINU’s ability to track true atti-
tude were verified simulating gyrocompassing under with no error sources enabled. A Schuler test was per-
formed with the RINU model to verify that the navigation code in the model performed as expected when 
navigating in-place. </p>

<p>Frequency Response Verification  
</p>
<p>The Navigation team verified the frequency response of the sensor models by an analysis of the compo-
nent models that effect the frequency content of the sensor model outputs.  The team used vendor-provided 
data on the transfer functions that model the responses of the chassis and isolators to perform a code inspec-
tion the implementations of the models of the mechanical interfaces.  Then Bode diagrams were used to 
verify the implementation of the filters matched vendor data. For each of the software components that effect 
the phase and gain response of the sensor outputs, the Navigation team used vendor-supplied data on the 
responses of each of filter in the sensors software.  Code inspection showed that the models of filters were 
consistent with vendor documentation.  Similarly, Bode diagrams demonstrated that the modeled and pre-
dicted responses were very similar.  Where differences in the vendor’s and the modeled responses differed, 
the Navigation team demonstrated the model produced a more conservative response—less attenuation but 
with more phase lag then the vendor analysis.  
</p>
<p>FDIR Verification Activities 
</p>
<p>Due to the unique sensor redundancy scheme, sensor failures have an impact on RINU performance and 
as a result, the modeling of the sensor pentad and FDIR logic has been included in DMM-CS-0411. Over the 
course of approximately a year beginning in mid-2013, the DMM-CS-0411 development team worked with 
the RINU hardware vendor to model RINU FDIR to a level of fidelity suitable for simulating RINU behavior 
and for FDIR threshold design.The Navigation team implemented the RINU FIDR logic based on the RINU 
Software Description document supplied by the vendor. The team regularly discussed the FDIR Implemen-
tation, configuration, and analysis-related details with the RINU vendor at various technical teleconferences. 
The primary analyst on the vendor side was brought in as a consultant to design FDIR thresholds for the 
RINU program and to discuss implementation details with the RINU model developers on the Navigation 
Team. The primary analyst had previously designed the FDIR thresholds for the heritage FTINU design for 
ULA’s Atlas V EELV.  In December of 2013 at a Navigation Technical Interchange Meeting the vendor’s 
facility, the MSFC team presented the initial set of FDIR verification results vendor. A number of discrep-
ancies were discovered, tracked, and subsequently resolved resulting in the presentation of detailed verifica-
tion results at a follow-up Navigation TIM in May of 2014. At the end of the TIM the RINU vendor verbally 
concurred with the model developers’ implementation of RINU FDIR, accuracy of results, and showed con-
fidence in the transfer of knowledge required to design RINU FDIR thresholds. 
</p>
<p>MODEL VALIDATION APPROACH  
For the purposes of this paper, validation analysis focuses on the comparison of the model to flight hard-
</p>
<p>ware. The intent of this analysis is to provide additional insight outside of algorithm and specification docu-
mentation. Similarly, this testing allows for an independent check on the assumptions used in interpreting 
design artifacts. The testing herein focused on the areas of performance that the GNC system is particularly 
sensitive to. The focus is on several key areas: demonstrated ability to gyrocompass under external dynamics, 
validation of noise characteristics, frequency response testing, integrated system latency, and validation of 
navigation performance between the model and a hardware development test unit. All of these tests utilized 
a flight-like hardware unit with actual sensors and flight software. This unit was produced in advance of 
flight units to allow for early testing and characterization. The primarily difference between this and actual 
flight units is the durations and levels involved in acceptance testing at the vendor level. Due to its purpose 
as a test unit with limited exposure to high vibration or thermal variations, reduced testing was acceptable to 
meet the needs of the test events. Although the environmental testing was limited, the unit was still put 
through testing to establish its performance within requirements, focusing on calibration and navigation ac-
curacy. As such, this platform provides an ideal platform for validation activities. 
</p>
<p> The validation testing suite utilized this flight hardware within controlled environments and provided 
targeted excitation to assess specific elements. These areas of focus can be captured in simulation and design 
documentation but are considered essential to the integrated GNC performance. For example, the key sensi-
tivity of the navigation performance is to the initial attitude error at the end of gyrocompassing. As such, the 
ability of the unit to gyrocompass is critical. While the detailed models of SLS do enable a simulation of on-
pad dynamics under various wind conditions, further hardware testing helps provide additional insight to </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 7 
</p>
<p>hardware robustness and allow for comparing the response to flight-like as well as worst case on-pad dynam-
ics. Similarly, to meet the stability requirements of the vehicle, the controls algorithms are designed around 
the sensors’ dynamics response. This includes both RINU and RGA frequency responses. Validation of these 
through hardware testing provides the additional confidence needed in assessing control margins and vehicle 
stability. Similarly, to meet insertion conditions, the onboard guidance algorithms are sensitive to integrated 
system latency, particularly at engine shutdown. Due to the high velocity of the vehicle, this latency must be 
taken into account when commanding this event to ensure the proper state is achieved to meet insertion 
requirements.

<p>The first validation test performed focused on testing the RINU’s ability to gyrocompass under simulated

overall effort. For this test, the RINU was mounted onto a large 6 degree of freedom (DOF) table at MSFC 
and was gyrocompass’ed through a variety of dynamics. The table allowed for comparison between response 
of the flight hardware and the RINU model to a common set of dynamics. This included both the dynamics 
used in vendor specification and analysis expected on pad dynamics, as well as scaled up on-pad dynamics 
to validate robustness under much larger than expected on pad motion. Figure 1 below provides a picture of 
the RINU mounted onto the table as well as a close-up image of the unit under test. 
</p>

<p>In addition to validating the performance of the algorithms between the software and hardware models, 
this testing provided additional insight into several integration constraints, which caused some limitations in 
post-processing. Two areas of limitation in the test is due to having a multi-rate navigation systems, which 
is typical for integrated navigation boxes and laid out by sources such as Savage4. Based on the interface of 
the unit, only the low rate navigation data was available. As such, detailed validation of the sensor error 
models and processing was reduced. However, this data could be used to enable detailed checks of the 
onboard navigation software. This testing focused on validation of the implementation of the gyrocompassing 
algorithms in the RINU model. The other limitation of the test setup was manifested in the form of missed 
samples. Due to asynchronous nature of the 1553 bus, there is a potential for missed samples of data. While 
this does not affect in-flight performance since the latest state is always captured by flight software, this does 
limit the ability to re-create the entire software processing loop within the software model. For example, in 
Figure 2, missed samples can be seen to cause divergence between the unit under test and the software 
model. This plot shows the difference between the two solutions. In order to correct this, an interpolated data 
point was inserted to enable better matching performance, but an exact comparison was not able to be 
achieved. Even with this limitation though, the algorithms were able to be validated with this testing within 
expectations.

<p>Development Test Unit/Integrated Comparisons 
</p>
<p>Analysis of stand-alone hardware testing and detailed modeling is very important in being able to predict 
vehicle performance. Most hardware testing is performed with a test set interface with limited non-flight 
conditions, and usually performed with an engineering development unit (EDU). The next step in hardware 
testing is system integration testing, using HWIL, which plays an important part in the full testing program. 
For the SLS program, HWIL testing is done with a RINU development test unit (DTU), which has the same 
parts and software as a flight RINU, but with the sensors replaced with a PAM card, which provides the unit 
with an external interface to a dynamics simulation. The dynamic simulation (ARTEMIS) provides dynam-
ics, actuator and some sensor modeling independent of MAVERIC. 
</p>
<p>But the benefits of HWIL testing is not just to ensure the hardware and software all interact correctly 
together, although that is also a critical need and invaluable risk mitigation tool. For us, this test environment 
provided opportunities to analyze, validate and improve our modeling of the sensor, system and how we 
evaluate them. Evaluating the performance of the HWIL sensors allowed us to validate and better understand 
the latency of the sensors. By using the ARTEMIS system, we were able to inject temporary biases into the 
sensors and measure the latency of the sensor response, using a bus monitor of the 1553 traffic. Because the 
RINU and RGAs both have internal filters that were not part of the latency budget, we had to compensate for 
that by analytically removing the filter response to determine the net latency. Latency is important for control 
system stability, but critical for affecting orbit injection and determining when to shut down the engines. 
With multiple runs, we were able to accurately measure actual latency and compare it with hardware speci-
fications and expected variations due to asynchronous operation and variability. 
</p>
<p>Previous latency measurement methodologies at the system level focused on a method showing a phase 
lag response from the controller by injecting sinusoidal biases. Due to the system noise and sensitivity of the 
controller, as well as the 20 millisecond (msec) quantization of the controller process, preliminary testing in 
MAVERIC showed that this method would not be possible to resolve latency levels accurate enough to val-
idate latency specifications. Thus, a new method was developed using larger, short duration, square wave 
biases that caused only temporary disturbances to the controllers and allowed much finer (&lt;1 msec) meas-
urement of the latency, as well as step-wise change evaluation through the flight computer and actuator com-
mands at a system level. Figure 9 below provides a sample of the data used to perform the measurements/anal-
ysis. </p>

<p>The process of evaluating the sensors at the 1553 data rather than output from the flight computer, espe-

have been discovered until much later in the program, or not detected until flight, resulting in a catastrophic 
system failure. This provided further evidence of the importance of analyzing data at every interface and not 
just an end-to-end analysis.

internal RINU software correction was not being performed.

<p>The detailed analysis into both the ARTEMIS and MAVERIC models provided additional validation of 
good correlation. It also brought focus onto the process of calculating the navigation error, which is simula-
tion-only calculation (truth states of actual flight are not available, although post-processing using upper 
stage GPS data and ground tracking data can provide a better estimate for post-flight analysis). A comparison 
of the ARTEMIS team navigation error and navigation/MAVERIC team allowed us to discover minor errors 
on both sides. The ARTEMIS data did not provide propagation between the RINU navigation timetag based 
states and the states of the simulation truth. The simulation truth states are updated at 2.5 msec, and these 
steps are broken further into 1200 Hz incremental angle and velocity changes to feed into the RINU DTU. 
The states of the RINU are internally filtered, and the timetag also corrects for expected internal latencies, 
resulting in timetags that are not aligned closer than 2.5 msec. However, the expected accuracy of this results 
in small (&lt;50 feet) errors. The errors between the two simulations was still several hundred feet. Independent 
determination of the navigation errors that performed separate time alignment and propagation for both sim-
ulations showed performance much closer than that shown in MAVERIC, which had previously been evalu-
ated and determined to be accurate. This additional analysis provided the direction to evaluate the navigation 
error performed in MAVERIC. This resulted in two error findings. One was an error using the timetag from 
an incorrect RINU subaddress. This error was actually introduced due to the high level of modeling of the 
multiple, complex RINU outputs with similar mnemonics. Once discovered, it was also found that this time-
tag mnemonic error propagated into the state used for guidance and engine shut-down determining. Correct-
ing it improved the orbit injection accuracy.

navigation step in the MAVERIC simulation. While this error did not affect injection performance, it mate-
rialized as a larger than expected navigation error. When the error was corrected, it resulted in much improved 
comparative results with the SIL results, as shown in Figure 10. The larger attitude errors in the SIL is traced 
directly to the 2.5 msec asynchronous operation of the ARTEMIS simulation and DTU, coupled with high 
roll and pitch-over maneuvers performed shortly after lift-off.

similar efforts. It is important to be careful about readout noise specification and the rate at which that noise 
is applied in modeling. Hardware testing can easily validate this and help to match the model to hardware. 
While time-consuming and data-intensive, Allan Variance assessment of the sensors under static conditions 
proved very useful, allowing for a statistical assessment of the error behavior. Similarly, it is important to 
maintain focus on purpose of models, such as an interface emulator or a performance model. But it is also 
important to maintain an awareness of the sensor ICD and ensure matching of the sensor outputs ahead of 
FSW integration and testing, i.e. maintaining an output structure matching the ICD in terms of data content 
rather than specific message structure such as modeling all health and status information. During analysis for 
the vehicle, integrated latency became very important to characterize and understand due to its impact on 
controller lag and insertion accuracy. This testing of an integrated system can only be performed in a hard-
ware-in-the-loop environment allowing inclusion of FSW and integrated flight hardware. 
</p>
<p>The biggest enabler of this effort is the large amount of integration and support between the NASA de-
velopers, the prime contractor, Boeing, and the hardware vendor, Honeywell. This team was well integrated 
throughout the hardware development and model integration process with Boeing and NASA Stages being 
the primary customers of the model. This model was used by NASA VM in performance assessment. As 
such, reviews after each test event and integrated validation and verification activity included key personnel 
across each organization to provide confidence and insight into the model and hardware. The amount of 
documentation provided enabled a large amount of insight into performance of the units and its capability. 
Additionally, use of data from Acceptance and Qualification testing is also a great asset, although its use may 
be limited if representative truth data is not available. A key lesson of this work is to focus on hardware 
testing to fill in known gaps and provide confidence in areas of high sensitivity. Due to flying heritage-based 
sensors, the hardware tests were designed to capture insight into expected performance in focused areas. As 
such, a flight-like development unit with live sensors was critical for GCA testing and detailed static analysis.  
A particular interest of these sensors is their frequency response at low frequencies. This proved difficult to 
assess, due to having to induce low frequency dynamics at low magnitudes to limit aging of the units. Even 
with this, the results still provided key insight into confirming the overall response and behavior of the units 
under test. 
</p>
<p>Another key takeaway from this work is the need for oversampling, particularly in the case of asynchro-
nous hardware interfaces such as 1553. This problem exhibited itself in gyrocompassing testing as missed 
data frames and limited the ability to perform cross-verification between the model and hardware. To address 
this issue, the frequency response test utilized a higher sampling rate than the standard output of the unit 
under test to ensure no missed samples. While this did cause increased complexity in post-processing soft-
ware, having the data on-hand is well worth the effort. This oversampling isn’t needed in SLS Block 1 flight 
due to the vehicle always having access to the latest navigation state data, since the sensor observations are 
integrated within the RINU, rather than in SLS FSW. 
</p>
<p>The navigation team is taking these lessons learned and applying them to model development, verifica-
tion, and validation for new sensor models needed for the B1B EUS, particularly GPS. A key area of differ-
ence is in terms of onboard software. For GPS receivers, the performance can ultimately be traced back to 
the tracking loops internal to the device. This is typically implemented in digital logic as opposed to typical 
software. To model these in real-time requires running the model at a very high rate in order to model the </p>

<p>high frequency radio signals involved. As such, the scope of modeling will need to be balanced between the 
needs of performance capability and runtime concerns in an integrated simulation. Conversely, through the 
use of RF simulators, extensive testing can be performed on flight-like units to provide a high level of insight 
into the operational characteristics of the device. Similarly, for a multi-sensor system, integrated system level 
verification and validation is necessary to validate performance and ensure operational capability. The les-
sons learned from the verification and validation has provided a large amount of benefit. The final result of 
this effort, the RINU and RGA models, have been tested to a high level of confidence prior to the first SLS 
flight, and allow for extensive analysis and development for future designs. Post-fight, these models will be 
reviewed to validate how well they match during flight conditions and provide further confidence. It is the 
hope of the authors that as the industry moves towards an ever increasing usage of software models and 
simulation to develop confidence and performance assessment, these lessons learned and approach can pro-
vide a starting or comparison point for other missions.
