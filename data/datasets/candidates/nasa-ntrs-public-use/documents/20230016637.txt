<p>Validation of the HyperSolve CFD Solver for Entry Descent and
Landing Applications
</p>
<p>Kyle B. Thompson∗ , Matthew D. O’Connell† , Andrew D. Hinkle‡ , and Clark C. Pederson§
</p>
<p>NASA Langley Research Center,
Hampton, VA 23681, USA
</p>
<p>The functional equivalence of the HyperSolve unstructured edge-based, finite-volume
computational fluid dynamics code to the Langley Aerothermodynamic Upwind Relaxation
Algorithm multiblock structured grid code is documented for applications of interest to the Entry,
Descent, and Landing community. A suite of cases using a range of thermochemical gas models
on relevant vehicle configurations were analyzed with both codes and the results compared. A
tolerance of ±4% difference in surface pressure and surface heat flux from a benchmark LAURA
solution was used as the criterion for functional equivalence, and comparisons of flowfield
quantities are also included to verify that the thermochemical nonequilibrium capabilities in
HyperSolve match those of the LAURA code. In general, HyperSolve predictions for surface
pressure and surface heat flux are in close agreement with those predicted by LAURA.
</p>
<p>I. Introduction
Using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to predict surface heat transfer rates and pressure has become a common
</p>
<p>practice, particularly for vehicles operating in the hypersonic flow regime. Recently, a series of novel edge-based,
finite-volume techniques implemented in the HyperSolve CFD code were shown to be effective at achieving accurate
surface heat transfer predictions over a hemisphere on simplex-element meshes when combined with a mesh adaptation
technique [1]. While this ability to accurately predict surface heat transfer on simplex meshes is a highly enabling and
sought after capability [2–4], these techniques were demonstrated using an ideal gas assumption that is typically not
suitable for the hypersonic flow regime where high-energy physics modeling is required. To employ the HyperSolve CFD
code to aerothermal analysis for Entry, Descent and Landing (EDL) applications, the code has been extended to support
thermochemical nonequilibrium modeling with similar functional equivalence to the Langley Aerothermodynamic
Upwind Relaxation Algorithm (LAURA) multiblock structured finite-volume reacting flow solver [5]. This work focuses
on validating the surface heat flux and pressure predicted by the HyperSolve finite-volume discretization via LAURA
benchmark predictions for a series of cases with conditions and geometries that are relevant to EDL applications.
</p>
<p>The work is this paper consists of three parts: (1) a description of the HyperSolve and LAURA CFD solvers, (2)
verification of the correctness of the models and numerical methods implemented in HyperSolve via the Method of
Manufactured Solutions (MMS), and (3) a functional equivalence test suite, similar to a previous effort involving the
FUN3D and LAURA codes [6]. The MMS verification tests show that HyperSolve achieves the design order of accuracy,
which is an important step in building developer confidence in the correctness of the numerical methods and models
implemented. The functional equivalence tests presented are important for building confidence that HyperSolve is able
to produce similar results with similar mesh resolution as the flight-validated benchmark solver, LAURA. In general,
HyperSolve surface pressure and surface heat flux predictions closely match those predicted by LAURA when both
solvers were run on an identical structured mesh.
</p>
<p>II. HyperSolve and LAURA CFD Solvers Overview
HyperSolve is an edge-based, finite-volume Navier-Stokes solver, which is being developed at the NASA Langley
</p>
<p>Research Center. Both the inviscid and viscous fluxes are computed at the interfaces of dual control volumes around
each vertex in the mesh. The inviscid fluxes at interfaces of neighboring control volumes are computed by extrapolating

<p>the solution variables and flux to the interface location. This extrapolation uses gradients computed by an unweighted,
linear least-squares technique. The viscous fluxes are computed at the same interface as the inviscid fluxes, using
a weighted, linear least-squares technique in conjunction with the alpha-damping technique by Nishikawa [7]. Full
details of the HyperSolve edge-based, finite-volume discretization are described in Ref. [1]. HyperSolve is a fluid solver
dynamically loadable plugin in the T-infinity ecosystem [8], and uses a Jacobian-free Newton-Krylov (JFNK) method to
iteratively converge the solution to steady state. This JFNK method is implemented via the Enigma library [9], which
provides a simplified interface to the PETSc [10] library. In this work, the additive-Schwarz-ILU0 preconditioner with
level-1 domain overlap is used to right-precondition the GMRES Krylov linear solver in PETSc.
</p>
<p>The LAURA [11] code has been used for decades as a CFD tool to simulate high-energy, reacting flows. LAURA
has supported numerous NASA missions, including predicting a static instability in the Mars Pathfinder vehicle [12],
contributing to code verification with FIRE-II [13], and assisting in the design and analysis of the Orion heatshield [14].
The code solves the reacting flow equations with support for high-energy physics, including thermal and chemical
nonequilibrium modeling [15], surface catalysis, and ablation [16]. The Symmetric Total Variation Diminishing (STVD)
scheme [17] is employed for computing the inviscid flux, with a central scheme employed for the viscous flux. The
latest release of LAURA, version 5.6, includes many new capabilities for simulating reacting flows, including better
parallel performance, new options for specifying surface roughness and turbulent transition locations to the suite of
algebraic turbulence models, and improved spline interpolation techniques [18].
</p>
<p>III. Verification Tests
Verification tests are essential to building confidence in a CFD code and serve a complementary role to validation.
</p>
<p>Traditional verification tests compare a numerical solution to a known, analytical solution to assess the error. For
HyperSolve, the Method of Manufactured Solutions is used to verify the implementation of the models and numerical
schemes. In MMS, an analytical solution is chosen for each solution variable. Since the solution does not exactly satisfy
the governing equations, source terms are added to the right-hand-side of the equation. Several verification test results
were described in Ref. [1].
</p>
<p>New verification tests have been added to HyperSolve that use the software library called Manufactured Analytical
Solution Abstraction (MASA) [19]. This library provides both the solution and source terms for many manufactured
solutions, spanning the Euler equations, the Navier-Stokes equations, and the Favre-averaged Navier Stokes (FANS)
equations with a Spalart-Allmaras (SA) model. One important aspect of MASA is that it is developed independently of
the HyperSolve code; the verification tests using MASA compare the HyperSolve models and discretization with a
solution produced by a third party. This reduces the amount of “teaching to the test” that occurs in verification. The
following sections focus on the MASA tests for the full Favre-averaged Navier-Stokes equations, though MASA has also
been used to test the Euler and Navier-Stokes equations.
</p>

<p>B. Near-Wall Manufactured Solution
This is the “physical” or near-wall manufactured solution described by Oliver et al. [20]. It uses an idealized
</p>
<p>boundary layer profile. The paramters, such as ` and 𝑝0, were set to the values in Ref. [20] with no changes. The
boundary-layer like solutions can be seen in Fig. 2. While Oliver et al. [20] tested both a low and high Reynolds number
solution for the Spalart-Allmaras turbulence model, only the high Reynolds number is shown. When tested, the low
Reynolds number just produced a laminar boundary layer and did not engage the turbulence model. This was not
considered a valuable test of the coupled FANS equations.

<p>Four meshes were used, each with dimensions of 0.5m×0.03m. The coarsest grid had 69×9 nodes in the streamwise
and wall-normal directions, respectively. The finest grid had 513×65 nodes. Each successive mesh was refined by a
factor of 2 in both directions. A quadrilateral mesh was setup for each grid, then decomposed into triangle elements.
Decomposing into the quadrilateral mesh into triangle elements was done to ensure that the order-of-accuracy is not
affected by a biased element splitting. The convergence of the high Reynolds-number case is shown in Figure 3, where
ℎ0 is the average grid scale on the coarsest mesh. Better than second-order convergence was observed for all variables.

<p>IV. Functional Equivalence Tests
Each functional equivalence test (FET) is run by both the LAURA and HyperSolve codes on an identical multiblock
</p>
<p>structured mesh to confirm that HyperSolve is able to provide a similar level of fidelity as LAURA with similar mesh
resolution. Because the HyperSolve code is an unstructured mesh solver, the multiblock structured mesh is converted
into an unstructured mesh of hexahedral elements automatically upon startup. The structured mesh is adapted using
the standard LAURA adaptation algorithm [21] to align the outer boundary to the bow shock and cluster points in
the boundary region near the vehicle surface. Comparisons of the surface pressure and heat flux are made between
the HyperSolve and LAURA codes. The choice of pressure and heat flux is due to the typical aerodynamic and
aerothermodynamic analysis needs of EDL planetary applications. The tolerance of ±4% relative agreement between
the codes is chosen based on a similar exercise done between the FUN3D and LAURA codes [6]. Table 2 lists the
thermodynamic model and freestream density 𝜌∞, velocity 𝑉∞, and temperature 𝑇∞, used for each of the validation
cases considered in this work.

<p>C. 2D Cylinder
This is a standard test case in the literature, with the conditions and 5-species air gas model described in Ref. [2].
</p>
<p>Figure 6a shows that the surface heat flux and pressure predicted by HyperSolve are in excellent agreement with the
LAURA predictions at all radial positions, 𝑍 , normalized by the cylinder radius, 𝑅. Figure 6b shows a comparison of the
species mass fractions predicted by both codes along the stagnation line, with the HyperSolve predictions matching the
LAURA predictions to within 4% except near the bow shock. This demonstrates equivalent chemical nonequilibrium by
both codes, with the small differences near the bow shock attributable to the differences in the shock capturing abilities
of both codes.

The conditions and geometry for this AGARD Working Group 18 wind tunnel case are described in Ref. [22]. A
</p>
<p>5-species air model identical to the one used in Section IV.C is employed here, although chemical nonequilibrium effects
are not expected for these flow conditions. An isothermal wall boundary condition is employed using a noncatalytic

<p>V. Concluding Remarks and Future Work
A verification and validation study was conducted to ensure that the models and numerical schemes employed in
</p>
<p>HyperSolve compare favorably with the flight-validated benchmark solver, LAURA. The MASA library was used to
perform code verification using the method of manufactured solutions, demonstrating that HyperSolve achieved design
order of accuracy in multiple scenarios. Grid convergence studies on sinusoidal and “physical” near-wall manufactured
solutions demonstrated the expected second-order accuracy when solving the FANS equations using a modified version
of the Spalart-Allmaras turbulence model.
</p>
<p>To validate the ideal gas, thermochemical nonequilibrium, and FANS turbulence model capabilities in HyperSolve,
a series of validation cases were run with the HyperSolve and LAURA codes to compare surface pressure and heat flux
predictions. Each case was run on an identical mesh by HyperSolve and LAURA to confirm that HyperSolve is able to
provide a similar level of fidelity as LAURA with similar mesh resolution. In general, the predicted surface pressure
and heat flux matched the LAURA predictions to within a ±4% relative difference. All cases exhibited differences
in pressure along the symmetry plane near the bow shock and most cases also exhibited differences near the outflow
plane. The differences near the bow shock are expected due to differences in the numerics of the flux capturing schemes
used by HyperSolve and LAURA. The differences near the outflow plane could be explained by the differences in the
implementation of the outflow state extrapolation boundary condition employed in HyperSolve and LAURA. None of
these differences in the flowfield pressure strongly affected the surface heat flux and pressure agreement between the
codes.
</p>
<p>Future work will include investigating the difference in surface heat flux seen in the Mars 2020 leeside region, which
was the only case that showed a disagreement exceeding 4% between the codes. Additionally, the functional equivalence
testing will be expanded to include higher enthalpy conditions that verify the ionization and thermal nonequilibrium
capabilities implemented in HyperSolve.
</p>
