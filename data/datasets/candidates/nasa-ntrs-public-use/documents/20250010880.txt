<p>Validation of Computational Fluid Dynamics Model of Pressure
Control in a Flight-scale Liquid Hydrogen Tank using a Spray
</p>
<p>Bar
</p>
<p>Chintan S. Patel ∗
</p>
<p>Qualis LLC (Amentum Space Exploration Division), NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, AL
</p>
<p>A state-of-the-art computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling methodology is developed to
simulate depressurization tests of a flight-scale cryogenic propellant tank using a spray bar. The
two-phase flow solver Loci-Stream-VOF models liquid and gaseous hydrogen as Eulerian fluid,
while sub-grid scale liquid hydrogen spray droplets are represented by Lagrangian particles.
The Lagrangian and Eulerian solvers are two-way coupled. Phase change for both Lagrangian
droplets and Eulerian gas-liquid interface is modeled with thermal equilibrium model or gas
kinetic theory based Hertz-Knudsen-Schrage model. Turbulence is captured with an improved
delayed detached eddy simulation, and an asymmetric interface turbulence transport model is
also employed to ensure accurate phase change and thermal gradient predictions at the Eulerian
two-phase interface. Droplet breakup, coalescence and scattering are modeled based on Weber
number. Without any model parameter calibration, the methodology predicts depressurization
time with errors of +0.28% for the 50% fill height test and -11.27% for the 90% fill height test,
demonstrating a robust Lagrangian-Eulerian model for predicting pressure control with spray
bar to support NASA’s human spaceflight missions.
</p>

<p>II. Introduction
</p>
<p>Cryogenic fluid management (CFM) is a critical enabling technology for the National Aeronautics and Space
Administration’s (NASA) Moon and Mars human-landing missions. CFM addresses the challenges of safe, low-cost,
</p>
<p>and reliable in-space and on-surface long-term propellant storage; pressure control and propellant positioning within
tanks for safe engine operations; and in-space and on-surface propellant transfer.
</p>
<p>NASA’s Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) has established the CFM Portfolio Project (CFMPP) to
advance CFM technologies to enable future spaceflight missions. In addition to advanced hardware development and
demonstration, there is a need for reliable predictive modeling of CFM systems and their operations to support rapid,
reliable, and low-cost spacecraft design and operation. The CFM Modeling Portfolio within the CFMPP improves the
modeling capabilities.
</p>
<p>CFD modeling is indispensable for high-fidelity predictions and for providing insight into flow phenomena that are
difficult or impossible to measure or test. Under the CFM Modeling Portfolio, the Propulsion Fluid Dynamics Branch
(ER42) within the Engineering Directorate at NASA Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC) is assessing, improving,
developing and validating the Loci-Stream-VOF CFD solver to predict CFM operations in support of current and future
flight projects such as the Human Landing System and Commercial Lunar Payload Services.
</p>
<p>One of the CFM operations of importance is depressurizing the propellant tank to maintain its structural integrity.
Venting of the ullage gas, and injection of liquid propellant in spray or jet form are among the common techniques used
for this purpose. This study’s focus is to formulate and validate a simulation methodology to predict propellant tank
</p>
<p>2</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>depressurization with injected liquid sprays. It is a multi-physics problem requiring modeling of two-phase turbulent
flow; heat transfer; phase transition at the gas-liquid interfaces including the droplets; and droplet breakup, collision,
and coalescence modeling.
</p>
<p>For Eulerian fluid modeling, the present work applies a CFD modeling methodology that has been validated for
prediction of self-pressurization [? ? ], autogenous pressurization [? ], pressure control using an axial jet, and
non-condensable pressurization (planned publication).
</p>
<p>Modeling of droplet vaporization has been studied for several decades in the context of combustion applications [? ?
], but few analytical studies predict spray injection in stored cryogenic propellants. Early nodal studies [? ? ] pioneered
numerical predictions, but because they could not include detailed flow dynamics, they had relatively higher error
than modern CFD predictions [? ? ], which have improved prediction accuracy. The study presented here provides a
comprehensive CFD methodology that requires no calibration of the physics models while delivering high accuracy.
</p>
<p>III. Test Descriptions
</p>
<p>A. Testing Facilities
Flight-scale ground tests of self-pressurization were conducted at NASA MSFC’s test stand 300 during 1996-1998
</p>
<p>with the multipurpose hydrogen test bed (MHTB) tank (Figure 1a). The 5083 aluminum tank was a double domed
cylindrical tank of diameter 3.05𝑚 with 3.05𝑚 total height including two 2:1 oblate hemispheres. The test tank with
cryogenic insulation and supporting equipment was enclosed in a cylindrical environmental shroud (Figure 1b). The
tank was equipped with a thermal venting system (TVS) and a recirculation pump near the bottom of the tank and a
spray bar/heat exchanger along the major axis of the tank. Two vertical rakes in the tank housed 24 temperature probes
for characterizing the fluid temperatures. Entire assembly was housed in a vacuum chamber with liquid nitrogen cold
walls. [? ]
</p>
<p>The spray bar (Figure 2) has four spray tubes each with 21 injector holes of 1.7𝑚𝑚 diameter and was ≈ 0.5𝑚 offset
from the tank’s major axis (Figure 3).
</p>

<p>B. Experimental Procedure
The tank was filled with liquid hydrogen to a desired fill level and then vented to within a set pressure range for
</p>
<p>several hours to cool the tank structure. The ambient heat leaks to the tank were calculated with boil-off testing. Boil-off
testing also set-up consistent initial condition for tank self-pressurization and TVS tests. Two heat leak conditions were
tested. For low heat leak set-up, the walls of the vacuum chamber containing the tank were cooled with liquid nitrogen
</p>
<p>3</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>to minimize the heat leakage. The high heat leak set-up consisted of additional venting and recirculation hardware at the
bottom of the tank which introduced more conductive heat leakage. Additionally, the liquid nitrogen was not circulated
through the vacuum chamber walls. [? ]
</p>
<p>After a desired conditioning of the hardware was achieved, the tank was sealed, and the self-pressurization test
began. When the set upper pressure limit of 137.9𝑘𝑃𝑎 was reached, the self-pressurization test concluded. Then the
pressure control test began with spray bar activation.
</p>
<p>The circulation pump was turned on to feed subcooled liquid hydrogen from the bottom of the tank (Figure 2a) to
the spray bar/heat exchanger (Figure 2b) at a constant mass flow rate of 0.136𝑘𝑔/𝑠. The liquid hydrogen then passed
through the spray tubes (Figure 2b and 2c) then injector holes to exist as sprayed droplets. The droplets cooled the ullage
gas by two means: (1) sensible heat transfer, and (2) latent heat transfer due to droplet evaporation and depressurizes the
tank. The pump is operated until the lower pressure limit of 131𝑘𝑃𝑎 was reached and the spray bar pressure control test
ended.
</p>
<p>Two tests were selected for CFD model validation: (1) 51 W heat leak test with 50% fill height, and (2) 54.1 W heat
leak test with 90% fill height.
</p>

<p>IV. Computational Model
The governing equations and models described below are implemented in Loci-Stream CFD solver — a pressure-
</p>
<p>based, all-speed CFD solver for generalized meshes in the rule-based programming framework Loci [? ? ]. Loci [? ] is
a rule-based declarative framework that automatically assembles the numerical solution components of an arbitrary set
</p>
<p>4</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>of partial differential equations and enables solution on large parallel computing systems for efficient parallelization
across thousands of CPU processes. This technique enhances the flexibility of simulation tools, reducing the complexity
of CFD software that includes various boundary conditions, complex geometries, and different physical models. The
Lagrangian particle transport capabilities described in this section are adapted from Loci-Chem CFD solver. [? ]
</p>

<p>1. Frozen Two-phase Interface Model
The frozen or stationary gas-liquid interface model, also referred to as sharp interface, takes advantage of a
</p>
<p>stationary/quasi-stationary interface. The frozen-interface method enforces a sharp, mesh-defined phase boundary,
eliminating volume of fluid (VOF) advection and geometric reconstruction. An order of magnitude larger timestep is
afforded by time-implicit frozen-interface method compared to typical time-explicit VOF methods with strict Courant
number based stability criteria. The spurious/parasitic interfacial velocities commonly observed in VOF advection are
also avoided, allowing careful resolution of thermal gradients in interfacial region without the stability and affordability
problems faces by VOF transport.
</p>
<p>Velocity is continuous at the interface/phase boundary. Tangential velocities on the gas and liquid sides of the
interface are equal(Equation 5)and so is tangential shear stress (Equation 6). Interface normal velocity is assumed to
be zero (Equation 7) — even when phase change occurs because the phase change mass transfer and corresponding
velocities are very small.
</p>

<p>3. Turbulence Modeling
Menter’s 2003 shear stress transport (SST) [? ] is used for Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) turbulence
</p>
<p>closure. An improved delayed detached eddy simulation (IDDES) approach [? ] is also utilized for turbulence
modeling. IDDES is a hybrid RANS/LES (Large Eddy Simulation) approach: near-wall regions and unresolved scales —
high-shear or high-vorticity regions where mesh refinement for LES is impractical — are modeled with RANS equations
and resolved scales are modeled with sub-grid scale (SGS) modeling. An implicit LES approach is employed where
grid-induced dissipation provides the sub-grid scales. A blending/shielding function 𝑓𝑑 distinguishes the resolved (LES)
and unresolved (RANS) regions: RANS mode is indicated by 𝑓𝑑 = 1 and SGS mode is indicated by 𝑓𝑑 = 0.
</p>
<p>4. Interface Turbulence Transport
Menter (1994), Wilcox (1998) and others have shown that eddy-viscosity based turbulence models predict non-
</p>
<p>physically high turbulence near the interface compared to the experimental observations [? ]. A review of the two-phase
literature [? ] shows that, near the gas-liquid interface, turbulence on the gas side resembles a wall viscous boundary
layer, whereas the liquid side has either slightly enhanced or unchanged turbulence kinetic energy. [? ? ? ? ? ]
RANS and hybrid RANS models must therefore be modified near the interface to capture these altered turbulence
characteristics.
</p>
<p>A common approach proposed by Egorov [? ] modifies the 𝜔-equation with a source term that adds a wall-like
treatment to reduce turbulence kinetic energy at the interface. This approach has shown to be effective in wide-ranging
applications [? ]. The physical rationale is that, for two-phase flows with high density ratios, the lower-density fluid
effectively "sees" the higher-density fluid as a wall. However, Egorov’s method is typically applied symmetrically to
both phases. An asymmetric treatment in which the near wall turbulence characteristics on the heavier fluid side are free
of boundary layer effect (i.e., slip-wall-like turbulence) is more appropriate [? ].
</p>
<p>These observations are encapsulated in our simple but highly effective interface turbulence model for frozen interface
method [? ? ], in which the gas side of the interface is treated as wall-like while the liquid side is unaffected and has
no boundary influence on the turbulence characteristics. This asymmetric turbulence damping technique has been
instrumental in validation with multiple tests [? ? ], including the present work; an additional validation work is planned
for future publication.
</p>

is adopted, i.e., the particle density is assumed to be much larger than the fluid density, allowing omission of added-mass

<p>H. Discretization and Solver Set-up
SIMPLEC pressure-velocity coupling is used for solving the Navier-Stokes equations. Second order upwind (SOU)
</p>
<p>flux scheme is used for all but turbulence transport equations which use first order upwind scheme for increased stability.
Second order backward difference formula is used for time integration. All simulations are well-converged with at least
three orders of magnitude residual drop during each timestep.
</p>
<p>Spatial and temporal resolution insensitivities are discussed in the Results section. Timestep size of 0.1𝑠 is sufficient
for temporal-resolution-invariant predictions. To model phase change, the thermal balance scheme is used for the
Eulerian flow and thermal evaporation model is used for the Lagrangian flow.
</p>
<p>For Lagrangian particles, initial particle diameter is assumed to be the same as the injector diameter and allowed to
break up and coalesce as per the breakup and coalescence models described above. In a typical set-up, a parcel initially
contains 0.001 particles to allow for increase in the number of particles per parcel due to breakup. Spray is assumed to
have 20-deg cone spread angle. Insensitivity to initial particles per parcel, spray spread angle, and variation in the inlet
spray velocity are demonstrated in the Results section.
</p>

<p>B. Flow Dynamics — Insights from CFD
Multiple interacting physical phenomena must be modeled with sufficient fidelity to achieve parameter-free validation
</p>
<p>across both tests.
The spray droplets rapidly breakup upon their introduction to the tank because their Weber number is higher than
</p>
<p>the critical Weber number. Figure 7 shows a close-up view of the topmost injectors with parcels of different size —
generated by the breakup and coalescence model, along with the Eulerian vapor temperature field. Figure 8a shows the
distribution of parcels at 20 s for the 50% fill height test. There is a continuous cascade of larger parcels to smaller
length-scales. The CFD predictions show that the existing droplets continue to breakup from millimeter scale to
micrometer scale to even lower length scales — very strongly suggesting liquid hydrogen misting and atomization in the
tank. Large surface area provided by the microscale droplets enhances heat and mass transfer between the droplets and
the ullage.
</p>
<p>The smaller droplets enhance drag and slow down the spray velocity. Figure 8b shows significantly reduced particle
speeds compared to their inlet speeds of 6.4 m/s. The droplet deceleration increases the resident time of the droplets in
the ullage, helping enhance the heat and mass transfer to cool and depressurize the ullage. Additionally, the cascade of
droplet breakup produces droplets small enough that some remain suspended and are carried by the Eulerian flow in the
ullage, providing strong evidence for misting/atomization, and further enhancing droplet resident time in the ullage for
enhanced heat and mass transfer.

<p>F. Accommodation Factor Sensitivity
Accommodation factor in the Hertz-Knudsen-Schrage equation (Equation 9) is a coefficient that linearly modulates
</p>
<p>the rate of evaporation/condensation. Its value is expected to be a function of fluid properties, and thermodynamic state.
Some experimentally calculated values of the accommodation factor are available in literature for certain fluids but
tend to vary in a wide range. This uncertainty is greater concern for thermodynamically open systems where mass
is added/removed from the domain. But for the accommodation factor value greater than 1𝑒 − 4, the closed systems
predictions have historically shown to be independent of accommodation factor [? ? ] when sufficient interfacial mesh
resolution is provided. The sensitivity analyses presented here (Figure 16) conform to these historical observations.

<p>G. Parcel Injection Rate
Lagrangian particle transport is a Monte-Carlo simulation. For a given particle mass flow rate and particle size(s),
</p>
<p>the parcel injection rate determines how many particles are initially held in a parcel. For a fixed particle mass flow rate,
increasing parcel injection rate reduces each the number of Lagrangian particles per injected parcel. When one particle
per parcel is injected the Monte-Carlo simulation becomes a direct simulation of every particle.
</p>
<p>With the particle breakup model, a breakup of an injected droplet would increase the number of particles in a parcel,
so fractional particles per injected parcel to ensure there are not too many particles in a parcel after the breakup. Since
the breakup depends on the flow parameters, necessary parcel injection rate is not known a priori. A wide range of
parcel injection rates are therefore tested for their impact on depressurization prediction.
</p>
<p>Initially, 1 particle per parcel is injected at injector hole diameter as a baseline. Up to 1000 times the baseline
injection rate is simulated, i.e., 1𝑒 − 3 particles per parcel are injected. The resulting breakup for each simulation results
in a distribution of number of particles per parcel. A distribution mode of 1, i.e., at when the Monte-Carlo simulation
becomes a direct simulation, the depressurization prediction becomes independent of parcel injection rate as shown in
Figure 17. The particle per parcel distribution is shown in Figure 18 for parcel injection rate 100 times higher than
the baseline. Most parcels have 1 or fewer particles in it, which correlates to virtual insensitivity to increasing parcel
injection rate on depressurization rate prediction (Figure 17).

<p>VI. Conclusion
CFD methodology for simulating Lagrangian-Eulerian multiphase flow modeling is developed for validation using
</p>
<p>MHTB tank spray bar depressurization tests. The predictions compare well with the tests with relative depressurization
time errors of +0.28% for the 50% fill height test and -11.27% for the 90% fill height test. A parameter-free validation
against flight-scale MHTB tank pressure control strengthens confidence in applying CFD methodology to support
NASA’s future spaceflight projects.
</p>
<p>Weber number based Lagrangian droplet break-up and droplet coalescence models adapted from non-cryogenic
literature performed well in conjunction with the Eulerian fluid model as evidenced by accurate prediction of
</p>
<p>17</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Fig. 19 Turbulence Model Sensitivity Fig. 20 Interface turbulence model sensitivity
</p>
<p>depressurization rate. These models dictate total droplet surface area available for heat and mass transfer and are
therefore essential for accurate modeling of cooling for the ullage and depressurization of the tank.
</p>
<p>Careful turbulence transport is crucial for reliable modeling of spaceflight CFM operations. Consistent with the
previous CFM validation efforts, asymmetric turbulence transport at the interface is again demonstrated to be essential
for accurate phase change and consequently depressurization prediction.
</p>
<p>The frozen interface model avoids volume of fluid method’s limitations of spurious interface velocities and strict
timestep requirement, enabling resolution of refined length/time scales at faster simulation times. Effort to apply the
CFD methodology from this study to VOF simulation is ongoing.
</p>
<p>The CFD predictions provide indirect insights into the spray depressurization process, which may be difficult
to measure and visualize through experiments: the droplet breakup, droplet-droplet interactions, and consequently
misting/atomization with their size distributions and their impact on cryogenic fluid management operations.
</p>
<p>For future work, two-way conversion capability between Lagrangian particle and volume of fluid method has been
developed. At a critical mass/volume fraction of Lagrangian particle, the particles can convert to Eulerian liquid and
vice versa. Future work should test this new model for simulation of moving and morphing gas-liquid interfaces with
Lagrangian particle transport.
