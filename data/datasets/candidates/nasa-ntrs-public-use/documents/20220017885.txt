<p>1 INTRODUCTION 
</p>
<p>Long-duration, deep-space exploration missions present significant challenges to crew 
health and performance. These challenges include the individual and combined effects 
of microgravity, radiation exposure, isolation, limited resources (mass, volume, power, 
data, and crew time), limited options for evacuation, and those associated with delayed 
or constrained communications. Each of these challenges necessitates greater degrees 
of crew autonomy as our distance from Earth increases. Specifically, as communication 
delays intensify - and evacuation capability diminishes the further we explore space - 
the unqualified need for Earth-independent medical operations focused on autonomous 
diagnosis, treatment and prevention will become key to mission continuation and 
success. This need will be especially true should a crewmember become ill or injured 
wherein treatment and disposition “in-situ” ultimately falls to the crew itself to determine. 
To augment the requisite knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs) of a time-constrained 
exploration mission crew operating under stressful conditions, combatting fatigue, and 
facing a potential medical crisis, a robust clinical decision support system (CDSS) is a 
probable solution. A CDSS would facilitate, guide, and inform Earth-independent 
medical operations while assisting crewmembers through various clinical presentations. 
The CDSS would allow crewmembers to take advantage of pre-mission training tied to 
the in-flight/in-mission use of pre-planned protocols that offer both a range of diagnostic 
options and “just-in-time” (refamiliarization) training and assistance. CDSS will expand 
such capabilities by improving the utility and effectiveness of various available 
diagnostic, treatment, and health maintenance tools, techniques, and measures. 
The Exploration Medical Capability (ExMC) Element of the Human Research Program 
(HRP) is expanding the boundaries of space medical systems to advance the care of 
astronauts on future exploration missions beyond low Earth orbit. ExMC is actively 
identifying and testing next-generation medical care and crew health maintenance 
technologies. The Clinical Decision Support (CDS) project addresses gap Medical-701 
within the Inflight Medical Conditions risk: “Enhance medical capabilities within an 
exploration medical system.” Though mass, volume, and power will certainly and 
necessarily face increasing constraints in deep-space exploration mission sets, the 
projected computational capabilities of spacecraft systems will increase exponentially as 
information technology continues to advance this decade and beyond. Hence, data, 
software, and computational resources will play an essential and synergistic role in 
maintaining crew health, wellness, and performance in deep space missions. 
The focus of the CDS project is to develop recommended requirements for an in-vehicle 
CDSS that acts as a “virtual “assistant for delivering optimal health, performance, and 
medical care during exploration missions. The CDSS is envisioned as a lightweight, fully 
integrated software-based tool deployed on a laptop computer or handheld device but 
capable of integration with the spacecraft system. The CDSS will assist both crew and 
ground support as needed or as communications delays/challenges allow. Crew and 
ground support staff will interact with clinically relevant knowledge/databases (e.g., 
medical practice and records, pharmacy, schedule, imaging files), instrumentation (e.g., 
imaging, physiological monitoring devices), and habitat (e.g., wellness system, task 
performance system) and vehicle systems (e.g., environmental system, communication 
system). In addition, the CDS human interface will employ contextually based reasoning </p>

relevant information that would otherwise increase cognitive load. This framework will 
reduce task saturation by facilitating effective assimilation and efficient use of time-
sensitive, medically relevant information. The CDSS will simultaneously combine, 
collate, and characterize large, disparate data sets providing the user(s) with critical 
insights and actionable items to help make viable, mission-sparing health and wellness-
related decisions. The architecture of an ideal spaceflight CDSS is to include core and 
advanced analytical features that incorporate work from collaborators while maintaining 
a flexible, “learning” platform capable of integrating new technology within its framework 
in future updates and versions.

<p>1.1 PURPOSE 
</p>
<p>Use Cases defined in this document are intended to derive essential functionalities for 
the CDSS based on the ExMC and CDSS ConOps documents, while also highlighting 
how the CDSS architecture enables extensibility, a data pipeline, integrated artificial 
intelligence (AI) models, user interface and user experience. This approach 
demonstrates how externally developed analyses and tests critical for a comprehensive 
CDSS, such as AI algorithms or decision tree type models, can be added to expand 
clinical decision-making capabilities. This document identifies several datasets and 
workflows necessary for implementation of the use cases identified. 
Specifically, the purpose of this Use Case document is to inform CDSS software 
implementation development activities. To fully develop requirements for an exploration 
CDSS, software implementation will provide the necessary insights. These use cases 
are established to exercise a range of components of a CDSS and will result in the 
development of CDSS requirements. In addition, the project will also assess impacts 
data system functionality to enable a final update of Crew Health and Performance 
(CHP) Habitat Data System requirements. 
</p>
<p>1.2 SCOPE 
</p>
<p>The use cases developed in this document are not a comprehensive set of methods 
and manners in which a CDSS will be operated. Rather, the use cases are intended to 
represent examples where a CDSS is envisioned to be an integral part of the CHP 
System. Figure 2, reproduced from ExMC CDSS Architecture Recommendation (HRP-
48032), shows the CHP System as represented by several subsystems. Central to the 
CHP is the Data System Domain, which is where the CDSS is anticipated to reside. The 
use cases defined here are specifically intended to drive functionality that demonstrate 
select aspects of clinical decision-making to be performed in a CDSS. These use cases 
were initially conceived and generated at the beginning of the CDSS requirements 
development process and included a broad range of concepts that were known to 
potentially exceed project development resources. Hence, select aspects of the use 
cases were prioritized for implementation. The components and the extent of the 
specific implementations are noted at the end of the use case description. However, the 
content will be archived for project historical purposes and future reference for future 
CDS project implementation. </p>

<p>3.2.4 Assumptions 
Keyboard timing and response time is a relevant method to measure cognitive function. 
Based on Ribeiro et al. (2013), the use case will use key down time, error per key and 
time between clicks. The model will not adapt to personal typing styles in this 
implementation.  
 
The CDSS can potentially use keyboard usage information such as timing to determine 
levels of cognitive fatigue and neurological conditions. Cognitive and neural conditions 
may present with symptoms such as headache which are usually followed up with 
neurological exams such as upper limb tests that utilize movements like keyboard 
usage. Coslett et al. (2009) showed a difference in response times were greater with 
patients suffering from focal hemisphere lesions. Brizan et al. (2005) showed keyboard 
usage could detect cognitive load on 350 subjects using 450 characters. Al-Libawy et al. 
(2016) used keyboard entry on a smart phone to determine fatigue levels. Text entry 
error rate was used to represent psychomotor measures of alertness/fatigue and was 
modelled with a support vector machine model to classify fatigue. This was a binary 
classifier which may be insufficient for the CDSS but shows promise to this approach. 
Ribeiro et al. (2013) report keyboard and mouse usage can be used to detect mental 
fatigue. Features used include key down time, errors per key pressed, mouse velocity, 
mouse acceleration, time between keys, total excess of distance, average excess of 
distance, double click speed, number of double clicks, distance while clicking, signed 
sum of angles, absolute sum of angles, time between clicks. A k-NN (k nearest 
neighbor) model was used on features of significance. Interestingly all features except 
time between clicks where corelated with fatigue which. Fatigue was loosely controlled 
at the beginning and end of workday. The data collection application was in the 
background and did not interfere with the persons tasks. The three most significant 
features were key down time, error per key and time between clicks. 
</p>
<p>3.2.5 Architecture

cognitive model will use this information to present a cognitive fatigue value between 0 
to 10. There will be an alarm value default set to 4. When this threshold drops below a 
value of four, the CDSS will alert the CMO and user. 
 
Future development may consider use of alerts to initiate a diagnose/treat session 
resulting in a treatment suggestion.

<p>3.4 Collapsed Lung (Pneumothorax) 
</p>
<p>3.4.1 Context 
Pneumothorax is introduced to get the CDSS closer to a real clinical intervention where 
the initial diagnosis is incorrect. The system will perform a diagnosis and treatment and 
introduces an ‘observe’ state, where the patient does not improve, and the system goes 
back and re-diagnoses with the new information that the treatment didn’t not work. The 
inference engine will be updated to take this into account.  
 
This scenario was developed to illustrate how the CDSS can diagnose a condition, 
recommend treatments, and confirm or change diagnoses based on follow- up 
assessments. The diagnostic algorithm must correctly evaluate the probability of the 
most likely conditions as well as the probability of the most dangerous conditions 
associated with the patient’s symptoms. CDSS will use information from history, 
context, symptoms, vital signs, blood and urine labs and passive sensors to guide 
targeted diagnostic testing to reduce the probability of the dangerous conditions below 
an acceptable lower limit and increase the probability of the most likely condition above 
the threshold to recommend treatments. A period of observation with reassessment can 
be used to confirm an initial diagnosis and validate a treatment plan or, if a CM does not 
respond to an intervention, CDSS will use this information to update the probability lists 
and recommend a new course of action. 
 
This narrative presents an unplanned event where the CM initiates a session with the 
CDSS following an injury. After an initial work-up, the CDSS recommends treatment, </p>

<p>4.3 Personalized Nutrition 
</p>
<p>These scenarios were developed to maintain health quality by demonstrating the ability 
to note nutritional deficiencies in multiple CMs. In Narratives A &amp; B, an individual's 
nutritional status can be determined by integrating various factors, including food intake, 
physiological health, omics, metabolism, and physical activity measures. Finally, 
narrative C demonstrates how the system can consider trending data to support the 
validation of anomalous test results. The use of a data-driven approach will require 
developing a personalized health data system comprised of advanced computational 
technologies. 
</p>
<p>4.4 Personalized Medicine 
</p>
<p>Diagnostics and treatment are personalized. e.g. dosage-to-effectiveness for pain, 
massage vs. stretches. In addition, the two fields, “most common last choices for the 
CM” and “what others chose,” are modelled and displayed.  
 
Models are adaptive to people over time. For example they will learn your typing style 
during pre-mission training and this knowledge base will be loaded into the vehicle CDS 
before take-off. During the mission a CM injures a finger which affects their typing for a 
month. During the mission the keyboard wears out leading to longer key press times 
and the ‘a’ key failing. The crew decide to use the ‘z’ key and the model needs to adapt 
to not have false alerts of cognition scores.  
</p>
<p>4.5 Bone Health 
</p>
<p>This scenario was developed to assess the risk for bone fracture injuries to the crew 
and recommendations for providing mitigation strategies. Spaceflight-induced bone loss 
is an adaptive response to reduced loading or forces to the skeleton due to a lack of 
gravity. Spaceflight conditions that reduce bone mass and changes to bone structure 
could result in bone fractures during or after a mission. Therefore, astronauts are at an 
increased risk for fracture, given the hazardous environment of spaceflight mission 
operations. An individual's bone strength can be determined by integrating multiple </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> ExMC CDS Use Cases for CDSS  HRP-48069 Rev. B 
</p>
<p>43 
 
</p>
<p>factors, including bone mass, bone mineral density, geometry, and bone 
microarchitecture. The use of a data-driven approach will require developing a 
personalized bone health system comprised of advanced analytics for assessing bone 
fragility and providing mitigation options to lessen impacts on bone health. A potential 
narrative is a planned evaluation prior to EVA. 
</p>
