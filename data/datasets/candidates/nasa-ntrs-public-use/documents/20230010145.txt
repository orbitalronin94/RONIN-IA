<p>EARLY RESULTS FROM NOAA-21 (JPSS-2) VIIRS ON-ORBIT CALIBRATION 
 
</p>
<p>X. Xiong1, A. Angal2, J. Sun2, N. Lei2, K. Twedt2, and K. Chiang2 
 
</p>
<p>1Sciences and Exploration Directorate, NASA/GSFC, Greenbelt, MD 20771, USA 
2Science Systems and Applications, Inc., Lanham, MD 20706, USA 
</p>
<p> 
 
</p>
<p>ABSTRACT 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Launched on November 10, 2022, the NOAA-21 (N-21) 
</p>
<p>VIIRS has successfully completed its initial post-launch 
</p>
<p>testing (PLT) and intensive calibration and validation (ICV) 
</p>
<p>activities. It is now operated in its nominal configuration 
</p>
<p>and characterized using measurements from its on-board 
</p>
<p>calibrators (OBC) and lunar observations. In this paper, we 
</p>
<p>provide a brief description of N-21 VIIRS on-orbit operation 
</p>
<p>and calibration activities and present results derived from its 
</p>
<p>early mission performance assessments, including examples 
</p>
<p>of its OBC performance, spectral band responses, as well as 
</p>
<p>detector signal to noise characteristics. As shown in this 
</p>
<p>paper, the overall performance of N-21 VIIRS is better than 
</p>
<p>that of its predecessor currently operated on the S-NPP and 
</p>
<p>comparable to the one onboard the N-20, with an exception 
</p>
<p>of relatively large changes in its SWIR band responses. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Index Terms - NOAA-21, VIIRS, on-orbit calibration 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>1. INTRODUCTION 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The VIIRS is one of the key instruments on-board the JPSS-
</p>
<p>2 satellite, also referred to as the N-21 following its launch 
</p>
<p>on November 10, 2022, currently operated in tandem with 
</p>
<p>S-NPP and NOAA-20 (N-20) satellites launched on October 
</p>
<p>28, 2011 and November 18, 2017, respectively. Near-
</p>
<p>identical VIIRS instruments will also be onboard the JPSS-3 
</p>
<p>and JPSS-4 satellites that are scheduled to launch in 2027 
</p>
<p>and 2033, respectively [1]. The VIIRS collects data in 22 
</p>
<p>spectral bands, including M1-M16 (moderate resolution 
</p>
<p>bands), I1-I5 (imaging bands), and DNB (day and night 
</p>
<p>band). Among them, M1-M11 and I1-I3, with wavelengths 
</p>
<p>from 0.41 to 2.2 m, are the reflective solar bands (RSB) 
</p>
<p>and bands M12-M16 and I4-I5, covering wavelengths from 
</p>
<p>3.7 to 12 m, are the thermal emissive bands (TEB). Seven 
</p>
<p>of the M bands (M1-M5, M7, and M13) are the dual gain 
</p>
<p>bands that can make observations at either high- or low-
</p>
<p>gain. The DNB can collect data at three different gain stages 
</p>
<p>and thus covers an extremely large dynamic range. 
</p>
<p>The calibrated VIIRS observations or its sensor data 
</p>
<p>records (SDR) include scene radiances, reflectances, and 
</p>
<p>brightness temperatures and serve as input for producing 
</p>
<p>various environmental data records (EDR). The VIIRS EDR 
</p>
<p>support a broad range of operational applications and 
</p>
<p>scientific studies and further extend many of the long-term 
</p>
<p>data records generated from MODIS observations [2-6]. As 
</p>
<p>expected, the EDR quality depends strongly on the quality 
</p>
<p>of its SDR and thus the sensor on-orbit performance in 
</p>
<p>terms of its calibration accuracy and stability. This paper 
</p>
<p>provides a brief description of N-21 VIIRS early mission 
</p>
<p>operation and calibration activities, including the calibration 
</p>
<p>maneuvers, and presents its on-orbit performance using 
</p>
<p>examples derived from its OBC measurements and lunar 
</p>
<p>observations. Early results show that the N-21 VIIRS on-
</p>
<p>orbit performance meets its overall design requirements and 
</p>
<p>is generally better than its predecessor currently operated on 
</p>
<p>S-NPP and comparable to the one on N-20 [7-9]. An 
</p>
<p>exception is that the N-21 VIIRS has experienced relatively 
</p>
<p>large change in its SWIR band responses. Also discussed in 
</p>
<p>this paper are future calibration activities and improvement 
</p>
<p>strategies as more calibration data become available. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>2. ON-ORBIT CALIBRATION 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The VIIRS on-orbit calibration is performed primarily using 
</p>
<p>measurements made from a set of OBC that include a solar 
</p>
<p>diffuser (SD), a solar diffuser stability monitor (SDSM), and 
</p>
<p>a blackbody (BB). The SD provides on-orbit calibration 
</p>
<p>reference for the RSB and the BB calibrates the TEB. In 
</p>
<p>addition, regularly scheduled lunar observations made 
</p>
<p>through the instrument’s space view (SV) port provide an 
</p>
<p>independent stability monitoring for the RSB and calibration 
</p>
<p>adjustments on an as needed basis [7]. Illustrated in Fig. 1 
</p>
<p>are the VIIRS instrument, its rotating telescope assembly 
</p>
<p>(RTA), and on-board calibrators. 
</p>
<p>Shortly after launch, the N-21 experienced an anomaly 
</p>
<p>with its Ka-transmitter on December 16, 2022. Following a 
</p>
<p>series of comprehensive diagnostic testing, a decision was 
</p>
<p>made to switch to the secondary Ka-transmitter. This 
</p>
<p>anomaly led to a large data gap of 48 days. To date, the N-
</p>
<p>21 VIIRS has completed all the planned PLT and ICV 
</p>
<p>activities, including various calibration maneuvers and mid-
</p>
<p>mission outgassing (MMOG). It is now operated in a 
</p>
<p>nominal configuration with BB temperature set at 292.5 K 
</p>
<p>and cold focal plane assembly (CFPA) temperature set at 80 
</p>
<p>K (changed from initial setting of 82 K). The VIIRS SD 
</p>
<p>views the Sun through an attenuation screen and calibrates </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>the RSB every orbit. Its on-orbit degradation is tracked 
</p>
<p>regularly by the SDSM with 8 filtered detectors (D1 to D8) 
</p>
<p>covering wavelengths from 0.41 to 0.93 m. In general, the 
</p>
<p>SDSM operation is performed every orbit at mission 
</p>
<p>beginning and gradually reduced to daily and weekly 
</p>
<p>intervals. As of May 20, 2023, the N-21 has performed 3 
</p>
<p>scheduled lunar observations. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fig. 1 VIIRS instrument, its rotating telescope assembly 
</p>
<p>(RTA), and on-board calibrators (OBC). 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>In addition to roll maneuvers regularly performed for 
</p>
<p>the lunar observations, the spacecraft also performed the 
</p>
<p>yaw and pitch maneuvers at the mission beginning, allowing 
</p>
<p>VIIRS to collect special data to help validate, derive, and 
</p>
<p>improve several key calibration parameters, such as TEB 
</p>
<p>response versus scan-angle (RVS), the SD and SDSM 
</p>
<p>screen transmission functions, and the DNB offset tables. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>3. INSTRUMENT PERFORMANCE 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Due to frequent solar exposure, the VIIRS SD bidirectional 
</p>
<p>reflectance distribution function (BRDF) degrades on-orbit. 
</p>
<p>Fig. 2 shows the N-21 VIIRS SD on-orbit degradation since 
</p>
<p>launch at wavelengths of its detectors 1-8 (D1: 0.41 m, D2: 
</p>
<p>0.44 m, D3: 0.49 m, D4: 0.56 m, D5: 0.67 m, D6: 0.75 
</p>
<p>m, D7: 0.86 m, D8: 0.93 m). The large data gap (days 
</p>
<p>37-85) was due to the spacecraft Ka-transmitter anomaly 
</p>
<p>occurred shortly after launch. The overall SD degradation 
</p>
<p>trends of N-21 are very similar to that of S-NPP and N-21, 
</p>
<p>with large changes at short wavelengths. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fig. 2 SD on-orbit degradation at different detector 
</p>
<p>wavlengths (D1-D8: 0.41-0.93 m). 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The on-board BB is the calibration source for the TEB and 
</p>
<p>is nominally controlled at 292.5 K except during its warm-
</p>
<p>up/cool-down (WUCD) and spacecraft or instrument safe 
</p>
<p>hold events. Fig. 3 illustrates N-21 VIIRS BB performance, 
</p>
<p>in terms of its (a) short-term stability and (b) spatial 
</p>
<p>uniformity. The BB short-term stability is demonstrated by 
</p>
<p>the scan-by-scan temperatures averaged over its six 
</p>
<p>thermistors and its spatial uniformity by their standard 
</p>
<p>deviations. As shown in Fig. 3, the BB performance in the 
</p>
<p>nighttime orbits is more stable and uniform than in the 
</p>
<p>daytime orbits. During part of the daytime orbits, the N-21 
</p>
<p>VIIRS BB spatial uniformity fails to meet the specified 
</p>
<p>requirements of 30 mK. Meanwhile, both S-NPP and N-20 
</p>
<p>VIIRS BB show good uniformity that meets the specified 
</p>
<p>requirements. The N-21 VIIRS performed its first BB 
</p>
<p>WUCD on March 10, 2023, which enabled a more 
</p>
<p>comprehensive assessment of TEB on-orbit performance. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>Presented in Fig. 4 are on-orbit changes in spectral band 
</p>
<p>responses (or normalized gains) of N-21 VIIRS for (a) 
</p>
<p>bands with wavelengths less than 0.5 m (M1-M3); (b) 
</p>
<p>bands within 0.5-1.0 m (I1, I2, and M4-M7); (c) SWIR 
</p>
<p>bands (I3, M8-M11) with wavelengths in 1.0-2.3 m, and 
</p>
<p>(d) TEB (I4, I5, M12-M16) with wavelengths above 3.5 m. 
</p>
<p>The detector gains are equivalent to 1/F-factors with F-
</p>
<p>factors (or the calibration scaling factors) derived using 
</p>
<p>sensor responses to the calibration source or target, such as 
</p>
<p>on-board SD for the RSB and BB for the TEB. Results 
</p>
<p>shown in Fig. 4 are averaged over all detectors within a 
</p>
<p>band for HAM side-A and high gain (HG) for the dual gain 
</p>
<p>bands. For N-21, the VIS and NIR data became available 
</p>
<p>shortly after the instrument switched to its science mode on 
</p>
<p>Nov 20, 2022. Except for the orbits at very beginning, the 
</p>
<p>N-21 VIS and NIR responses have been stable. This 
</p>
<p>performance is very similar to that of N-20 VIIRS and better </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>than that of S-NPP. As mentioned earlier, the large data gap 
</p>
<p>in the spectral band responses was due to the spacecraft Ka-
</p>
<p>transmitter anomaly.     .

</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The SWIR bands and TEB are located on the CFPA. 
</p>
<p>Their on-orbit responses were not fully effective until 
</p>
<p>shortly after opening the instrument’s cryoradiator cooler 
</p>
<p>door on February 8, 2023 (or day 91 since launch). Unlike 
</p>
<p>spectral bands on the VIS/NIR focal plane, the SWIR bands 
</p>
<p>have shown a relatively large wavelength dependent 
</p>
<p>decrease in their responses (or detector gains), up to 5% for 
</p>
<p>M8 (1.24 m) and M9 (1.38 m). This feature is not present 
</p>
<p>in N-20 SWIR bands. Compared to S-NPP, the N-21 VIIRS 
</p>
<p>did not have large wavelength dependent degradation in its 
</p>
<p>NIR bands over the same performance period. Because of 
</p>
<p>large changes in SIWR responses, more frequent calibration 
</p>
<p>look-up table (LUT) updates are necessary in order to 
</p>
<p>maintain sensor on-orbit data quality. On the other hand, the 
</p>
<p>TEB overall responses have been very stable, less than 1.0% 
</p>
<p>for all bands, with most changes in the MWIR bands. Apart 
</p>
<p>from the large data gap at mission beginning, there have 
</p>
<p>been a few small data discontinuities or changes in spectral 
</p>
<p>band responses, especially for the TEB, which are due to 
</p>
<p>special operation or calibration events, such as MMOG, 
</p>
<p>change of CFPA setting from 82K to 80K, and deep space 
</p>
<p>pitch maneuver.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
Fig. 5 RSB along-scan and along-track BBR derived from 
</p>
<p>lunar observations. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Using regularly scheduled lunar observations, the 
</p>
<p>sensor spatial performance can be evaluated via its band-to-
</p>
<p>band registration (BBR). Fig. 5 displays the results of N-21 
</p>
<p>VIIRS RSB BBR in both along scan and track directions. 
</p>
<p>The BBR results in Fig. 5 are averaged over the first 3 lunar 
</p>
<p>observations and referenced to M1, indicating excellent 
</p>
<p>spatial performance with all BBR being within ±0.1 of the 
</p>
<p>M-band pixel size.    
</p>
<p>As part of sensor radiometric performance assessments, 
</p>
<p>the signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) are routinely computed for 
</p>
<p>the RSB using detector responses to the sunlit SD. The 
</p>
<p>computed SNR at different signal levels are fitted to a 
</p>
<p>function to help derive the SNR at specified typical radiance 
</p>
<p>levels. Similarly, the TEB noise equivalent temperature 
</p>
<p>difference (NEdT) are measured at different temperatures 
</p>
<p>during BB WUCD and at its nominal temperature (292.5 K) 
</p>
<p>and results are scaled to their specified typical temperatures. 
</p>
<p>Table 1 is a summary of VIIRS SNR design specifications 
</p>
<p>for its VIS/NIR spectral bands (high gain only), and the N-
</p>
<p>21 pre-launch and on-orbit performance expressed using 
</p>
<p>ratios of the measured to the specified SNR. As a result, 
</p>
<p>ratios with values of larger than 1 correspond to better SNR </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>performance. Provided in Table 2 are the VIIRS TEB NEdT 
</p>
<p>at specified typical scene temperatures), and the N-21 pre-
</p>
<p>launch and on-orbit performance via ratios of the measured 
</p>
<p>to specified NEdT. In this case, ratios with values of less 
</p>
<p>than 1 indicate better performance. Not included in Tables 1 
</p>
<p>and 2 are the SWIR bands and the low gains for the dual 
</p>
<p>gain bands. Their SNR (or NEdT) performance also meet 
</p>
<p>design requirements with margins.

<p> 
 
</p>
<p>Overall, the N-21 VIIRS performance is similar to N-20 
</p>
<p>and slightly better than S-NPP in terms of calibration 
</p>
<p>stability. Although it is not presented in this paper, the N-21 
</p>
<p>DNB performance has also been very stable. Furthermore, 
</p>
<p>the DNB stray light effect in the high gain stage is clearly 
</p>
<p>smaller than that in S-NPP and N-20. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>4. FUTURE IMPROVEMENTS 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Current RSB calibration uses the SD BRDF and its screen 
</p>
<p>transmittance function, and the SDSM screen transmittance 
</p>
<p>function (all in relative terms) derived from measurements 
</p>
<p>made during spacecraft yaw maneuvers. As more and more 
</p>
<p>on-orbit calibration data become available, these parameters 
</p>
<p>can be improved further. This improvement will likely help 
</p>
<p>reduce small variations in the SD degradation trending 
</p>
<p>shown in Fig. 2 as well as small and similar variations 
</p>
<p>evident in the spectral response trending in Fig. 4 [10]. 
</p>
<p>As mission continues, there will be additional regularly 
</p>
<p>scheduled lunar observations, enabling an independent and 
</p>
<p>more accurate monitoring of RSB calibration stability. 
</p>
<p>Based on lessons from S-NPP and N-20, additional 
</p>
<p>adjustments to the RSB calibration parameters derived from 
</p>
<p>SD and SDSM will be made, including SD degradation at 
</p>
<p>SWIR wavelengths as the on-board SDSM can only cover 
</p>
<p>the wavelengths from 0.41 to 0.93 m, and adjustments 
</p>
<p>based on lunar trending. Remaining concerns of large 
</p>
<p>changes in SWIR responses and the N-21 VIIRS calibration 
</p>
<p>consistency with S-NPP and N-20 will be continuously 
</p>
<p>examined and addressed accordingly. 
</p>
