<p>Mishap Summary
• DATE: July 16, 2013
</p>
<p>• TIME: 1241 GMT (0741 CDT)
</p>
<p>• LOCATION:  Outside ISS, on orbit during US EVA 23
</p>
<p>• BRIEF DESCRIPTION:
</p>
<p>• Roughly 44 minutes into EVA 23, ESA Astronaut Luca Parmitano reported water inside his 
helmet on the back of his head. The EVA ground team, Luca and his spacewalking partner 
Chris Cassidy were unable to identify the water’s source. As they continued to work, the 
amount of water in Luca’s helmet increased and eventually migrated from the back of his head 
onto his face. EVA 23 was terminated early and the crew safely returned to the Space Station. 
What started as a normal EVA day on ISS became one of the most serious mishaps in the 
history of Spacewalking.</p>
<p></p>

<p>Post EVA 23
• Ground teams were unaware of the serious 
</p>
<p>danger Luca was in during the spacewalk 
until a crew debriefing that took place 
several hours later where he described his 
harrowing experience
</p>
<p>• It was later learned that during Luca’s return 
to the airlock, water in his comm system was 
causing intermittent failures, preventing him 
from communicating with the ground teams 
or his partner
</p>
<p>• Almost 1.4 liters of water was determined to 
have entered Lucas’s helmet by the end of 
the EVA 23
</p>
<p>• Luca’s calm reaction to a very dangerous 
situation possibly saved his life
</p>
<p>• The suit failure that occurred on EVA 23 was 
a shock to the Spacewalking team, but could 
it have been prevented?
</p>

</p>
<p>EVA 22 – one week earlier…
• EVA 22, Luca’s first EVA, began with Chris and Luca one 
</p>
<p>week earlier on July 9th, 2014
</p>
<p>• The EVA was completed with no issues, however, during 
repress Luca noticed water in his helmet 
</p>
<p>• When his helmet was removed, almost ½ liter of water was 
discovered
</p>
<p>• Subsequent conversations led to an assumption that water 
had leaked from Luca’s drink bag during his time in the 
airlock, so the drink bag was replaced prior to EVA 23
</p>
<p>• Later investigation determined that the drink bag was not the 
source of the water in Luca’s helmet, but other possible 
failures were not investigated, and EVA 23 proceeded on 
time one week later
</p>
<p>ISS EVA 23 Lessons Learned 6</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>Summary of Findings
• The causes for this mishap evolved from:
</p>
<p>• Inorganic materials causing blockage of the drum holes in the suit water separator resulting 
in water spilling into the vent loop
</p>
<p>• The NASA team’s lack of knowledge regarding this particular failure mode
</p>
<p>• Misdiagnosis of this suit failure when it initially occurred on EVA 22
</p>
<p>7ISS EVA 23 Lessons Learned</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>Root Causes Of Real-time Mishap
• The root causes of the mishap were:
</p>
<p>• Program emphasis was to maximize crew time on orbit for utilization
</p>
<p>• ISS Community perception was that drink bags leak
</p>
<p>• Flight Control Team’s perception of the anomaly report process as being resource 
intensive made them reluctant to invoke it
</p>
<p>• No one applied knowledge of the physics of water behavior in zero-g to water coming from 
the PLSS vent loop
</p>
<p>• The occurrence of minor amounts of water in the helmet was normalized
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>• Snorkel and Helmet Absorption Pad

<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration

<p>Summary of Investigation Findings
• Numerous suit components were replaced and tests were performed to 
</p>
<p>determine anomaly was in the suit’s fan pump water separator (FPS)
</p>
<p>• Silica-based contamination in the water separator blocked holes resulting 
in water back-flowing into the ventilation loop
</p>
<p>• Silica quantity and precipitation in the water separator never seen 
before
</p>
<p>• Water separator is basically a centrifuge which facilitates 
precipitation of contaminants
</p>
<p>Drum Hole Contam
</p>
<p>FPS Removed; FOD in Bore
</p>
<p>Water Separator Drum
</p>
<p>250.00 um
Loose FOD
</p>

<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>Ion Bed Contamination
• Premature saturation of Ion Beds and subsequent use with Luca’s suit resulted in 
</p>
<p>release of high concentration of silica into water loop
</p>
<p>• Ion bed saturation resulted from insufficient quality of water used during ground processing
</p>
<p>• Multiple ion beds used on-orbit were later found to have been contaminated
</p>
<p>» Anomalous water condition likely spanned 8+ years
</p>
<p>• Lot of ion beds built in December 2013 corroded due to water contamination
</p>

<p>ISS EVA 23 Lessons Learned 13</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>It is human nature…
…to want patterns and structure, but this tendency can lead to the belief that 
something that’s been OK before will continue to remain so
</p>
<p>14</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>We’ll never know everything…
…about our hardware, systems or operations and the longer we operate the 
hardware, the more likely we are to believe that we do</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Don’t assume
Don’t assume something is safe because we’ve done it before</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Stay hungry and vigilant –
ask questions
Having diversity and new members on a team can help with infusion of new ideas &amp; questions</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Gaps in data and theories 
present risk
• If you don’t have the full data set, understand where gaps exist and what risks this presents
• Be aware of what you know as fact versus what you’re assuming or inferring </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Anyone in any organization
…(engineering, safety, ops, program, etc) could have had a considerable 
impact in preventing the EVA 23 mishap</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>It can happen to you
Nobody is smart enough to avoid all problems. A preoccupation with failure 
results in high reliability organizations
</p>
<p>20
</p>
<p>Lessons  Learned – Courtesy of Wayne Hale, former NASA Flight Director</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>FOCUS
“Aviation in itself is not inherently dangerous. But […] it is terribly unforgiving of 
any carelessness, incapacity, or neglect.”
</p>
<p>- Capt. A. G. Lamplugh, RAF</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>You are not nearly as smart as 
you think you are
“one mouth, two ears” - Too many people are so busy giving their thoughts 
that they fail to hear warnings of a disaster. Then, don’t just listen, but 
comprehend and take action</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Question conventional wisdom
“People in groups tend to agree on courses of action which, as individuals, 
they know are stupid.” 
</p>
<p>- Dr. David Aiken</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Use your imagination
Apollo 1 was referred to by Frank Borman as a failure of imagination. They 
couldn’t imagine a ground test could be hazardous. Keep vigilant and have an 
active imagination of possible hazards.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>Conclusion
• NASA considered this close call an extremely serious wake up call and took action 
</p>
<p>• As individuals, we must also take action to prevent future, similar mishaps
</p>
<p>• ISS has a long successful history, similar to the suit Program’s history  
</p>
<p>• We can’t let our Programs successes lull us into assuming we have all the answers for the future
</p>
