<p>NEAR-FIELD SEPARATION AND DYNAMICS ANALYSIS OF 
NASA'S SPACE LAUNCH SYSTEM BLOCK-1 AND BLOCK-1B 
</p>
<p>SECONDARY PAYLOADS 
</p>
<p>Jared T. Rucker*, Carole J. Addona†, Peter J. McDonough‡,                        
Benjamin S. Burger§, Michael M. Sanders*, Zachary T. Muscha*,           
</p>
<p>Rekesh M. Ali**, William J. Harlin††, and Drake A. Ranquist* 
</p>
<p>The NASA Space Launch System (SLS) Program Block-1 and Block-1B missions 
</p>
<p>are expected to carry a number of CubeSat secondary payloads (SPL) to space, 
</p>
<p>where they will be ejected from internal SLS structures to begin their own mis-
</p>
<p>sions. This near-field ejection process must be closely evaluated to ensure that no 
</p>
<p>SPL contacts the SLS structures, as contacts could result in SPL component dam-
</p>
<p>age, mission loss, or aberrant SPL trajectories. To this end, multiple SPL ejection 
</p>
<p>events were analyzed for both Block-1 and Block-1B mission configurations us-
</p>
<p>ing CLVTOPS, a NASA Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC) developed multi-
</p>
<p>body dynamics, proximity analysis, and visualization toolchain. This paper details 
</p>
<p>the SPL-to-SLS clearance assessment process, with a focus on simulation setup 
</p>
<p>and results as well as SPL design requirement analysis. Relevant CLVTOPS, sta-
</p>
<p>tistics, and mission backgrounds are covered; important constraints, difficulties, 
</p>
<p>and assumptions are also documented. Furthermore, the importance of SPL hous-
</p>
<p>ing geometry on near-field vehicle clearance is highlighted, and examples are 
</p>
<p>shown. 
</p>

<p>SPLs serve a variety of missions, including technology demonstrations, science exploration, and 
</p>
<p>data gathering. For example, Figure 2 above shows the design and function of NEA Scout, one of

</p>

</p>
<p>the SPL exits the SLS vicinity without recontacts (“near-field”) and remains outside of the desig-
</p>

</p>
<p>cuses on near-field clearance.

</p>

</p>
<p>are discussed. The discussion contains explanations of useful plots and a method used to help set 
</p>
<p>certain requirements.

<p>TOOLCHAIN BACKGROUND 
</p>
<p>To measure SPL near-field clearances, the NASA Marshall Space Flight Center Liftoff and 
</p>
<p>Separation Dynamics (LO&amp;Sep) team utilizes the CLVTOPS toolchain to simulate trajectories, 
</p>
<p>measure proximities, process the results, and generate graphics for use in reports. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 3. Structure of the CLVTOPS Toolchain. 1 
</p>
<p>Figure 3 above shows the composition of the LO&amp;Sep team toolchain. This paper generally 
</p>
<p>refers to the toolchain in its entirety as CLVTOPS, with the exception of specific extension pro-
</p>
<p>grams such as Tree3D* and the MinDist tool. 
</p>
<p>The CLVTOPS Simulation 
</p>
<p>CLVTOPS is a wrapper around the NASA-funded TREETOPS flexible multi-body time-do-
</p>
<p>main dynamics and controls simulation software. TREETOPS computes the translations and rota-
</p>
<p>tions between connected bodies and elements by automatically creating the equations of motion 
</p>
<p>using Kane’s method.1,2 CLVTOPS provides accommodation for custom FORTRAN modules con-
</p>
<p>sisting of system models, forces and moments, plus other computational components, and integrates 
</p>
<p>them into the underlying TREETOPS computational physics engine.1 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>* https://software.nasa.gov/software/MFS-34076-1 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 4 
</p>
<p>Ordered Statistics 
</p>
<p>While singular nominal simulation runs are useful, CLVTOPS utilizes the Monte Carlo method 
</p>
<p>to explore the effects of random variations of input parameters (dispersions) on simulation outputs 
</p>
<p>to verify requirement compliance. Furthermore, input parameters may not be exactly known, so 
</p>
<p>they can be randomly generated within a reasonably expected or estimated range. Ordered statistics 
</p>
<p>are used to systematically quantify output parameters that result from these varied inputs.3 This 
</p>
<p>approach seeks to sample a value’s distribution to create desired bounds for success probability at 
</p>
<p>a given confidence level, such as the 99.865% highest value with a 90% level of confidence.4 To 
</p>
<p>ease the analysis and interpretation, these bounds are referred to with σ-level notation. For example, 
</p>
<p>the 99.865% ejection velocity at a 90% confidence level is referred to as the +3σ bound and can be 
</p>
<p>interpreted as the largest statistically significant ejection velocity output from the simulation. The 
</p>
<p>bounds are used to filter CLVTOPS outputs into statistically meaningful data, usually to indicate 
</p>
<p>the valid upper and lower extents of a given clearance which can help verify no-recontact require-
</p>
<p>ments. Lower levels such as ±1σ and ±2σ bounds can also be useful metrics for classifying outputs 
</p>
<p>at a lower success probability. 
</p>
<p>Tree3D 
</p>
<p>Tree3D* is a NASA-developed trajectory visualization tool created to parse and animate 
</p>
<p>CLVTOPS, or general 6-DOF (degrees of freedom), simulation output.1 The tool utilizes computer-
</p>
<p>aided design (CAD) models and simulation data to position the models in 3D space through time. 
</p>
<p>The tool can also co-animate clearance plots as well as force vectors acting on the bodies. The full 
</p>
<p>animation as well as individual frames can be recorded for use in presentations and reports. Using 
</p>
<p>Tree3D to animate the trajectories of the SPLs and vehicle is helpful in verifying that the simulation 
</p>
<p>is set up correctly and that any reported contacts are legitimate.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 4. SPL Ejection Modeled in the Tree3D Program. 
</p>
<p>Figure 4 contains two sample frames from a Tree3D animation of the Block-1B SPL simulation, 
</p>
<p>one showing a side view of an SPL ejection and the other showing a camera attached to a previously 
</p>
<p>ejected SPL tracking the vehicle housing. 
</p>
<p>MinDist Tool 
</p>
<p>The MinDist tool, short for Minimum Distance, is a software package wrapped into CLVTOPS 
</p>
<p>that measures the distance between bodies during the simulation.1 The MinDist tool implements 
</p>
<p>the externally developed Proximity Query Package† that computes the proximities between pairs 
</p>
<p>of triangular meshes, which are then filtered into the minimum distances between custom-set pairs. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>* https://software.nasa.gov/software/MFS-34076-1 
† https://gamma.cs.unc.edu/SSV/ </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 5 
</p>
<p>These minimum distances, or clearances, are one of the most important parameters with respect to 
</p>
<p>separation dynamics, as they help expose requirement violations, set risk tolerances, and inform 
</p>
<p>vehicle design. SPL mission success is predicated on having positive clearance between concerned 
</p>
<p>bodies at all times after initial separation. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>EVENT BACKGROUND AND SIMULATION SETUP 
</p>
<p>To determine near-field clearances, CLVTOPS must first be set up according to the cases being 
</p>
<p>studied. SPL and dispenser characteristics as well as vehicle housing geometries differ across the 
</p>
<p>Block-1 and Block-1B vehicle configurations. Various assumptions are also made to make the 
</p>
<p>setup simpler, such as neglecting gravitational accelerations and solar pressure that could act on 
</p>
<p>the bodies. Numerical details are sparse in this paper due to limitations on their public release, so 
</p>
<p>some components may only be discussed generically or are outright omitted. 
</p>
<p>SPL Ejection Event Background 
</p>
<p>The future Block-1 and Block-1B flights are expected to carry a varying number of SPLs to be 
</p>
<p>ejected at some point during in-space flight. At the time of the analysis, four payloads were mani-
</p>
<p>fested for Block-1, and fifteen were manifested for Block-1B. The SPLs will be stored in canis-
</p>
<p>ters/dispensers and ejected from internal SLS structures. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 5. SPL Ejection in the Artemis I Configuration. 
</p>
<p>For this analysis, SPLs are considered solely to be CubeSats or CubeSat-style payloads, as no 
</p>
<p>specific geometry or SPL design was provided at the time. A CubeSat is a small satellite that fits 
</p>
<p>into a cubic shape of standard incremental dimensions. The basic building block of the incremental 
</p>
<p>dimensioning is the 10cm3 U, a cube that can be conjoined to form varying structures with stand-
</p>
<p>ardized dimensions. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 6. Sample CubeSat Sizes. * 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>* https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/10-things-cubesats-going-farther/ </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 6 
</p>
<p>Figure 6 above shows a variety of standard CubeSat sizes. There are only a few CubeSat sizes 
</p>
<p>considered for the analysis. Previously, Artemis I utilized 6U SPLs; however, Artemis II plans to 
</p>
<p>utilize up to four 12U SPLs, and Block-1B plans to carry up to 27U SPLs. As no 27U mounting 
</p>
<p>hardware models existed at the time of the analysis and 12U SPLs bound smaller CubeSats with 
</p>
<p>regards to near-field clearance, only 12U SPLs are considered. Smaller CubeSats would be ejected 
</p>
<p>faster and would therefore clear the vehicle housing before any recontact concerns manifest, indi-
</p>
<p>cating that the 12U SPL bounding the 6U SPL preserves conservatism. 
</p>
<p>This analysis focuses on the Artemis II mission and an arbitrary Block-1B Artemis mission. A 
</p>
<p>small amount of previous work has been presented on the Artemis I SPL analysis1, and the analysis 
</p>
<p>presented herein is the original simulation updated to fit the different mission profiles. Models used 
</p>
<p>for both missions are described first, then mission-specific models are introduced. 
</p>
<p>Mission-Independent Models 
</p>
<p>Models used across mission profiles are the dispenser and SPL characteristics, such as SPL mass 
</p>
<p>properties and dispenser ejection force profiles. These models are integrated into various 
</p>
<p>CLVTOPS components, such as problem definition mass property files and custom-made 
</p>
<p>FORTRAN modules that interact with and apply the ejection forces. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 7. Simplified 12U Canisterized Satellite Dispenser Dimensions. 
</p>
<p>The dispenser characteristics are taken from the Planetary Systems Corporation* Canisterized 
</p>
<p>Satellite Dispenser (CSD) datasheet for 3U, 6U, and 12U CubeSats.5 The 12U dispenser physical 
</p>
<p>characteristics are shown in Figure 7 above. The datasheet also contains information for determin-
</p>
<p>ing the SPL ejection velocity based on SPL mass and dispenser configuration.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 8. Dispenser Ejection Velocity Curve-fit.6 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>Figure 9. Calculation of the Dispenser Ejection Force.  
</p>
<p>Utilizing simplified physics calculations with appropriate assumptions, one can determine the 
</p>
<p>force applied by the dispenser to the SPL and reactively back into the vehicle, which can both then 
</p>
<p>be implemented into CLVTOPS. Figure 8 shows the vendor-provided dispenser ejection velocity 
</p>
<p>curve-fit, and Figure 9 shows the calculation of the ejection force fed into CLVTOPS. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 10. V&amp;V Between CLVTOPS and Datasheet Specifications. 
</p>
<p>Figure 10 shows a verification and validation (V&amp;V) activity performed with the datasheet and 
</p>
<p>CLVTOPS ejection velocities to ensure CLVTOPS was properly modeling the 12U dispensers. 
</p>
<p>Arbitrary SPL masses were tested up to the maximum considered for the analysis. Data was taken 
</p>
<p>the first timestep after full ejection, where unconstrained 6-DOF motion first begins. The data was 
</p>
<p>then compared to what the curve-fit predicted the velocity should be. As seen in Figure 10, 
</p>
<p>CLVTOPS is in very good agreement with the manufacturer provided curve fit. 
</p>
<p>In addition to ejection forces, ejection moments were also considered. These moments are not 
</p>
<p>intentional dispenser design but rather inescapable effects of an imperfect ejection vector. Any 
</p>
<p>deviation from the intended ejection path will cause non-axial forces between the tip of the dis-
</p>
<p>penser and aft end of the SPL. These non-axial forces create moments on the vehicle and SPL, 
</p>
<p>which are called tipoff moments. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 8 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 11. Two Tree3D Frames Highlighting Tipoff Rotation in an SPL, Mid-Ejection (left) and a 
</p>
<p>Few Seconds Later (right). 
</p>
<p>Due to the mass difference between the vehicle and the SPL, the SPL experiences the full effect 
</p>
<p>of these moments as seen in Figure 11 above. Tipoff moments were included in the simulation 
</p>
<p>using the manufacturer specified limit on dispenser-imparted rotation rate to calculate the torque 
</p>
<p>necessary to induce that rate. Such rotation is imperative to include, as it can reduce clearance by 
</p>
<p>rotating longer sections of the SPL towards the vehicle housing. 
</p>
<p>The analysis utilizes arbitrary SPLs to generalize setup for any Artemis mission. To accomplish 
</p>
<p>this generalization, a cuboid the size of the 12U dispenser with the mass properties provided in the 
</p>
<p>CSD datasheet was used. Figure 11 shows the cuboid being ejected, and the surface of the SPL 
</p>
<p>shows the triangular mesh that is used in the MinDist tool. 
</p>
<p>Mission-Specific Models 
</p>
<p>Models particular to a mission are the vehicle housings and vehicle kinetic states, both of which 
</p>
<p>are highly important for clearance. The kinetic states of the vehicles are controlled information, so 
</p>
<p>only the vehicle housings are detailed here. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 12. Simplified 2D Drawing of the Block-1 Vehicle Housing. 
</p>
<p>At the time of the SPL ejection event, the SLS Block-1 vehicle consists of an empty Interim 
</p>
<p>Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (ICPS) beneath the Orion Stage Adapter (OSA), also called the Mul-
</p>
<p>tipurpose Crew Vehicle (MPCV) Stage Adapter (MSA), which itself sits beneath the Spacecraft 
</p>
<p>Adapter (SA). Dispensers are mounted around the OSA pointed through the opening of the SA. 
</p>
<p>This creates a concave housing, where the structure narrows towards the separation plane opening 
</p>
<p>that the SPL must fly through. The Block-1 configuration is shown in Figure 12 above. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 9 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 13. Simplified 2D Drawing of the Block-1B Vehicle Housing. 
</p>
<p>For the Block-1B SPLs, the vehicle will consist of an Exploration Upper Stage (EUS) beneath 
</p>
<p>a Payload Adapter (PLA) that houses the New Exploration Secondary Transportation (NEST) at 
</p>
<p>the top. Additionally, there is a piece of leftover separation hardware attached to the top of the PLA 
</p>
<p>that the SPL must fly past called the Payload Separation System (PSS). The dispensers are mounted 
</p>
<p>to the NEST ring just below the separation plane. As opposed to the concave Block-1 housing, the 
</p>
<p>Block-1B configuration creates an open housing, with only the hardware surrounding the dispenser 
</p>
<p>posing clearance concerns. The Block-1B configuration can be seen in Figure 13 above. 
</p>
<p>Major Assumptions 
</p>
<p>Certain key assumptions have been made for this analysis in order to make the problem palatable 
</p>
<p>to CLVTOPS and keep the workload manageable. These assumptions involve forces with minute 
</p>
<p>effects on SPL trajectory, complex models that would be difficult to integrate, and concerns high-
</p>
<p>lighted by the manufacturer. 
</p>
<p>Negligible Forces. Due to the relatively close proximity of the SPLs to the vehicles and rela-
</p>
<p>tively short timeframe of near-field analysis, all gravitational accelerations acting on the vehicles 
</p>
<p>and SPLs are assumed constant and are therefore not modeled. Furthermore, all SPLs were assumed 
</p>
<p>to be passive for this time duration, meaning that the SPL does not perform any propulsive or 
</p>
<p>control maneuvers. It is also assumed that no other source of force or torque disturbances, such as 
</p>
<p>solar radiation or vehicle venting, act on the vehicle or SPLs. These assumptions simplify the sim-
</p>
<p>ulation by excluding forces with minute effects on short-term near-field clearance. Fewer forces to 
</p>
<p>program also helps to streamline and hasten the simulation. 
</p>
<p>Complex Models. All bodies in the simulation are assumed to be rigid. SPLs retain constant 
</p>
<p>mass and inertial properties, meaning that the properties are not updated if the SPL deploys ap-
</p>
<p>pendages after ejection. The ejection force vector is assumed to act perfectly parallel to the mount-
</p>
<p>ing bracket, and no dampening or lateral forces, drag in particular, are imparted to the SPL from 
</p>
<p>the interior dispenser walls, with the exception of generalized tipoff moment and ejection velocity 
</p>
<p>dispersions. These assumptions ease the workload by excluding complex, time-consuming, or ex-
</p>
<p>ternally developed models that may or may not be possible to implement into CLVTOPS. 
</p>
<p>Manufacturer Concerns. The dispenser manufacturer provided data involving dispenser door 
</p>
<p>settling time, general ejection velocity variability, and temperature effects on ejection velocity. 
</p>
<p>Firstly, the dispenser door has not been modeled and is assumed to be at its full extension. In all 
</p>
<p>cases, the SPLs did not exit the dispenser railings prior to door settling, making this a safe assump-
</p>
<p>tion. For another concern, manufacturer data shows that spring forces vary with temperature and 
</p>
<p>eject payloads at varying ejection velocities. Although Monte Carlo dispersions should cover this 
</p>
<p>variability, the dispersions are centered around what is assumed to be the manufacturer reported 
</p>
<p>nominals. </p>

<p>ANALYSIS OF SIMULATION OUTPUTS 
</p>
<p>With CLVTOPS set up, it is possible to generate meaningful results. Default TREETOPS output 
</p>
<p>records the motion of the vehicles and forces between bodies, and custom CLVTOPS output mod-
</p>
<p>ules and the other post-processing generate clearance results, animations, and other useful analyses. 
</p>
<p>Each plot is the result of at least 2000 runs for each vehicle as well as for each special study, and 
</p>
<p>post-processing filters these results according to set parameters and ordered statistics. 
</p>
<p>Trajectory Analysis 
</p>
<p>Using CLVTOPS output, one can reconstruct the trajectories of the bodies. This is useful for 
</p>
<p>determining if the bodies are behaving as intended and to detect any obvious contacts. For near-
</p>
<p>field SPL cases, a majority of the possible output parameters are unimportant so long as the SPL 
</p>
<p>clears the vehicle in a reasonable time without recontact.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 14. Trajectories for Baseline Block-1 and Block-1B Cases. 
</p>
<p>Simulation trajectories are shown in Figure 14 above. The figure shows 2000 dispersed center-
</p>
<p>line trajectories for each SPL for both vehicles, with the outside black bars representing vehicle 
</p>
<p>geometry. By plotting all the runs together, the effects of dispersions can be seen visually. A large 
</p>
<p>spread on any one set of lines can indicate sensitivity to some dispersion, although further correla-
</p>
<p>tion analysis is needed to determine the particular sensitivities. Any curvature in the trajectories is 
</p>
<p>due to vehicle attitude rates, as the trajectories are presented in the vehicle-fixed frame with the 
</p>
<p>observer attached to the rotating vehicle while tracking the SPL locations. These cases had fairly 
</p>
<p>low initial attitude rates, which is evidenced by relatively straight SPL trajectories. 
</p>
<p>Clearance Analysis 
</p>
<p>Although studying the trajectories is useful, the most significant analyses performed on simula-
</p>
<p>tion outputs are the clearance analysis and sensitivity analysis. Clearance is determined using the 
</p>
<p>MinDist Tool along with provided CAD geometries. For separation dynamics, clearance is the main 
</p>
<p>measure by which an event is considered successful, as contact poses many mission risks ranging 
</p>
<p>from generating simple disturbances to damaging hardware to failing missions. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 11 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 15. Clearance Results for the Baseline Block-1 and Block-1B Cases. 
</p>
<p>Figure 15 shows vastly different trajectory clearances prior to the full separation, with post-
</p>
<p>separation trajectories showing increasingly positive clearance. The gray bar represents the range 
</p>
<p>of separation times, where the SPL fully crosses the top of separation plane, indicating that the pre-
</p>
<p>separation clearance differences are mostly a result of SPL housing geometry differences. The plot 
</p>
<p>on the left uses the concave Block-1 housing, indicating that the SPL is launched away from the 
</p>
<p>walls but comes in close contact with the upper piece of the housing later. The plot on the right 
</p>
<p>uses the Block-1B open housing with SPLs launched directly at the separation plane, resulting in 
</p>
<p>monotonically increasing clearance albeit with a lower initial clearance. 
</p>
<p>Sensitivity Studies 
</p>
<p>Analysis of the nominal is important for understanding the expected dynamics of the case; how-
</p>
<p>ever, real flights do not always stay within expected parameters. In order to protect for this, addi-
</p>
<p>tional simulations are run to determine the effects of these extreme cases with a focus on sensitive 
</p>
<p>parameters. These extreme conditions are typically drawn from worst case scenario conditions, or 
</p>
<p>worst case combinations of conditions, in an attempt to create a bounding box that defines the 
</p>
<p>absolute worst conditions, within reason, that an event can undergo. This bounding box helps to 
</p>
<p>determine flight risk under off-nominal scenarios and to determine the need for preventative 
</p>
<p>measures as well as failure protocols. 
</p>
<p>Correlation studies on the baseline case help reveal the sensitive parameters. For the SPL cases 
</p>
<p>launching axially, there are only a few sensitive variables of concern with respect to near-field 
</p>
<p>clearance: vehicle attitude rates and SPL ejection forces. Vehicle attitude rates impart undesirable 
</p>
<p>velocities to the SPLs, which can cause them to fly into the vehicle housing. Likewise, if the SPL 
</p>
<p>ejection force is too low, then the SPL may not separate completely or may be too slow to separate 
</p>
<p>before the vehicle rotates itself into the SPL. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 12 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 16. Roll Rate Sensitivity Study for the Block-1 Case. 
</p>
<p>Roll rate was tested first at varyingly large positive and negative rates. As can be seen in Fig-
</p>
<p>ure 16, clearance is indeed reduced by higher roll rates. The decrease in clearance as roll rate be-
</p>
<p>comes larger in magnitude is due to roll imparting a proportionally large lateral velocity onto the 
</p>
<p>SPL, which will fling the SPL into the vehicle housing walls before it is able to exit the housing. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 17. Tree3D Animation Frames Depicting Lateral Motion Imparted to SPL. 
</p>
<p>Figure 17 above shows the flinging of the SPLs. The figure shows the Block-1 configuration at 
</p>
<p>the time of the red SPL ejection, and the system after 2 seconds have elapsed. The red SPL is 
</p>
<p>ejected under vehicle axial rotation with the red dashed line representing the dispenser ejection 
</p>
<p>force vector. Under non-rotational circumstances, the SPL would fly straight along that line, but 
</p>
<p>due to the rotation-imparted velocity it flies laterally into the walls. It is assumed that roll rate is 
</p>
<p>the most concerning of the standard attitude rates (roll, pitch, and yaw) for SLS secondary payloads 
</p>
<p>as roll rate acts along the main axis of the vehicle and is typically much larger than pitch and yaw. 
</p>
<p>Studies were also performed with generally larger pitch and yaw attitude rates than the baseline, 
</p>
<p>but not to the same extent shown for the roll rate clearance study. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 13 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 18. General Attitude Rate Study for the Block-1 Case. 
</p>
<p>In Figure 18 above, the significant curvature of the trajectories in the right plot is due to the 
</p>
<p>large attitude rates imparting undesired motion onto the SPLs when viewed from the vehicle-fixed 
</p>
<p>frame. From the trajectory plots, it is obvious that clearance is reduced as SPLs curve back towards 
</p>
<p>the vehicle housing. An important note is that the shape of the vehicle housing significantly char-
</p>
<p>acterizes the clearance margins, as the concave housing provides ample opportunity for recontact 
</p>
<p>during SPL exodus. To highlight the effect of housing geometry on clearance, a similar study was 
</p>
<p>performed for the more open Block-1B housing. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 19. Attitude Rate Sensitivity Study for the Block-1B Case. 
</p>
<p>As can be seen in Figure 19, the SPLs remain in far less danger of recontacts than in the Block-
</p>
<p>1 housing, despite the significantly larger attitude rates. There simply is no hardware for the SPL 
</p>
<p>to curve back toward with the open-housing style. The attitude rates affect the SPL trajectories in 
</p>
<p>the same manner, but the vehicle housing itself determines whether or not that is a concern. Less 
</p>
<p>opportunity for recontacts allows for more variation of attitude rate. 
</p>
<p>There are several configurations for the SPL ejection canisters provided by the manufacturer. A 
</p>
<p>study was performed with the Artemis I simulation to determine the minimum ejection velocity 
</p>
<p>necessary to successfully eject the SPL from the Block-1 housing and place it on its long-term 
</p>
<p>trajectory. The study involved comparing spring configurations: a baseline 4-spring configuration 
</p>
<p>and a reduced velocity 2-spring configuration. This study was not repeated for the Block-1B hous-
</p>
<p>ing under the assumption that the Block-1 housing is more conservative. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 14 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 20. Ejection Velocity Sensitivity Study for the Block-1 Case. 
</p>
<p>In Figure 20, the plot on the left shows the baseline clearances for all simulated SPLs, and the 
</p>
<p>plot on the right shows the clearances for the reduced ejection velocity. For the same initial condi-
</p>
<p>tions, the SPLs with reduced ejection velocities ended up significantly closer to the vehicle housing, 
</p>
<p>even recontacting in some cases. This is largely due to the vehicle rotating into the SPL. As seen 
</p>
<p>when comparing the first sections of the plots, the clearances are similar, albeit with a less rapid 
</p>
<p>increase in clearance in the reduced velocity configuration. After that initial segment, the baseline 
</p>
<p>escapes the housing quickly, but the reduced velocity configuration lags and narrowly avoids the 
</p>
<p>concave housing. To ensure safe and reliable separation, the 4-spring configuration yields the most 
</p>
<p>ejection velocity for an SPL and therefore facilitates good clearance. 
</p>
<p>Requirement Setting 
</p>
<p>The purpose of analyzing SPL clearances is not solely to determine recontact risk but also to set 
</p>
<p>limits for canister-constrained deployables, such as solar panels or antennas. These limits are de-
</p>
<p>fined as deployment volumes that extend beyond the undeployed SPL with varying dimensions. 
</p>
<p>SPL providers did not have access to detailed information about the geometry of the vehicle and 
</p>
<p>had to rely on these established volumetric limits to determine if their SPLs would successfully exit 
</p>
<p>the vehicle housing without recontacts. Therefore, the analysis included determining the allowable 
</p>
<p>deployment volume for the SPLs. To do this, simple geometries were used in place of the un-
</p>
<p>deployed SPL CAD model. The geometries could then be scaled depending on the results of each 
</p>
<p>simulation to achieve the maximum deployment volume with minimum risk of recontact. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 21. Sample Shapes Used in the Deployment Volume Analysis. 
</p>
<p>As shown in Figure 21, the shapes utilized in the deployment volume analysis involved cubes 
</p>
<p>and cuboids. The max dispenser volume was used to represent the size of an undeployed 12U, and </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 15 
</p>
<p>the other shapes were modeled as extensions beyond that original size. Spheres were considered 
</p>
<p>originally but were not used because cubes are more likely to cover unconventional deployable 
</p>
<p>geometries, especially when considering cube rotation due to tipoff rates. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 22. Clearance Results for a 40” Cubic Deployment Volume for the Block-1 Case. 
</p>
<p>In all cases, the deployment volume decreases clearance. The shape of the volume can be iter-
</p>
<p>ated upon until a desirable shape is found, balancing size and risk. The effects of adding a deploy-
</p>
<p>ment volume on minimum clearance can be seen in Figure 22 above. 
</p>
<p>In addition to reducing clearance in general, clearance concerns may present themselves in new 
</p>
<p>ways when adding the deployment volume. For example, the deployment volume could be in con-
</p>
<p>tact with vehicle housing prior to when the expanded volume is considered valid and active. Fur-
</p>
<p>thermore, ignoring the invalid recontacts, the Block-1 SPLs may now be recontacting earlier due 
</p>
<p>to the deployment volume’s proximity to its neighboring dispensers immediately after ejection. 
</p>
<p>Recontacts in this timeframe are difficult to spot using only CLVTOPS and the MinDist tool.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 23. Deployment Volume Narrowing to Eliminate Recontact with the Neighboring Dispenser. 
</p>
<p>To identify and verify these types of recontacts, Tree3D is easily used to visualize the physical 
</p>
<p>area of concern during the relevant timeframe. Using the information obtained through Tree3D 
</p>
<p>examination, alternate cube configurations were created that involved various shifting and </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 16 
</p>
<p>narrowing of the volume. For example, in Figure 23, the deployment volume was narrowed to avoid 
</p>
<p>recontacting the neighboring dispenser immediately after ejection. Iterating upon volume sizes and 
</p>
<p>transformations using the CLVTOPS toolchain allowed for the development of an effective volume 
</p>
<p>requirement for SPL canister-constrained deployables. 
</p>

<p>CONCLUSION 
</p>
<p>With rapidly increasing forays into space research, travel, and utilization, small project interest 
</p>
<p>in ridesharing with these new bigger projects has skyrocketed. NASA’s Artemis I has carried nu-
</p>
<p>merous small projects to space alongside its main payload, and future Artemis missions plan to 
</p>
<p>carry many more secondary payloads in the same way. As more and more vehicles carry SPLs or 
</p>
<p>other separable payloads, reliable simulations are necessary to ensure mission success where full 
</p>
<p>tests are not possible. For Artemis SPLs, the CLVTOPS multiple flexible body time-domain dy-
</p>
<p>namics simulation tool is used. The Liftoff and Separation Dynamics Team analyzes the CLVTOPS 
</p>
<p>outputs using post-processing tools such as Tree3D and the MinDist Tool to generate clearance 
</p>
<p>results, trajectory animations, and report graphics. Using the simulation along with the post-pro-
</p>
<p>cessing tools allows users to influence design by determining risk or validate requirements such as 
</p>
<p>positive clearance through separation. 
</p>
<p>In the paper, certain aspects of CLVTOPS simulation setup for SPLs riding on Artemis II and 
</p>
<p>future Block-1B Artemis missions were summarized. Particularly, the dispenser ejection forces and 
</p>
<p>moments as well as separate mission specific vehicle housing designs were shown. Furthermore, 
</p>
<p>notable assumptions that likely would not have affected results and the reasoning behind making 
</p>
<p>such assumptions were described. The analysis consisted of processing, animating, and iterating 
</p>
<p>upon CLVTOPS simulations to understand trajectories and clearances as well as to help set require-
</p>
<p>ments. Firstly, the trajectories and the clearances were discussed, showing what CLVTOPS output 
</p>
<p>produces as well as interpreting what the results mean for the SPLs. These initial results were used 
</p>
<p>to identify system sensitivities impacting clearance. The attitude rate sensitivities were iterated 
</p>
<p>upon for both vehicle configurations, identifying that roll rate is the most critical sensitivity for 
</p>
<p>SPLs on the SLS due to the lateral imparted motion and the likelihood of higher magnitude. The 
</p>
<p>other sensitivity of dispenser ejection force was also discussed. Lower dispenser ejection forces 
</p>
<p>were analyzed as these would produce lower ejection velocity. The lower ejection velocities proved 
</p>
<p>to be problematic for the SPLs as the vehicle would have time to rotate itself into the SPL and catch 
</p>
<p>it within the housing. Finally, various elements of requirement setting that this analysis helped with 
</p>
<p>was shown. Particularly, the development of the deployment volume required iterating upon 
</p>
<p>CLVTOPS, the MinDist tool, and Tree3D to determine the maximum volume that dispenser-con-
</p>
<p>strained deployables can utilize whilst minimizing the risk of recontact. Various challenges faced 
</p>
<p>while creating the deployment volume were also discussed. 
</p>
<p>The analysis did help reveal a key finding that can aid future SLS missions as well as other 
</p>
<p>vehicles that may carry secondary payloads. With regards to SPL clearance, the most important 
</p>
<p>aspect is the vehicle housing. As shown in the Block-1 design, SPLs ejected from deep within a 
</p>
<p>concave housing face a variety of potentially unnecessary challenges whereas, as shown in the 
</p>
<p>Block-1B design, SPLs ejected from near or just below the housing surface face comparatively few 
</p>
<p>challenges with regards to near-field clearance. As per the deployment volume section, volume size 
</p>
<p>is determined by recontact risk. For the concave housing, the high risk of recontact prevents a 
</p>
<p>substantial deployment volume until well after initial ejection. For the open housing, the risk of 
</p>
<p>recontact rapidly decreases, allowing for larger deployment volumes to be employed comparatively 
</p>
<p>sooner. Good vehicle housing design, with respect to near-field clearance, allots SPLs more oppor-
</p>
<p>tunity to deploy with less recontact risk. </p>
