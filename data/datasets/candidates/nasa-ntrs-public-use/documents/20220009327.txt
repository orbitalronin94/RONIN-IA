throughout  the  history  of  Earth  masks  the  complexity  of  its  impact  on  life.  Gravity  interacts  with  other

environmental factors to impact the formation of today’s Earth; for example, it is necessary for rain to fall, for water
to drain, and for heat to dissipate (convection), for air and water to separate, etc.

Microbes are expected to be less gravity sensitive than larger species. Nevertheless, various effects of spaceflight

II. Microgravity Effect on Bacterial Growth: Current Understanding

A. The Direct Effect of Microgravity on Bacteria: Too Small to Notice? 
With their simple structures and small sizes, microbes are expected to be less sensitive to gravity. In fact, physics

predicts that altered gravity will not cause any changes in cells because gravity is extremely weak compared with
other physical forces acting on or within cells.13  Single cells indeed can withstand 105 G, while plants, rats, and
humans can only briefly tolerate 30-40 G, 15 G, and 4-5 G, respectively.  

Early  theoretical  analysis  by  Pollard  examined  the  direct  gravity  effect  on  cellular  structure  and  function,
including  the  redistribution  of  cell  components,  such  as   DNA  and  ribosomes,  the  effect  of  density-driven
convection  on  cell  synthesis  inside  the  cells,  and  the  effect  of  stress  on  membranes  caused  by  gravity.   It  was
concluded that single cells smaller than 10 µm should show little effect of microgravity.14  

B. The Effect of Spaceflight on Bacterial Growth Observed  
Nevertheless, experiments have been conducted to study the spaceflight effect on bacterial growth, and various

effects have been reported.  In a recent review on the effects of spaceflight and simulated microgravity on bacterial
growth,9 Huang listed a large number of studies that have investigated the growth responses of bacteria, fungi, and
archaea  to  microgravity  in  space  and  to  microgravity  analogs  on  the  ground.   In  these  studies,  all  possible
microgravity impacts on growth (increased, unchanged, decreased) have been identified.  It seems that the microbial
growth  effects  are  associated  with  (1)  the microbial  species  and  strains,  (2)  the  strain’s  inherent  properties  (cell
motility), (3) the culture methods, (4) nutrient and oxygen availability, and (5) the rotation speed of the simulator.
In  conclusion,  the  author  suggested  that  to  predict  the  growth  behavior  of  bacteria  in  space,  the  effect  of
microgravity should be intensively studied, and the specific cause-and-effect mechanisms of microbial responses to
microgravity  should be  disclosed  at  the  molecular  level.   For precision,  future  studies  should carefully  consider
bacterial motility, culture methods (suspension or agar), medium nutrient concentrations and rotating speeds.  

To sum up, more and better studies are needed, we are not near to a complete understanding of the microgravity
effect on bacterial growth as yet.  But one must ask the question: what have we learned from the past studies?    

C. The Effect of Spaceflight on Bacterial Growth: The Systematic Approach  
In 1997, in an effort to systematically evaluate the effect of spaceflight on each phase of microbial growth (lag,

exponential or stationary phase), a series of experiments were carried out using in vitro suspension cultures of  E.
coli aboard  seven  US  Shuttle  missions.   The  results indicated  that,  as  a  result  of  spaceflight,  the  lag  phase  was
shortened, the log phase was extended, while the final cell population density was increased. 15 In the same study,
David  Klaus  also  highlighted  that  bacterial  growth  is  a  function  of  intracellular  and  environmental  factors.  The
availability  of  nutrients  and  the  removal  of  metabolic  by-products  are  the  most  important  factors,  which  are
facilitated by forces acting on the cell and on the bulk fluid. Gravity results in cell settlement and induces density-
driven convection within the bulk fluid.   It was suggested that the effect of gravity may impact suspended cells via
their fluid environment, where the immediate, direct influence of gravity might otherwise be deemed negligible.  

To  understand  the  discrepancies  in  the  literature,  Benoit  and  Klaus  analyzed  flight  test  results,  in  terms  of
bacterial  motility and culture media (liquid suspension or solid agar), and it became  clearer  that changes in non-
motile bacterial growth in liquid suspension during spaceflight, shorter lag and higher final cell numbers, are due to

the absence of both gravity dependent cell settlement and density-driven convection; while motile bacterial cultures
and solid agar cultures offset mass transfer variation caused by gravity differences.16 

To further demonstrate this proposed mechanism, a ground-based clinostat study was conducted to evaluate the
effects  of  reduced  cell  sedimentation,  using  neutrally  buoyant  E.  coli cultures  (genetically  modified  to  be  gas-
vesicle-producing).  Since the cell settlement in these cultures was already minimal, it was hypothesized that the
simulated microgravity effects on final population density would also be minimal. As a control, E. coli cultures that
do  not  produce  gas  vesicles  were  also  tested.  As  hypothesized,  no  significant  difference  was  observed  in  cell
populations between the clinostat and static cultures for neutrally buoyant cultures, but there is a 10.5% difference in
population density for the control.  These results further suggest that the lack of cell sedimentation and density-
driven convection is a dominant effect of spaceflight on non-motile bacterial suspension culture.17  

This  extracellular  mass  transfer  mechanism  was  also  confirmed  through  molecular  (transcriptomic)  genetic
analysis,  in  a  recent  ISS  flight  investigation.   It  was  hypothesized  that  in  E.  coli,  grown  in  space,  the  genes
associated  with  starvation  (due  to  reduced  nutrient  transfer  to  the  cell)  and  acidic  response  (due  to  reduced
dispersion  of  acid  product  from  the  cell)  would  be  overexpressed  comparing  with  the  Earth  controls.   These
hypotheses were validated.18 

While the proposed mechanism of microgravity’s effect on bacteria (lack of cell settlement and density-driven
convection) seems to explain the discrepancies in terms of bacterial motility and culture media for experiments with
various strains of E. coli, there are other difficulties. 

“However, it does not account for the findings of Kacena et al., who used a minimal medium to grow B. subtilis…… we
would  expect  that  bacterial  motility  was  maintained  throughout  the  experiment  based  on  the  growth  conditions,  and
therefore, we would not expect the increased growth that was observed due to space flight.”16    

So, it seems once more, we are at an impasse to a clear understanding of the microgravity effect on bacterial
growth.  Is it possible we have missed something? Can we possibly find the missing link to bridge the gap of our
understanding? In the next section, we will take a deep dive into the details of the past studies. 

III. The Effect of Spaceflight: What We Know 
First, let us briefly review what we know about spaceflight and microgravity. Based on the classic Newton’s

theory  of  gravitation,  all  things  with  mass  are  attracted  to  one  another.  Application  of  a  net  force  results  in
acceleration.  

In vacuum, all subjects would fall to earth at a speed that is accelerating until they hit the ground or a solid
surface.  In a fluid, such as water or air, the object has to displace the fluid, which results in another force, buoyancy.
As a result of the net force, heavier (denser) objects sink, while lighter ones float.19  These relative movement, or
density-driven convection, leads to mixing of the fluid, which speeds up the mass transfer processes. 

In orbit, the gravity pull is still at 90% of the ground level, and it provides the centripetal acceleration to keep it
in orbit.  Objects of different densities travel at the same velocity, they are motionless relative to each other, thus
there is no density-driven convection. Mass transfer occurs through diffusion alone, without forced mixing. 

Figure 1 shows some examples that are often associated with microgravity.  Everything floats together in space,
there is no relative motion, or density-driven convection caused by gravity.  As a result, mass transfer is slower.  For
a burning candle in space (in the presence of air), oxygen comes from all directions, through diffusion, so the candle
burns slower, and the flames are more spherical. 

The microgravity effect on the immediate environment of a non-motile bacterium can be depictured in Figure 2. 
Under normal gravity, in a liquid media:  
 Cells (heavier than the media) settle down, while the media around it move up.
 Cells consume nutrients and excrete  waste  and product in the media; typically, the media near  the cells

becomes lighter, and moves up, which causes other media around it to move down.
 The density-driven convection leads to the mixing of the media, which speeds up the mass transfer rates of

nutrient to the cells, and waste/product away from the cells.

Under microgravity, in a liquid media: 
 Objects of different densities stay motionless relative to each other. 
 Cell settlement or density-driven convection does not occur.  
 Mass transfer (nutrient to cell, waste/product away from cells) occurs through diffusion.

Figure 4A shows the baseline growth curves grown at 37  oC and 23 oC in 9-ml test-tubes, used to assist the curve
fitting for the flight and ground control data.  While there is a clear difference in the cell counts between the Fight
and  the  Ground  control,  a  closer  examination  also  reveals  the  large  difference  between  the  baseline  control  and
ground control data (at 37 oC).  

It seems that the handling process  of the  inocula  for  spaceflight,  such  as  the low temperature  and  starvation
before  the  initiation  of  the  growth,  has  negatively  impacted  the  cell  viability;  but  the  spaceflight  condition  can
reduce that impact.   Both low temperature and starvation would reduce the bacterial metabolism (as intended), and
possibly result in cells in various degrees of dormancy, such as “viable but nonculturable” bacteria (VBNC).  During
the initiation of the experiments, bacteria experience “nutrient shock” which is not favorable for the recovery of the
culturability. On the other hand, a lower nutrient level, and a microaerophilic incubation atmosphere (low oxygen,
high CO2) can be the optimal condition for the recovery of the culturability.35,36   Under this context, it appears that
microgravity can result in slow introduction of glucose and oxygen and faster CO 2 accumulation in the immediate
environment of the bacteria, these conditions favor the recovery of culturability, which would result in shorter lag,
and higher final cell number. 

If this assumption is valid, it can also account for the better growth of the motile B. subtilis during spaceflight.
22,23,28,29  Under similar testing conditions, microgravity can affect the culturability recovery of the motile bacteria as

While it is reasonable to believe that bacteria do not spend significant energy fighting gravity, the apparent lack

significant differences can be identified:

 Inoculation through rapid expulsion of the inocula into culture media (unlike the laminate mixing in

 The  media  container  shape  (a  thin  bag  compared  with  the  test  tube  shape  of  FPA),  minimizing  the

 Last, but not least, surface tension can become important for mass transfer in a thin bag geometry. 
Therefore, due to the flight hardware and experiment design, less gravity impact was expected in this study.

F. Spaceflight Effect on Bacterial Growth: B. subtilis22,23,28,29
Currently, all the  B. subtilis growth spaceflight experiments were carried out in VBE medium using FPA, as a

comparison to E. coli ATCC 4157, reported by Kacena in 1997 and 1999.22,23,28,29  Similar with E. coli ATCC 4157,
the final cell count of the ground control of B. subtilis is always lower than the baseline control, indicating a loss of
culturability during prolonged dormant period before the initiation of growth.  In a FPA experiment, the slower mass
transfer rate during spaceflight would benefit the culturability recovery of the motile bacteria as well. 

It should also be pointed out that the final cell numbers of B. subtilis grown in the spaceflight is higher than that
of the baseline control, so there are likely other factors beyond the culturability recovery. 

David Klaus38 suggested that under microgravity, the bacterial growth has a shorter lag, longer (possibly slower)
log,  and  higher  final  cell  density,  due  to  the  lower  mass  transfer  rate.   The  slower  mass  transfer  rate  under
microgravity can result in slower growth rate, and a higher yield can be associated with slower growth rate.43,44 

In  the  1999  study,22  Kacena  acknowledged  the  difficulty  of  growth  rate  determinations  in  spaceflight
experiments, attempted to curve fit the Flight and the Ground Control data using baseline control parameters (shown
in  Figure 4), and suggested that spaceflight resulted faster growth rate.  However, the baseline data is likely not a
good guide for the curve fit due to the possible lag variations.  It should also be noted that VBE contains two carbon
sources  for  B.  subtilis growth  (citric  acid  and  glucose),  it  is  possible  that  slower  mass  transfer  affords  easier
transition between the two growth phases, which results in the higher final cell counts during spaceflight.

H. Spaceflight Effect on Bacterial Growth: Other Bacteria

1. S. typhimurium: Mattoni 196830
In the 1968 study, Mattoni reported the result of the BIOSATELLITE experiment, which was designed to test

the effect of weightlessness and radiation on bacterial growth and lysogeny induction. Some experimental details
can be found in Table 4. 

E. coli C-600 (λ)/λ and  Salmonella typhimurium BS-5 (P-22) /P-22 were chosen  to grow cultures of lysogenic
bacteria with and without γ radiation. The flight hardware, lysogenic bacteria packages, includes  1.2 to 1.8 ml vial
shaped growth chambers.  The cultures were grown anaerobically; the individual growth chambers were completely
filled to avoid the effects of gas bubbles.  Bacteria were grown on LB broth, modified to include buffer and nitrate,
the latter serving as an electron acceptor for better anaerobic growth. 

According to Mattoni,30 “since  S. typhimurium  completed growth to the stationary phase, spaceflight provided
conditions for greater maximum density.  E. coli, on the other hand, was still in exponential growth at recovery.
Since the irradiated populations were significantly denser in flight than controls and temperatures of the irradiated
sets were virtually equivalent, it must follow that the growth rate of the flight populations was also higher.” 

This  was  an  earlier  study,  the  experimental  design  was  not  elaborate:  the  inoculation  was  performed  on  the
ground, and the termination of the test was done by icing the culture on arrival.  Nevertheless, the observations are
essentially  consistent  with  other  studies,  such  as  those  done  in  FPAs.   However,  the  higher  populations  do  not
always indicate higher growth rate. 

In the case of  S. typhimurium,  the ground control reached the expected  stationary  populations (about 3 x 108
cells/ml), while the flight growth surpassed that. As the culture was grown in LB broth, a rich media, possibly the
culture entered the stationary phase as a result of the cell settlement, rather than the depletion of the nutrients (as in a
minimal  media).  Thus,  the  lack  of  cell  settlement  in  space  would  result  in  higher  final  cell  density,  as  Mattoni
rationlized.30  

In the case of E. coli, the ground control did not reach the expected stationary population, so the space effect is
likely not for a  higher growth  rate,  but a  higher  recovery  of culturability in the inoculum, as explained  in other
studies.
