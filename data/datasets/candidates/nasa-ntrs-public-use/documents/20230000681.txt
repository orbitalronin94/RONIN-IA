<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Introduction</p>
<ul>	<li>4 Apollo-era pressure vessel failures were discussed in Lesson 4</li>
	<li>RCS oxidizer tank</li>
	<li>SPS fuel tank</li>
	<li>S-IVB helium tank</li>
	<li>Apollo 13 oxygen tank</li>
	<li>2 additional pressure vessel failures &amp; 2 tile problems also provide valuable lessons about materials durability</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Experimental “ArdeformedTM” Stainless Steel Tanks</p>
<ul>	<li>Built by ARDE Corporation</li>
	<li>New materials concept</li>
	<li>New manufacturing method</li>
	<li>Made from cryoformed 301-type stainless steel</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>1st ARDE Stainless Steel Tank Failure</p>
<ul>	<li>Failure during volumetric expansion test</li>
	<li>Proof pressure was 1,337 psi</li>
	<li>Tank exploded unexpectedly at 1,160 psi</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Underlying Problems</p>
<ul>	<li>New tank material</li>
	<li>First stainless steel tank was filled with water </li>
	<li>Tank immersed in protective aluminum vat, also filled with water</li>
	<li>Bottom of tank touched inside of vat</li>
	<li>Contact created galvanic cell</li>
	<li>Started localized corrosion process</li>
	<li>Released hydrogen</li>
	<li>Cryoformed 301 CRES sensitive to hydrogen</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>1st ARDE Tank Solution</p>
<ul>	<li>Barrier placed inside vat</li>
	<li>Tank placed on nonconductive pad</li>
	<li>No contact between vat &amp; tank</li>
	<li>No galvanic cell</li>
	<li>Damage mode eliminated</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>2nd ARDE Tank Failure</p>
<ul>	<li>Tank exploded during a pressure hold at 1,337 psi</li>
	<li>Tank immersed in water with galvanic barrier during test</li>
	<li>Strain gages applied to tank prior to hold test &amp; then waterproofed</li>
	<li>Waterproof coating attacked cryoformed steel when strain gages applied, causing localized cracking</li>
	<li>Stress corrosion proceeded at tips of induced cracking, growing until critical flaw size (onset of unstable crack growth fracture) was reached</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>ARDE Tank Lessons Learned</p>
<ul>	<li>New materials may act in an unanticipated manner during testing </li>
	<li>Hydrostatic tests can be very dangerous</li>
	<li>Energy released testing with liquid is &lt; energy released testing with gas</li>
	<li>Ullage at top of tank must be avoided</li>
	<li>Be aware of possible hydrogen embrittlement or stress corrosion potentials</li>
	<li>Keep open mind when doing failure analyses</li>
	<li>Do not jump to conclusions; new &amp; unexpected damage modes can be expected—especially with new materials</li>
	<li>Look for all related data that can be found</li>
	<li>Do not presume you know what happened &amp; try to design failure investigation to prove that assumption</li>
	<li>Verify events &amp; data before making them factor in investigation</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Impact on Future</p>
<ul>	<li>Pressure tests must be conducted remotely</li>
	<li>Be aware of what environment might do to material</li>
	<li>Be aware that cryogenically formed tanks may react differently than regular stainless steel tanks</li>
</ul>
<p>Cryoformed stainless steel tank</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Space Shuttle Thermal Protection System (TPS) Failures</p>
<ul>	<li>TPS protects Space Shuttle from: </li>
	<li>Heat during reentry </li>
	<li>Hazards of space </li>
<p></p>
	<li>TPS tiles are blocks of fibrous silica</li>
	<li>Soft porous structure</li>
	<li>Not strong structure</li>
</ul>
<p>Tile system structure</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Orbiter Tiles Fall Off During 1st 747 Aircraft Ferry Flight</p>
<ul>	<li>May 1979—Several tiles fell off Orbiter when it was ferried by 747 aircraft to Kennedy Space Center</li>
	<li>Tiles vital to protection of Orbiter during reentry</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Underlying Problem</p>
<ul>	<li>Tiles were glued to Orbiter skin with room-temperature vulcanizing (RTV) silicon adhesive &amp; Nomex strain isolation pad (SIP)</li>
	<li>SIP “needling” caused localized stress concentrations along bondline interface with tiles</li>
	<li>Concentrations resulted in low bond strength</li>
	<li>Original tests of bond strength conducted on small (2"×2") flatwise tensile specimens, not full-sized tiles  </li>
	<li>Dynamic testing had not been started</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>TPS Tile Loss Initial Solution</p>
<ul>	<li>Massive proof testing effort  </li>
	<li>Conduct acoustic emission test on each tile</li>
	<li>Replace any tiles that come off</li>
	<li>Test took 3 hrs/tile</li>
	<li>Space Shuttle has ~6,000 tiles</li>
	<li>Testing not reliable enough or fast enough—stopped before complete</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>TPS Tile Loss Final Solution</p>
<ul>	<li>Densification of tile’s bottom surface:</li>
	<li>Eliminated voids</li>
	<li>Exposed denser, stronger surface to RTV at bond</li>
	<li>Allowed stress concentrations to be “neutralized” &amp; not a performance factor</li>
	<li>2 densification methods developed:</li>
	<li>LUDOX process, which added silica to tile surface</li>
	<li>Tetraethylorthosilicate (TEOS) process, which also added silica to tile surface </li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>TPS Tile Loss Lessons Learned</p>
<ul><p></p>
</ul>
<ul>	<li>Important to demonstrate durability of new materials, new designs &amp; their combinations before proceeding with assembly</li>
	<li>Once assembled, problems can cause delays &amp; disruptions</li>
	<li>Testing should be completed on representative hardware, not just small samples </li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Tile Coating Repairs Fail During Vibration Testing</p>
<ul>	<li>TPS tiles coated with thin film of borosilicate glass</li>
	<li>Coating prevents tiles from absorbing water </li>
	<li>Coating easily damaged</li>
	<li>Initial repair method only certified for thermal environments</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Underlying Problem</p>
<ul>	<li>Installation of tiles preceded completion of dynamic TPS testing</li>
	<li>Repair method not certified for dynamic performance</li>
</ul>
<ul>	<li>During vibration testing, many tile surface repairs fell out</li>
	<li>Launch vibration might cause loss of tile repairs needed for reentry protection </li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Environment Aggravated Situation</p>
<ul>	<li>Tile material very soft  &amp; lightweight</li>
	<li>Repair material very hard &amp; heavy</li>
	<li>During dynamic movement, hard repair material damaged surrounding soft tile material</li>
	<li>Repair popped out</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>TPS Vibration Solution</p>
<ul>	<li>Use TEOS &amp; tile material to make repair “nugget”</li>
	<li>Densify repair area to reduce density gradient between tile material &amp; repair material</li>
	<li>Method became standard for tile repair</li>
	<li>More than 100,000 repairs done</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>TPS Lessons Learned</p>
<ul><p></p>
</ul>
<ul>	<li>Very risky to start vehicle assembly for flight before all testing is completed</li>
	<li>Quick fixes not always possible</li>
	<li>Do as much work up front as possible</li>
	<li>Dual approaches with resolutions of specific issues of concern can be valuable for:</li>
	<li>Maintaining schedules</li>
	<li>Making needed design changes</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Summary</p>
<ul>	<li>Materials durability &amp; damage modes are strongly influenced by new materials &amp; new applications</li>
	<li>Low safety factors (low margins) make materials more sensitive to environments &amp; damage modes</li>
	<li>Bring out unknowns</li>
	<li>Invite unpredictable problems </li>
	<li>New materials are often rushed into use &amp; pushed to their assumed limits</li>
	<li>New designs are often rushed into manufacturing of flight hardware before testing is completed </li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Summary (cont.)</p>
<ul>	<li>Unexpected problems/failures are especially true of new materials, high-strength materials &amp; new material combinations &amp; applications </li>
	<li>Embrace your technical problems</li>
	<li>They indicate something is wrong </li>
	<li>Source of many valuable lessons </li>
	<li>Focus of new designs is often about                avoiding past failures</li>
</ul>
</div>
