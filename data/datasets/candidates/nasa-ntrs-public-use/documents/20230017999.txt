<p>The present paper investigates the flow physics of Tesla valves and studies how various 
</p>
<p>geometric parameters affect their performance. The performance of the valve is measured by 
</p>
<p>its diodicity, evaluated by comparing pressure drops and discharge coefficients in the forward 
</p>
<p>and reverse flow directions. First, only the flow return angle of a baseline geometry was varied 
</p>
<p>to understand the impact of return angle on valve performance. These baseline geometries are 
</p>
<p>first analyzed with CFD, and then fabricated and installed in a steady-state experimental test 
</p>
<p>rig equipped with pressure transducers, to measure forward and reverse discharge 
</p>
<p>coefficients. Seven additional geometries are also tested in cold flow to identify the impact of 
</p>
<p>diode count, diode volume, and orifice size on valve performance. Next, a multi-objective 
</p>
<p>optimizer is allowed to vary 12 geometric parameters to produce an optimum geometry.  
</p>
<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>The diodic profile considered in this work is based on Nikola Tesla's Valvular Conduit, originally patented in 1920 
</p>
<p>[1]. This passive flow-control device uses a fixed internal geometry to promote low resistance to flow in one direction, 
</p>
<p>but high resistance to flow in the opposite direction. The simplicity of this design has led it to be considered across 
</p>
<p>many disciplines, including biomedical, agricultural, and aerospace. Previous optimizations of the Tesla valve can be 
</p>
<p>found in literature: Bohm et al. generated elevated-diodicity valves at the microfluidic scale by performing a largely 
</p>
<p>unconstrained topological multistage optimization [2]. At the macrofluidic scale, another topological optimization was 
</p>
<p>performed in Gaymann et al. [3], including considerations for manufacturability of the geometry with additive 
</p>
<p>techniques. Numerical analysis performed in Nobakht et al. show that the geometry of the junction between the reverse 
</p>
<p>flow channel and the main flow channel is critical for valve performance [4]. Thompson et al. presents a numerical 
</p>

<p>technique to model flow inside a Tesla valve [5]. Gamboa et. al performed a 2-D optimization of a micro-scale diodic 
</p>
<p>profile at low Reynolds numbers and identified the angle of injection of the secondary flow during reverse operation 
</p>

</p>
<p>Nguyen et al. theorize that pulsatile flows have the potential to vastly improve valve performance in macrofluidic 
</p>
<p>applications [7]. This feature is of particular interest in applications such as the one considered by Yang et. al, where 
</p>
<p>Tesla-inspired valves were applied to inject a premixed kerosene-air mixture into a rotating detonation combustor [8] 
</p>
<p>as well described by [9]. This study investigates the fluid dynamics of Tesla valves, utilizing a multi-objective 
</p>
<p>optimization approach to enhance valve performance by modifying its geometry. Additionally, an experimental 
</p>
<p>steady-state panel was built at Purdue University to calculate the diodicity of various injectors.

<p>III. Numerical Methodology 
</p>
<p>Through a multi-objective 2-D optimization approach, we aim to maximize the diodicity of the fluidic diode while 
</p>
<p>simultaneously minimizing pressure loss in the forward flow direction. This optimization is controlled by the CADO 
</p>
<p>multi-objective optimization tool [11]. The following procedure is conducted for each geometry: 
</p>
<p>A) Geometry is parametrically created from control variables in MATLAB® 
</p>
<p>B) 2D mesh generated with Numeca IGG™ and HEXpress™ 
</p>
<p>C) Evaluation with Metacomp CFD++ [12] 
</p>
<p>D) Post processed in TecPlot® 
</p>
<p>In the following section, each of the above subroutines is further elaborated. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>C. 2-D CFD Evaluation and 1-D Validation 
</p>
<p>To evaluate the diodicity, two CFD runs are carried out for each geometry: one in the forward flow direction (left 
</p>
<p>to right in Fig. 3) and one in the reverse flow direction (right to left in Fig. 3). 
</p>
<p>For the forward flow simulation, the boundary conditions are depicted in Fig. 3: A total pressure/temperature is 
</p>
<p>imposed on the inlet, a simple back-pressure is imposed at the exit, and all other boundaries are treated as viscous 
</p>
<p>adiabatic walls. For the reverse flow case, the total pressure/temperature and backpressure boundary conditions are 
</p>
<p>simply swapped. The 2D Reynolds averaged Navier Stokes equations are solved, and for turbulence closure the k-
</p>
<p>omega-SST model is employed. Incompressible water is used as the fluid. 
</p>

<p>IV. Experimental Methodology 
</p>
<p>A. Steady-State Test Rig 
</p>
<p>To experimentally evaluate fluidic diode performance, several 3-D printed plastic injectors are fabricated, each 
</p>
<p>containing different diodic internal geometries. These injectors are evaluated at steady-flow conditions with the help 
</p>
<p>of a steady-state test rig. 
</p>
<p> 
Fig. 7    CAD Model of steady-flow rig &amp; water tanks 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>A schematic representation of the steady-state test rig along with the actual experimental setup is shown in Fig. 7. 
</p>
<p>A manual regulator is used to set the ullage pressure of the run tank, which is filled with water. A set of four solenoid 
</p>
<p>valves are used to control the flow direction through the injector which allow forward and reverse flow to be tested 
</p>
<p>without having to remove the injector from the system. The dump tank collects water through the test to be pumped 
</p>
<p>back into the run tank for subsequent tests. 
</p>
<p> The panel has a maximum nitrogen and water flow rate of 2265 SLPM and 9.1 SLPM respectively. The injectors 
</p>
<p>can experience a max pressure ratio of 5.25 and can support a maximum orifice diameter of ~3 mm. The panel has a 
</p>
<p>Coriolis flow meter for mass flow measurements, with a range up to 2 g/s precision and a precision &lt;1%. 
</p>
<p> The entire testing and operation of the steady-flow rig is automated using a LabVIEW VI interface. The LabVIEW 
</p>
<p>VI provides live estimates of the pressure drop, flow rate and the 𝐶𝑑 for the injector. Based on the pressure drop set 
</p>
<p>by the operator, the 𝐶𝑑 value for the forward and reverse direction can be readily obtained through an automated test 
</p>
<p>sequence. The valves are appropriately cycled to ensure a settling time and data acquisition time per experiment to 
</p>
<p>obtain the steady flow discharge coefficient. This system is readily used for various pressure drops and flow rates of 
</p>
<p>interest for the given injector. The uncertainty for the pressure transducers is +/- 1.0% and the Coriolis flow meter is 
</p>
<p>+/- 0.1%. Using error propagation, the uncertainty for each of the calculated 𝐶𝑑  values is +/- 0.71%.  
</p>

<p>B. Numerical Baseline Geometry 
</p>
<p>To produce a baseline for further numerical optimization, the parameterization of Fig. 2 is adapted to generate 
</p>
<p>geometries like those found in literature for Tesla valves. This adapted parameterization, shown in Fig. 13, constrains 
</p>
<p>the diode wall into a semicircular shape and creates a concentric centerbody, which together make a return channel 
</p>
<p>for the reverse flow to travel through. The geometry of the diode and centerbody is controlled by the channel width, 
</p>
<p>expressed as a percentage of the center diameter, as well as the return angle, which describes the angle that the reverse 
</p>
<p>flow moving through the return channel is injected into the main flow. 
</p>
<p>In the experiments performed by Gamboa et. al., there is a high correlation between the flow return angle and the 
</p>
<p>diodicity. To explore this, two geometries were assessed, one with a high flow return angle and one with a low flow 
</p>
<p>return angle. This baseline geometry is an attempt to scale the geometry presented in Gamboa et. al. to match the inlet 
</p>
<p>and outlet diameters that match those tested by the experimental team in cold-flow testing. The boundary conditions 
</p>
<p>for this run as well as all subsequent optimization steps remain unchanged: Water is used as the fluid with a 3 Bar 
</p>
<p>total pressure upstream and 1 bar back pressure downstream. </p>
<p></p>

</p>
<p>performance, showing a 17% improvement in diodicity over Geometry #1.

<p>D. CFD Optimized Geometry

</p>
<p>implemented. To allow more freedom in the geometries considered, the parameterization was modified based on the

</p>
<p>angle and the flow return angle are allowed to vary. Furthermore, 5 control points are placed on both the diode and 
</p>
<p>the center body, allowing the geometry of the return passage to vary. These parameters and an example geometry are 
</p>
<p>shown in Fig. 13 and Table 3. </p>

<p>VI. Conclusions  
</p>
<p>In this work, we have devised an approach and developed the tools to improve the geometry of a diodic profile for 
</p>
<p>steady incompressible fluids, inspired by the geometry of a Tesla valve. Several parameterization strategies have been 
</p>
<p>described to provide flexibility in profile geometry with a minimum number of parameters, and an optimization was 
</p>
<p>carried out numerically to understand the parameters that promote elevated-diodicity designs. We have found that the 
</p>
<p>angle at which the reverse flow in the diodic passage impinges upon the main flow path (flow-return angle in our 
</p>
<p>discussion above) should be maximized, and the geometry should be shaped to allow most of the reverse flow to 
</p>
<p>transit through the diodic passage. The numerical tools were validated and additional parameters such as diode count 
</p>
<p>were investigated by experimental testing of 3-D printed diodic profiles in a steady-state cold-flow test-rig. 
</p>
<p>Specifically, two geometries from the computational analysis were manufactured and tested, and the observed 
</p>
<p>improvements from the CFD were confirmed by the experiments.  
</p>
