<p>PIV Measurements of Shock-Wave/Boundary-Layer Interactions
at Mach 2.5 in a Circular Test Section
</p>
<p>Heath H. Reising∗ and David O. Davis∗
</p>
<p>NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, 44135
</p>
<p>The particle image velocimetry (PIV) measurement technique has been applied to make
detailed measurements in an impinging shock-wave/boundary-layer interaction (SWBLI)
flowfield in a test section of circular cross-section. By virtue of this test section geometry,
the flowfields studied are free from effects of the complex coupling of sidewall and corner
interaction into the primary impinging shock interaction typical of a rectangular test section.
The cone-cylinder shock generator hardware was arranged in both coaxial alignment with the
test section to generate axisymmetric SWBLIs and in offset positions to generate swept SWBLIs.
The angle of the conical section was also varied to alter the strength of the impinging shock wave.
The application of the PIV technique in this test section required meticulous design to provide
adequate optical access without disturbing the SWBLI flow. Details of the stereoscopic PIV
apparatus and the velocity measurements obtained for a Mach 2.5 freestream are presented.
Key features of the mean velocity and turbulent stress fields are highlighted. This data set
should enable validation of computational fluid dynamics results for these flowfields.
</p>

<p>I. Introduction
</p>
<p>Validation of turbulence models is paramount in the pursuit of accurate computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
predictions in the complex flowfields present in realistic aerospace vehicle and propulsion designs. Typically,
</p>
<p>methods which rely on modeling of the more complex fluid physics (e.g. Reynolds stresses and heat flux vector in
Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) simulations) are less computationally expensive and can be used in parametric
optimization studies. However, the models used within these lower-fidelity methods are almost universally calibrated in
equilibrium turbulence and require careful tuning to accurately predict the production, transport, and dissipation of
turbulence in complex flowfields. Shock-wave/boundary-layer interactions are typically identified as a problematic case
for RANS solvers due to their large departure from an equilibrium turbulence state [1].
</p>
<p>A dearth of high quality experimental data in supersonic and hypersonic shock-wave/boundary-layer interactions for
use in code validation efforts was identified by Settles and Dodson [2]. There, many of the candidate datasets were
rejected due to the lack of well-defined boundary conditions, undocumented 3D effects, inconsistency between repeated
measurements, or a lack of repeated measurements. A specific methodology was laid out in Ref. 3 for planning useful
CFD code validation experiments which acknowledges the interdependency between the requirements and capabilities
of experimental and computational results.
</p>
<p>In addition to the overall lack of high-quality experimental data, there are also issues with sidewall influences in
many nominally 2D SWBLI datasets. References 4 and 5 show just two examples where blockage generated by corner
separations influence the strength of the primary SWBLI. Thus, the current campaign aims to use an axisymmetric wind
tunnel test section and shock generator cone to circumvent this issue.
</p>
<p>The Turbulent Computational Fluid Dynamics Validation Experiment (TCFDVE) has been an ongoing effort funded
by the Transformational Tools and Technologies Project at the NASA Glenn Research Center (GRC) to provide a
high-quality experimental dataset of a SWBLI which is free of sidewall effects. Previous work on the TCFDVE in
the NASA GRC 225 cm2 Wind Tunnel utilized oil flow visualization, wall static pressure distributions, Pitot probe
surveys, and hot-wire probe surveys to provide a detailed characterization of several SWBLIs in an axisymmetric test
section [6, 7] at Mach 2.5. An important component of a high-quality CFD validation dataset is the corroboration of a
multitude of independent redundant measurements. Thus, in addition to the Pitot and hot-wire datasets, particle image
velocimetry (PIV) was selected as a measurement technique for the validation dataset because:
</p>
<p>2</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>1) it provides a direct measurement of flow velocity,
2) it is non-intrusive measurement and, thus, eliminates probe effects,
3) is capable of collecting large 2D fields of dense data in a short period, and
4) can simultaneously measure all 3 velocity components.
</p>
<p>This report summarizes the velocity measurements obtained using the PIV technique in both axisymmetric and swept
SWBLIs at a freestream Mach number of 2.5.
</p>
<p>II. Apparatus and Procedure
A schematic of the flowfield being studied is shown in Fig. 1. Simple cone-cylinder bodies were used to generate the
</p>
<p>initial shock wave which impinges on the naturally-occurring test section boundary layer. This provides a relatively
thick profile in which to perform the detailed measurements required. The cone half-angle, 𝛼, was varied to provide
both unseparated and separated interactions. A variable offset of the body, ℓ, was provided through the mounting strut
which allowed for the generation of both axisymmetric and swept interactions. The strut supporting the cone-cylinder
was also rotatable about the test section z-axis.
</p>
<p>The details of the shock generator geometry and mounting are presented in Section II.B. The axisymmetric case
requires sampling only in one 𝑥-𝑟 plane to fully describe the flowfield (symmetry of the freestream flow was verified in
Ref. 6). Also, by rotating the support strut in \, the entire swept shock flowfield may be rotated by moving the shock
generator body to various azimuthal positions. This allows for complete surveying of the flowfield with measurements
capable only of surveying in one x-r plane. For the TCFDVE campaign, the near-wall measurements were made at the
\ = 180◦ position. Thus, a secondary vertical coordinate, 𝑦 = 𝑦 + 𝑅, is introduced which aligns the presented data with
typical wall-referenced coordinates used when presenting boundary layer data. This not only reduced the maximum
amount of cantilever required in the traverse system used for probe measurements, but also was key in enabling wider
application of PIV in this flowfield.

<p>A. Test Facility
The NASA Glenn 225 cm2 Wind Tunnel is a continuous-flow facility. It utilizes a lab-wide shared pressurized air
</p>
<p>source which provides clean (filtered to 0.5 µm) and dry (typical dew point of 235–255 K) air at a gauge pressure up to
276 kPa (40 psig). Inlet air may be heated up to 339 K (150 ◦F) to provide precise control of the total temperature.
The facility exhausts to a centralized vacuum source which provides 660 Torr (26 inHg) of vacuum. Test sections with
circular, square, and octagonal cross-sections are available with essentially identical cross-sectional area.
</p>
<p>For the test results presented herein, the tunnel was configured with its axisymmetric 17 cm diameter test section
and a Mach 2.5 converging-diverging nozzle. This is depicted in Fig. 2 below. A new test axisymmetric test section
was fabricated for this work which provides the same basic flowpath dimensions as in Refs. 6 and 7, with the addition
of window ports for optical access. In order to gain clearance for camera mounting at the extreme angles required,
a 203.2 mm long cylindrical spacer was fabricated and installed between the nozzle and test section to allow for the
windows to be installed at a more upstream station within the test section. An equal length spacer was added to the
cone-cylinder assembly so that the interactions occur at the same axial position as in previous probe testing.
</p>
<p>Fig. 2 NASA GRC 225 cm2 Wind Tunnel as configured for this test.
</p>
<p>The PIV test section itself has the same overall length and diameter as the baseline configuration [6, 8]. As shown,
a simple cylindrical extension piece was designed to be installed upstream of the PIV version in order to shift an
equivalent 𝑥 station forward within the test section by 8 in (203.2 mm) relative to the baseline. Cutouts for the standard
51.3 mm× 279.4 mm window inserts were placed at \ = 0◦ and \ = 180◦ as in the baseline test section, but both the port
and starboard cutouts were omitted to allow for larger optical windows to be installed. The PIV test section assembly is
shown in Fig. 3.
</p>
<p>The forward edge of the PIV window cutouts are located at 𝑥 = 495.3 mm(𝑥 = 0 is defined in the TCFDVE campaign
to be at the nozzle exit station). By placing the windows aft of the axisymmetric inviscid shock impingement station, the
influence of geometry change at the window on the SWBLI region is minimized. The 241.3 mm× 67.4 mm through cuts
were made at a 20◦ downward angle to accommodate the intended PIV measurement being along the test section wall at
\ = 180◦. Each window assembly consists of an aluminum frame, a 7.6 mm thick piece of fused silica sandwiched
between two rubber gaskets, a steel wedged peripheral shim ring, and the aluminum retaining flange. Each window
frame was designed to precisely match the inner mold line curvature of the test section, while the nearly tangent glass
had inevitable recesses at the top and bottom (maximum depth of 5 mm).
</p>
<p>The use of curved windows in this application was eliminated due to the requirement that all test hardware withstand
the maximum pressure available in the facility (40 psig). This drove the glass material thickness high enough that
a strong lensing effect would be created and image distortion would be insurmountable when combined with the
already difficult viewing angles. A "dual-pane" assembly which uses a thin curved flow surface window with an outer
pressure-containing flat window could be pursued in future efforts, but was deemed too complex for this entry. The
window assemblies were pneumatically pressure tested to avoid moisture-induced crack growth acceleration.
</p>

<p>B. Shock Generator Hardware
The cone-cylinder centerbodies used to generate the impinging shock waves were highly configurable. Three tip
</p>
<p>cone half-angles were available to generate interactions of various strengths: 10◦ for an unseparated interaction, 13.5◦
for incipient separation, and 16◦ for a fully separated interaction. The cylindrical portion of the body was 5 cm in
diameter and was comprised of a collection of spacer pieces to generate the desired axial length. A specific length of
spacer stack was selected for each cone angle to place the inviscid impingement at the same axial station as in Ref.
7. The cone-cylinders were cantilevered from a support strut spanning the aft end of the section. This strut had a
sharp leading edge to reduce blockage and, thus, lower the minimum Reynolds number condition available for testing.
Three mounting positions are available on the strut. A centerline position allows for the generation of an axisymmetric
interaction while two offset positions generate swept interactions as shown in Fig. 1. Figure 4 gives a cross-sectional
view of the test section to illustrate the relevant dimensions and variables for positioning the shock generator.
</p>
<p>To allow the PIV laser sheet to pass through the support strut when the shock generator is located at \ = 0◦ or
\ = 180◦ position, small (38 mm× 3.8 mm) slits were cut with by wire electrical discharge machining (EDM) though the
symmetry plane of the strut. Also, when presenting the PIV measurement results, it is convenient to use an alternative
azimuthal coordinate which describes the shock generator position relative to the measurement plane. This is shown as
\ in Fig. 4.

<p>C. Conventional Instrumentation
The PIV test section was instrumented with a reduced number of static taps from the conventional version.
</p>
<p>Ports were drilled 50.8 mm from the forward and aft ends (𝑥 = 254 mm and 𝑥 = 863.6 mm, respectively) at
\ = 0◦, 90◦, 180◦, and 270◦. However, a highly-instrumented window insert was available for installation in the cutouts
at \ = 0◦ and 180◦. This insert, installed in the \ = 0◦ port (opposite the PIV measurement region) during this PIV
entry, provided static pressure measurements over 𝑥 = 412.75–679.45 mm at 3.18 mm spacing.
</p>
<p>The static pressure measurements were used in this entry as an in situ confirmation of nominal facility operation
since they should provide redundant copies of the data taken in the baseline test section. The reader is referred to Ref. 8
for presentation of the typical static pressure profiles and a thorough analysis.
</p>
<p>D. Particle Image Velocimetry Measurement System
The introduction of the planar PIV technique into this test facility was described in detail in Ref. 9. The method by
</p>
<p>which seed particles would be introduced into the wind tunnel was carefully considered to optimize the PIV measurement
quality while minimizing the detriment to existing probe measurement capabilities. The resulting median particle
relaxation time was shown to be &lt;3 µs over the range of Reynolds numbers of interest to this study [9]. In addition, to
accommodate the selected laser sheet orientation, new hardware was fabricated for installation within the wind tunnel
diffuser to allow for laser ingress and to protect the optics which reside within the flowpath. Additional components
were added to this system to facilitate longer duration of seeder operation at high Reynolds numbers. The following
sections describes the PIV system in detail.
</p>
<p>1. Flow Seeding
Seed particles are generated using a condensation-based mineral oil droplet generator (ViCount 2200). This type
</p>
<p>of seeding device avoids agglomeration of particles in the supply piping, as the oil droplets are generated as the hot
oil vapor exits the supply piping. Mineral oil seed material is also a desirable choice for this facility as residual seed
material can be removed simply by operating at an elevated temperature and allowing adequate time for oil residue to
evaporate from the internal surfaces. In the present study, limiting the seeded region to the lower-center portion of the
tunnel keeps the observation windows clean and increases the particle image density in the region of interest. The rake
assembly is also mounted to a motorized translation stage for precise positioning during tunnel operation.
</p>
<p>2. Laser Sheet
The laser sheet for this study was introduced through a pressurized fairing within a diffuser section downstream of
</p>
<p>the test section. This allows the laser beam to be sent counter to the wind tunnel flow in the 𝑥-𝑦 plane while glancing on
the bottom of the test section to allow for near-wall measurements of the test section boundary layer with minimal flare.
The laser beam was generated by a dual head frequency-doubled Nd:YAG laser (Quantel Evergreen 200). The beam is
expanded vertically by a pair of cylindrical lenses and focused to a thin sheet by a 2 m focal length spherical lens. This
results in a collimated laser sheet which is 32 mm high at the measurement region.
</p>
<p>In order to protect the final laser mirror which is mounted within the tunnel flowpath, a protective fairing was
fabricated and installed in the diffuser of the wind tunnel. This fairing and the internal optics are depicted in Fig. 5. A
small glass window on top allowed the laser sheet to enter the fairing. To prevent the buildup of seed material on the
laser outlet window at the leading edge of this fairing, the glass is instead recessed within the fairing and no pressure seal
is made. Instead, a constant buffer flow of filtered dry air is supplied to the fairing which exits the fairing counter to the
primary wind tunnel flow through the same aperture as the laser sheet. In order to reduce the open area of this aperture
and increase the momentum of the outgoing buffer flow, a 3D-printed nozzle is installed at this aperture. The required
flow capacity of the complete buffer flow system and the aerodynamic design of the 3D-printed nozzle were thoroughly
studied with CFD simulation to ensure adequate protection would be provided for the laser mirror. The mount for the
final mirror was outfitted with a pair of piezoelectric actuators to allow for fine tuning of the sheet alignment with the
facility without removing the fairing. The distance from the final mirror to the center of the measurement region was
1.2 m
</p>
<p>The diffuser flowfield containing the opposing supersonic jets is highly turbulent and was found to generate noticeable
beam steering in the laser sheet. To create a more benign environment, a particle diverter wedge assembly was installed
in the preceding diffuser section which generated a wake region in which the laser sheet can propagate. This reduced the
pressure required within the fairing as well. These wedges had the additional benefit of diverting a good portion of the
</p>
<p>6</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>seed material away from the aperture of the laser mirror fairing. The assembly consists of a pair of 18◦ wedges (only
one visible in Fig. 5 due to sectioning) mounted on a bar which allows for an adjustable gap to be set between them.
This gap was set just large enough to allow the laser sheet to pass (∼3 mm). The entire assembly was mounted to the
facilty’s standard probe traverse which allowed for the precise alignment of the gap and the laser sheet. While multiple
axial stations are available on the mounting bar, the most forward position was used during this test entry.
</p>
<p>Fig. 5 Diagram of laser sheet routing into wind tunnel.

<p>3. Imaging
Images were captured by a pair of interline-transfer CCD cameras (LaVision Imager SX6M) with pixel size of
</p>
<p>4.54 × 4.54 µm and a resolution of 2752 × 2200. The stereoscopic pair were placed to capture back-scattered light at an
approximate elevation angle of 22◦ and sweep angle of 45◦. Off-the-shelf LaVision Scheimpflug mounts were used to
tilt the camera sensor plane into alignment with the image plane of the laser sheet. Due to the compound tilt in viewing
angle, careful rotation of the Scheimpflug tilt axis around the lens axis was required. Additionally, the camera sensor
was rotated such that the axial extent of the field of view common to the two cameras was maximized. A bandpass filter
(&gt;93% transmission at 532 nm and 80 nm FWHM passband) was placed in front of each camera to minimize stray light
from the test cell.
</p>
<p>The cameras were calibrated using a 58 mm × 58 mm double-sided bi-level dot target (LaVision 058-5) which has a
dot spacing of 5 mm and a level separation of 1 mm. The camera field of view relative to the test section was registered
by placing the forward edge of this target coincident with the forward edge of the lower instrumentation window at
𝑥 = 406.4 mm. A rendering of the camera arrangement used for 3-component (3C) velocity measurements is shown in
Fig. 6 where the test section is configured for camera calibration.
</p>
<p>All datasets used the same imaging distance and 100 mm lenses which resulted in an average pixel scaling of 65 pix
mm
</p>
<p>and a dewarped common field of view as shown in Fig. 7. Here the two views are overlaid in world coordinates at the
𝑧 = 0 plane. This trapezoidal field of view yields data relevant to the SWBLI investigation over a rectangular region
measuring approximately 50 mm in 𝑥 and 25 mm in 𝑦 for each position of the translating system, which is shown by the
red box in Fig. 7. As is clear from the figure, because the lower flow surface was removed when calibration images were
captured, the target was able to be placed to extend below 𝑦 = 0, thus removing any influence of edge effects in the
calibration equations in the region of interest.
</p>
<p>While a polynomial mapping function was used for the calibration of the images for the computation of velocities,
pinhole calibrations were also computed as a sanity check which yielded camera angles of (42 ± 1)◦ and (22 ± 1)◦ for
the axial sweep and elevation angles, respectively. The target-based calibrations were performed each at the start of each
test day. These calibrations were refined using the self-calibration procedures available in the LaVision software.
</p>

of rails which were precisely aligned with the test section axis. By remotely actuating the stepper motor driving the

<p>E. Test Parameters
The PIV data were obtained at several discrete camera positions which provided overlapping fields of view which
</p>
<p>spanned the region of interest in the axial direction. Each PIV data set covers a 50 mm × 25 mm region of the flow field.
Translation between successive measurement stations was 38.1 mm, which yielded 24% overlap for stitching of the
vector fields. The overall region sampled by PIV was 𝑥 = 406.4–569.0 mm and 𝑦 = 0–25 mm.
</p>
<p>All PIV data sets were collected with a pulse separation time of 500 ns to resolve the low wall-normal and spanwise
velocities. The PIV cross-correlation processing was performed with DaVis 10. An initial interrogation window size of
128 pix × 128 pix was applied with adaptive window weighting, which stretches the Gaussian-weighted windows in
response to local velocity gradients detected in the prior pass. The windows were progressively reduced in size by half
until the final window size of 32 pix × 32 pix was reached. Window overlap in the final pass was increased to 50% to
increase vector density. For the selected pulse seperation time, the freestream particle displacement is 15% and 58% in
the dewarped image coordinates for the initial and final window sizes, respectively. Spurious vectors were eliminated by
applying a minimum peak ratio threshold and 5 passes of a spatial median filter. Typical processing time for a set of
1000 vector maps was 8 hours on a 24-core CPU.
</p>
<p>A complete survey of the measurable test section region was made first with no model installed to characterize the
baseline test section boundary layer. For these measurements, 1000 unique velocity fields were obtained for each camera
position. For SWBLI datasets, ensembles of 1000 image pairs were again collected within and downstream of the shock
interaction region, while 500 pairs were taken in regions upstream where the velocity should be identical to the baseline
boundary layer measurements. Table 1 provides an overall summary of the breadth of SWBLI cases studied.
</p>
<p>Previously reported results from probe and wall static pressure measurements showed that the offset distance
did not have a large influence on the SWBLI behavior [8]. Therefore, to reduce the test matrix scope for the PIV
measurements, only the ℓ = 𝑅/4 offset was studied. This configuration provides for greater clearance between the
region of interest and shock generator, thus reducing background scattering collected by the cameras. Additionally,
the Reynolds number parameter space was consolidated to explore just the minimum and maximum cases for the two
weaker shock configurations.
</p>

the mean velocity and Reynolds stresses are also shown for comparison of the flowfield behavior under varying SWBLI
strength in both axisymmetric and swept interactions.

<p>Fitting the incoming boundary layer profiles to a composite canonical turbulent boundary layer profile as described
in Ref. 9 is challenging for this dataset. The extreme camera viewing angles required to avoid disturbing the SWBLI
flowfield lead to a relatively large distance to the wall for the nearest PIV measurement. Additionally, the high Reynolds
numbers and small scale of the test facility dictate that the viscous scale for the boundary layer is very small. This leads
to boundary layer velocity profiles which only contain a few points within the logarithmic region. Consequently, the
fitting routine faces an under-determined problem where many combinations of 𝑐 𝑓 , Π, and Δ𝑦0 lead to similar levels of
residual. This can result in PIV velocity profiles which look similar to the Pitot probe data, but have very different
parameters resulting from a global "best-fit". This is illustrated in Fig. 11a where the optimal 𝑐 𝑓 value from the PIV
data is 27% higher than that of the Pitot data while the overall profile and scale parameters are a near match (𝛿99 being
the most affected due to its high sensitivity to the velocity profile slope at the boundary layer edge). While the friction
coefficient from the PIV data is higher than the expected value in this Reynolds number range, the wake parameter is
more in line with what is expected for the slight adverse pressure gradient in the constant-area test section (Π = 0.55 for
zero pressure gradient). The problem of sparse data in the logarithmic region is only exacerbated at higher Reynolds
numbers where the boundary layer scale is reduced. The presence of many solutions near the global optimum can be
seen by constraining the fit of the PIV data to match the 𝑐 𝑓 of the Pitot profile. This yields a best-fit solution which
closely matches all parameters of the Pitot profile with a concurrent increase of 16% in the total residual error. However,
this result will not be obtained organically without a priori knowledge of the skin friction and manual constraints. With
this uncertainty in the skin friction acknowledged, future users of this data as a CFD validation dataset are encouraged
to match the velocity profile (or at least integral parameters) rather than a skin friction coefficient shown herein.

<p>While direct causation of the suppressed peak shear is difficult to isolate, there are a few candidates, primarily seed
particle inertia, finite spatial resolution of the technique, and optical distortion. The suppression of wall-normal stress in

due to the finite interrogation window size and particle inertia were found to act similarly. Both works attributed the
greater sensitivity of the wall-normal fluctuations to the flatter power spectrum containing more energy at scales smaller
than the measurement cutoff. Recently, investigation of the PIV spatial resolution effects were made using Mach 5
turbulent boundary layer DNS data and synthetic images [30]. The inherent dilemma of minimizing PIV interrogation
window size while controlling spurious correlations was illustrated.

<p>The unfortunate takeaway from this analysis is that the competing effects of finite spatial resolution and seed particle
inertia result in no discernible trend for better measurement accuracy at a particular Reynolds number. Attempts to
correct the PIV data for particle inertia effects are infeasible due to the unknown true Reynolds stress spectra for
a compressible turbulent boundary layer at this Reynolds number and the complexity in determining an accurate
correction at each spatial location. Polydispersity (non-uniformity in size) of the seed particles adds an additional layer
of complication (Fig. 14 are computed for a single particle size) as particle response to the same gas acceleration varies
non-linearly with scale. Thus, the turbulent quantities presented in this work are given directly with the acknowledgment
that the ultimate magnitude is suppressed somewhat near the wall by non-continuum effects. All effort was made to
minimize these effects by using a low-density seed material and generating the smallest droplets possible. Similarly, the
spatial resolution in PIV measurements is limited by the interrogation window size used when cross-correlating the
image pairs. The minimum size for these windows is typically limited by the concentration of seed material in the flow.
In the current experiment, the seeding system was operated in such a way that it provided the maximum seed particle
concentration without increasing the size of the droplets. This resulted in a seed concentration which was amenable to
PIV processing with a 32 × 32 final window size. A potential future endeavor to improve upon the spatial resolution of
the current dataset could investigate seeding system modifications or clever image processing techniques. However, a
further reduction in window size would likely necessitate a reduction in the scope of data collection due to the increase
in computational cost to process the data. In this manuscript, the minimum Reynolds number experimentally available
will be prioritized for analysis to make the flowfield computationally tractable for fully-resolved large eddy simulation
(LES), which already strains the current sate of the art [35].

<p>The turbulence behavior through these interactions can be further analyzed through inspection of the anisotropy.
Because all 3 components of velocity are measurable by the PIV technique, the full Reynolds stress tensor can be
computed at each spatial location. The behavior of this tensor is typically characterized by mapping the invariants on a

state. However, this technique is not readily applicable to spatial fields of data; thus, the visualization technique proposed

visually denotes the proximity to each of the 3 limiting states. This method was applied to the stresses obtained by
PIV measurement and is presented in Fig. 26. In the current implementation, the color blue was used to represent the
isotropic state (spherical Reynolds stress ellipsoid, denoted as "3C"), green for the two-component axisymmetric state
(disk-shaped Reynolds stress ellipsoid, denoted as "2C"), and red for the single-component limiting case (Reynolds
stress ellipsoid collapses to a line, labeled "1C" in the figure). In the flow upstream of the interaction, the expected
wall-bounded turbulence behavior is obtained where the isotropic freestream moves toward one-component behavior
along the right-hand boundary of the Lumley triangle as the distance from the wall decreases. Very near the wall, the
turbulence should become two-component in nature as the 𝑣′ is suppressed by proximity to the wall. As previously

<p>D. PIV Measurement Uncertainty
The velocity uncertainty of the PIV cross-correlation process is estimated by DaVis based on statistics of the
</p>
<p>observed correlation peak [43]. This method captures many common errors associated with practical PIV measurement
in complex flowfields (seeding density, velocity gradients, out-of-plane motion). The 95% confidence bound for the
uncertainty in mean velocity magnitude does not exceed 2% of 𝑢1,∞ for all the cases shown, typically peaking near the
reflected shock foot and falling to 0.1% in the incoming freestream. A possible future avenue for further assessment
of the particle displacement error is the collection of PIV images at several pulse separation times to disentangle the
random error from the turbulence within the underlying flowfield [44]. As discussed in Section III.A, applying this type
of trade study across all test conditions is infeasible and a reduced scope of interactions would need to be selected for
detailed analysis.
</p>
<p>Calibration error was minimized in this experiment by using a stereoscopic self-calibration procedure [45] in addition
to the initial target-based approach. This method makes small adjustments in laser plane location by cross-correlating
the two camera views of the same laser pulse and minimizing the disparity. However, the ability of the polynomial
camera calibration equations to completely account for the distortions of the convoluted optical path is strained in this
application. The ultimate error in PIV measurement associated with the camera calibration can be quantified by the
stereoscopic velocity reconstruction error. This is shown for two most upstream camera views in Fig. 37. The error
in the upstream view is relatively benign, ranging from 0.25 pix in the freestream to 0.5 pix at the wall. The region
of highest reconstruction error occurs at the shock waves, reaching 1 pix. As mentioned, motion of the shock wave
dominates the turbulent statistics in this region; thus, the reconstruction error is not the primary concern. Precisely how
this error propagates to the ultimate velocity measurements is an open question, but is estimated to be &lt; 5% of 𝑢1,∞.
</p>
<p>The accuracy and precision of the electronics used to control the timing of the various PIV equipment has a strong
effect on the measurement uncertainty. Due to the relatively short time delay required by the high freestream velocity
and small spatial scale of the current experiment, the timing precision becomes even more crucial. For The PIV system
used herein, the manufacturer specified output jitter for the pulse generator (LaVision PTU X) is 50 ps. In practice,
this uncertainty is dwarfed by jitter in the delay between Q-switch trigger and actual laser output which ultimately
drives the variability in time delay between the two laser pulses. This uncertainty was quantified with a photodiode and
oscilloscope with high sampling rate, resulting in a &lt; 0.5% (2.5 ns) timing error with 95% confidence.
</p>
<p>Some influence of finite seed particle is inherent to any particle-based velocity measurement technique when applied
in a turbulent flow. This results in a low-pass filtering effect in velocity data which is minimized by reducing the seed
particle size or material density. With a quadratic dependence of Mie scattering cross section on particle scale, a balance
</p>
<p>35</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>

</p>
<p>Fig. 37 Average stereo reconstruction error magnitude for camera views of primary interest in axisymmetric

<p>must be struck between providing adequate scattered light and faithful tracking of the full range of possible turbulent
accelerations. In the current work, the seeding system was operated to provide the minimum seed particle size available.
As discussed in Section III.A, the effects of seed particle inertia is noticeable in the boundary layer turbulent fluctuation
magnitudes. This is attributed to a local influence of non-continuum effects which continually depresses the particle
drag closer to the wall. The situation is further complicated by the polydispersity of the particle size distribution, which
can yield varying levels of particle tracking error even within each interrogation window. Thus, a precise quantification
of the uncertainty in the turbulence quantities due to particle inertia is beyond the scope of this work.

<p>IV. Conclusions
A new stereoscopic particle image velocimetry (PIV) measurement capability has been applied to make detailed
</p>
<p>measurements in an impinging shock-wave/boundary-layer interaction (SWBLI) flowfield in a test section of circular
cross-section. By virtue of this test section geometry, the flowfields studied are free from effects of the complex
coupling of sidewall and corner interaction into the primary impinging shock interaction. Data was collected for both
axisymmetric and swept interactions of varying impinging shock strength and over a range of Reynolds numbers. The
focus of the detailed analysis presented herein was for SWBLIs at 𝑅𝑒𝐷 = 2 × 106.
</p>
<p>The application of the PIV technique in this test section required meticulous design to provide adequate optical
access without disturbing the SWBLI flow. The influence of the non-axisymmetric features required for the application
of PIV was quantified and shown to be inconsequential to the results presented. The PIV measurement apparatus is
capable of rapidly acquiring data of all 3 velocity components in 2D planes. By translating the camera assembly, data
was collected across the entire SWBLI region without interrupting facility operation.
</p>
<p>The PIV measurement system in combination with the continuous flow wind tunnel allowed for many snapshots
of the velocity field (uncorrelated in time) to be collected for computation of mean and fluctuating components.
Comparisons of the mean and r.m.s. velocity in the upstream boundary layer were made with previously obtained
probe-based measurements and expected values for a turbulent boundary layer. The challenges in making accurate
turbulence particle-based measurements in compressible boundary layers are discussed. PIV datasets from multiple
camera positions were collated into panoramic fields of mean and turbulent quantities across the upstream boundary
layer, interaction, and recovery region.
</p>
<p>In particular, the strength of the stereoscopic PIV measurement applied in this work lies in the ability to resolve
three-dimensional velocity vectors. This allows for the determination of the out-of-plane velocity component which
is typically very difficult to obtain through probe-based measurement. The PIV data was able to quantify the strong
spanwise flow which occurs along the wall in the \ = 90◦ interaction as well as the associated 𝑢′𝑤′ shear stress. This
case provides a unique flowfield in which the three-dimensionality may challenge turbulence models in a new way. Also,
because the PIV measurement samples all 3 velocity components simultaneously, the complete kinematic Reynolds
stress tensor can be computed. With this data available, the degree and nature of the turbulence anisotropy may be
evaluated. In this report, two-dimensional maps of the anisotropy tensor invariants were generated to elucidate how this
</p>
<p>36</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>behavior varies spatially through the flowfields studied. The axisymmetric case with a weak impinging shock shows
very little deviation from the upstream turbulent boundary layer behavior while the separated case showed a distinct
deviation toward more isotropic behavior in the recovering boundary layer. In the swept interaction, the near-wall
turbulence is distinctly more two-dimensional, implying a lasting influence of the additional 𝑥-𝑧 shear generated by the
strong spanwise flow. The evolution of the turbulence anisotropy provides another benchmark for evaluating the ability
of turbulent calculation methods, from Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes methods to scale-resolving approaches such as
LES, in simulating the detailed behavior of SWBLIs.
</p>
<p>An additional asset of the PIV technique is that it supplies continuous planes of data upon which further spatial
analyses can be applied. In the present paper, the correlation of turbulence quantities with mean velocity gradient were
computed. It was observed that the region of high streamwise turbulence corresponds closely to the region of high 𝜕𝑈
</p>
<p>𝜕𝑥
</p>
<p>in the axisymmetric interaction, which creates a region of strong production of streamwise turbulence. The region of
high shear stress was distributed over a larger region and thus less correlated with gradient in the streamwise velocity. In
the swept interaction, the same behavior was observed for the streamwise turbulence. However, the primary shear in this
interaction occurs in 𝑢′𝑤′ which was found to be the strongest (measured) producer of spanwise turbulence near the
separated region. Further downstream in the region of highest spanwise velocity, the character reverses as 𝑣′𝑤′ 𝜕𝑊
</p>
<p>𝜕𝑦
</p>
<p>becomes the dominant shear-based production term. The relative significance of these terms may be compared with the
full turbulence budget available in a LES or DNS.
</p>
<p>The intent of this work is to provide a publicly available comprehensive dataset in an array of flow configurations
which are typically challenging for RANS models. This report points out key flow features that should be targeted
when evaluating simulation results. Additionally, some first order analyses were applied to the statistical distributions of
turbulent fluctuations to inform where equilibrium models may fail and for comparison with more sophisticated methods,
such as hybrid LES/RANS. A repository for this data is in the process of being established to release for public use.
</p>
