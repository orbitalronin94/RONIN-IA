<p>Actively Controlled Louver for Human Spacecraft Radiator 
</p>
<p>Ultraviolet (UV), Dust, and Freeze Protection 
 
</p>
<p>Darnell T. Cowan1  
</p>
<p>NASA Lyndon B. Johnson Space Center, Houston, TX, 77058 
</p>
<p>This paper examines the use of actively controlled louvers to attenuate UV and dust, as well as mitigate 
</p>
<p>freezing concerns for human spacecraft radiators during Artemis missions. Artemis missions to the lunar 
</p>
<p>orbit or surface will expose the radiators to high energy UV radiation and dust, which will degrade the 
</p>
<p>radiator’s coating emissivity and consequently reduce heat rejection performance. In addition, subfreezing 
</p>
<p>environmental temperatures during transit to lunar orbit and nighttime on lunar south pole can rupture 
</p>
<p>coolant tubes, reduce heat rejection performance, and worst-case scenario result in a Loss of Mission (LOM). 
</p>
<p>Louver technology would be a promising solution to maintaining radiator performance and integrity for 
</p>
<p>Artemis missions, but heritage louvers are passively controlled. This technology needs maturing to active 
</p>
<p>control, or motor actuation, to achieve faster thermal response times. Actively controlled louver design 
</p>
<p>considerations are discussed in this paper. The analysis that follows shows actively controlled louvers can 
</p>
<p>attenuate high energy UV radiation and dust, as well as protect the coolant from freezing. 
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>ational Aeronautical Space Administration’s (NASA) Artemis missions will return humans to the Moon for the 
</p>
<p>first time in over half a century, and the development of new technologies are necessary to enable a sustained 
</p>
<p>presence. Unlike the Apollo missions, astronauts will live and work near the lunar south pole, and gradually build 
</p>
<p>infrastructure to conduct long duration tests and experiments1. NASA also plans to assemble a space station in lunar 
</p>
<p>orbit called Gateway to conduct science, perform technology demonstrations, and be utilized as a pit stop for 
</p>
<p>spacecrafts to deliver cargo to the lunar surface. Conventional human spacecrafts (i.e., ISS and Shuttle) are cooled 
</p>
<p>using mechanically pumped liquid cooling loops to collect, transport, and reject heat using radiators. The radiator’s 
</p>
<p>performance is highly dependent on the surrounding environmental, or sink, temperatures, and available surface area.   
</p>
<p> These radiators can be body mounted, or imbedded, into the spacecraft’s structure, or deployable like wings on an 
</p>
<p>aircraft. State of the Art (SOA) body mounted radiators like those on Orion are historically lighter than deployable 
</p>
<p>radiators, but the limited surface area and exposure to cold environmental temperatures offers less heat rejection 
</p>
<p>capability than deployable radiators. SOA deployable radiators like those on the International Space Station (ISS) can 
</p>
<p>have an indefinite number of panels to increase surface area, articulate to optimize thermal environments, and reject 
</p>
<p>heat on both sides of the panel. This advantage is why the initial concepts for Gateway’s International Habitat (I-
</p>
<p>HAB) and the Lunar Surface Habitat2 (LSH) are derived from the ISS deployable radiators3.   
</p>

<p>II. Problem Statement 
</p>
<p>The ISS deployable radiators were designed to operate in Low Earth Orbit (LEO) and coated with Z-93 inorganic 
</p>
<p>white paint to maximize heat rejection. Z93 has an emissivity and absorptivity of 0.91 and 0.16, respectively, and 
</p>
<p>studies have shown exposure to high energy UV radiation or lunar dust can significantly degrade the absorptivity. In 
</p>
<p>addition, the environmental temperatures during a spacecraft’s transit to lunar orbit and surface are significantly colder 
</p>
<p>and last longer when compared to operating in LEO. These harsher lunar environmental conditions may reduce the 
</p>
<p>radiator’s heat rejection capability and jeopardize the success of the mission. This paper investigates the use of actively 
</p>
<p>controlled louvers to attenuate UV and dust, as well as mitigate freezing concerns for human spacecraft radiators such 
</p>
<p>as those on I-HAB and the LSH during Artemis missions. 
</p>
<p>A. UV Radiation Impacts 
</p>
<p>Artemis spacecrafts and habitats traveling through and beyond the Van Allen Belts will be exposed to ionized, or 
</p>
<p>high energy, UV radiation, which can significantly degrade the radiator’s absorptivity. The Apollo missions mitigated 
</p>
<p>ionized UV concerns by rapidly traveling through the belts and shortening mission durations to less than two weeks 
4. However, Artemis missions can linger in the Belts from minutes to days and can take months to reach the lunar orbit 
</p>
<p>or surface5. Experiments6,7 have shown the Van Allen Belt ionized UV particle densities can vary from 1x1013 to 
</p>
<p>1x1014 particles/cm2 @ 50 and 200 KeV, respectively, and exposure to more than 500 Equivalent Sun Hours (ESH) 
</p>
<p>under these conditions can degrade the Z-93 absorptivity from 0.16 to 0.24, or 50%, as shown in Figure 2. 
</p>

</p>

</p>

<p>C. Radiator Freezing Impacts 
</p>
<p>The I-HAB and LSH will operate with low heat loads without astronauts onboard for approximately eleven months 
</p>
<p>out of the year. The environmental temperatures during transit to lunar orbit and the fourteen-day lunar nights on the 
</p>
<p>Moon’s surface can drop below -213 °C (-351 °F)10,11. These temperatures are lower than the HFE 7200 coolant 
</p>
<p>working limit and freezing point of -100 °C (-148 °F) and -137 °C (-215 °F), respectively, and studies have shown 
</p>
<p>approximately 1 to 4 kW heater power is required to keep the coolant flowing and avoid freezing2. Heater power 
</p>
<p>availability may be limited during these conditions and the consequence of freezing, thawing, and rupturing of coolant 
</p>
<p>tubes could result in a decrease of heat rejection capability or Loss of Mission (LOM).    
</p>

<p>III. Solution Using Louvers 
</p>
<p> A possible solution to reduce Z-93 absorptivity degradation and mitigate coolant freezing concerns is equipping a 
</p>
<p>radiator with louvers. The conventional louver12 is constructed of aluminum blades, which are passively actuated by 
</p>
<p>a bimetallic spring as shown in Figure 5. Numerous testings and flight histories have shown louvers can vary a 
</p>
<p>radiator’s effective emissivity from 0.14 to 0.74. Heat is retained, reducing the emissivity to 0.14 when the louver is 
</p>
<p>closed. Heat is rejected while the louver is open, increasing the emissivity to 0.74. The radiator suffers a slight 
</p>
<p>reduction in capability since the emissivity without louvers is 0.91, which is explored in this paper.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

</p>
<p>The louver blades can attenuate ionized UV from reaching the radiator coating while closed and can limit Z-93 
</p>
<p>absorptivity degradation depending on the blade thickness. Conventional aluminum louver blades are ~ 1.3 cm thick8, 
</p>
<p>and the ionized UV attenuation can be calculated using Beer-Lamberts Law as shown in Equation 5.

<p>B. Dust Protection 
</p>
<p>Louvers can also block dust from settling on LSH radiators while in the closed position, and limit Z-93 absorptivity 
</p>
<p>and heat rejection capability degradation. Currently, the amount of lunar dust adhering to a bare radiator without a 
</p>
<p>louver for the LSH life cycle is undetermined, but assumptions can be made to assess the feasibility of using louvers 
</p>
<p>for protection. The current LSH concept positions the radiators on top of the habitat to limit dust impacts. Assuming 
</p>
<p>the bulk of the dust originates during landing, then the dust should only impact one side of the stowed deployable 
</p>
<p>radiator assembly. Using Equations 2 and 4 and the same assumptions for Figure 4, then the analysis showed a radiator 
</p>
<p>panel fully covered with dust on one side will degrade the total heat rejection capability of a four-panel radiator 
</p>
<p>assembly by approximately 20% as shown in Figure 7. Adding louvers to the radiator will protect the panel from dust 
</p>
<p>degradation and maintain heat rejection capability. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>D. Radiator Heat Rejection Capability with Open Louvers 
</p>
<p> The louver will shunt some of the heat rejection capacity because the blades will block some of the radiated heat, 
</p>
<p>and this reduction in capability can be calculated by adding a view factor to Equation 2 as shown is Equation 8. 
</p>
<p>Assuming the blades are perpendicular to radiator surface while in the open position, then the view factor can be 
</p>
<p>calculated as a rectangular-to-rectangular plate as shown in equations 9. The view factor will also depend on the 
</p>
<p>number of blades; this paper investigated using 14-blades per panel. The heat rejection capability can be calculated as 
</p>
<p>a function of sink temperature assuming the blade area and radiator surface area are equal.  
</p>
<p> The results shown in Figure 9 indicate a 13 to 7% reduction in heat rejection capability with the louvers. The slight

<p>IV. Actively Controlled Louver Design Considerations 
</p>
<p> Louvers have been used on satellites like New Horizons Pluto and Rosetta12 for decades, but not on heritage human 
</p>
<p>spacecrafts. Therefore, they have a low Technical Readiness Level14 (TRL), and advancement in the technology is 
</p>
<p>necessary to make it practical for human spacecraft radiators. For instance, the louver blades will need to be actuated 
</p>
<p>using active control, like a motor, rather than a conventional passive bimetallic spring, to improve thermal response 
</p>
<p>times. Passively controlled louver blades are actuated based on the environmental temperatures and can take hours 
</p>
<p>transitioning from open to closed.  
</p>
<p> The I-HAB and LSH heat loads3 can vary between 2 and 15 kW and it may be necessary to reject heat while 
</p>
<p>astronauts are onboard during the nighttime. Passively controlled louvers may prevent heat rejection during nighttime, 
</p>
<p>and temperatures can exceed limits if the transition time is not decreased. Therefore, active control is necessary for 
</p>
<p>operators to control when to open and close the louver and reduce the transition times to minutes.  
</p>

<p> The total surface area and weight of a 14-blade conventional louver is less than 0.14 m2 (217 in2)  and 1 kg (2.2

</p>

</p>

</p>

<p>V. Conclusions and Recommendations 
</p>
<p> Equipping human spacecraft radiators with louvers is a feasible solution to limit coating degradation due to ionized 
</p>
<p>UV and lunar dust, as well as mitigate coolant freezing concerns during Artemis Missions. Analysis in this paper 
</p>
<p>showed louver blades with standard ~1.3 cm (0.5 in) thickness can negate ionized UV that can otherwise reduce 
</p>
<p>rejection capability by 60%. In addition, the radiator’s heat rejection capability may be reduced up to 120% depending 
</p>
<p>on the amount of dust coverage and can be mitigated by closing the louver during events that will spur up dust. Louvers 
</p>
<p>may naturally reduce the heat rejection capability due to radiative view factors, but the slight reduction in capability 
</p>
<p>(7-13%) outweighs the reduction due to dust and ionized UV.  
</p>
<p>  The analysis also showed that adding louvers to a radiator can keep the coolant above the working and freezing 
</p>
<p>limits during transit to lunar orbit and nighttime on the Moon’s surface, therefore eliminating heater power needs and 
</p>
<p>reducing the Loss of Mission (LOM) risk. Technology development is necessary to advance the louver from the 
</p>
<p>conventional passive controlled to active control to reduce the thermal response time from approximately hours to 
</p>
<p>minutes.
