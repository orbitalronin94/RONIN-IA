<p>Velocity and Temperature Measurements in High-speed Flows with Naturally 
</p>
<p>Present Dust Particles Using Rayleigh and Mie Scattering 
 
</p>
<p>J. Panda 
</p>
<p>NASA Ames Research Center, Moffett Field, CA 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>E.D. Crowe 
</p>
<p>Metis Technology Solutions, Inc., Moffett Field, CA 
 
</p>
<p>Abstract 
</p>
<p>Dust particles and occasional moisture condensations are unavoidable reality of all wind tunnels. On the 
</p>
<p>path to pursue a goal of velocity and temperature measurements in high-speed wind tunnels we have 
</p>
<p>created a tabletop, spectrally resolved, Rayleigh-Mie scattering setup around a small jet fed by ambient 
</p>
<p>and air with different particle concentration to determine the accuracy of the technique. The other reality 
</p>
<p>of a wind tunnel setup is the spurious reflection and scattering of the incident laser beam by solid surfaces 
</p>
<p>(flare light), which contaminates the Rayleigh-Mie scattered light. This is simulated by backgrounds with 
</p>
<p>different reflectivity towards the collection optics. Light from a CW laser is delivered via an optical fiber 
</p>
<p>and the scattered light from a point on the laser path is spectrally resolved using a stabilized Fabry-Perot 
</p>
<p>interferometer, followed by imaging on an EMCCD camera. A model of the combined background glare, 
</p>
<p>Mie scattering, and the Rayleigh spectrum was fitted to the camera image using maximum likelihood 
</p>
<p>estimation. It was observed that the present modeling approach can extract velocity and temperature with 
</p>
<p>reasonable accuracy when the intensity of the Mie scattered light is less than or comparable to that of the 
</p>
<p>Rayleigh scattered light; beyond that error in velocity measurement remains reasonable ±12m/s but the  
</p>
<p>temperature measurement becomes progressively more inaccurate. Increasingly the flare light is found to 
</p>
<p>cause a bias error in velocity. Similar trend is observed in the presence of both the flare light and the Mie 
</p>
<p>scattered light. The setup has provided a set of data for the future improvement of the modeling procedure, 
</p>
<p>and demonstrated that even in the case of large amount of dust particles and large flare light the present 
</p>
<p>technique can provide measurement of velocity with reasonable accuracy. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>I. INTRODUCTION 
</p>
<p>Transonic and supersonic wind tunnels lack viable tools to measure basic quantities such as velocity, temperature, 
</p>
<p>and density, particularly when local shock waves are present. The lack of experimental data makes computational 
</p>
<p>fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations as the sole provider of many aerodynamic information. However, CFD cannot 
</p>
<p>fill the gap alone as the computational codes require experimental data for validation. Out of the many emerging 
</p>
<p>optical techniques that are attempting to provide these information, molecular Rayleigh scattering is a promising 
</p>
<p>candidate due to the relatively large signal levels, the appeal of a complete measurement of kinematic (velocity u) 
</p>
<p>and thermodynamic properties (temperature T, and density ϱ) , and the affordability of the opto-electronics. 
</p>
<p>Commercially available, particle-based PIV have found applications in large wind tunnels [1] , still contamination 
</p>
<p>by the seed particles is an issue for test facilities. Additionally, particle-based techniques are unreliable in shock 
</p>
<p>containing flows. Various molecular tagging velocimetry (MTV) techniques such as FLEET [2], use specialized 
</p>
<p>and expensive pulsed lasers to ionize some gas molecules and follow emissions from these molecules to measure 
</p>
<p>flow velocity. Coherent Anti-Stokes Raman Spectroscopy (CARS) based technique have been used to measure 
</p>
<p>temperature and standard deviation of temperature fluctuations [3]. The Rayleigh-scattering based technique is 
</p>
<p>non-intrusive and does not require injection of seed particles. Among the Rayleigh based techniques there exist 
</p>
<p>variations, which can be broadly divided into two categories based on the means of resolving the Rayleigh 
</p>
<p>spectrum [4]. For the spectrally resolved technique an interferometer, such as a Fabry-Perot [5-10] is used to 
</p>
<p>completely resolve the Rayleigh spectrum. For the filtered Rayleigh scattering technique a molecular aborption </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>2 
 
</p>
<p>filter, such as the hyperfine absorption lines of iodine vapors [11, 12] is used to convert the spectrum into light 
</p>
<p>intensity. The spectrally resolved path requires an extensive modeling of the measured spectra to extract the 
</p>
<p>velocity and temperature information. There exist multiple challenges for wind tunnel implementation of the 
</p>
<p>Rayleigh technique: contamination by the naturally present dust particles, scattering of incidence beam by 
</p>
<p>windows and beam dump (flare light), vibration of the test facilities, and limitations imposed on the velocity 
</p>
<p>component that can be measured via the available optical access ports in the facility. The motivation of the 
</p>
<p>present study is to find means to overcome the first two difficulties. The flare light and the Mie scattered light 
</p>
<p>adds to the burden of correct modeling. The goals of this work are to collect a set of data with different levels of 
</p>
<p>Mie and flare light, evaluate the accuracy of the present model to extract the underlying Rayleigh-Brillouin 
</p>
<p>spectrum and to help with the further development of the model.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Note that despite the above challenges the spectrally resolved Rayleigh has a proven record of measuring 
</p>
<p>spectrum of u/, T/, ϱ/, shear stresses such as ϱ/u/u/, and correlations such as &lt; ϱ/- u/&gt; , &lt; u/- T/&gt;, and more 
</p>
<p>importantly two-point statistics in relatively clean flows [13, 14,15]. The present work only attempts to evaluate 
</p>
<p>uncertainties with the time-averaged measurement of velocity and temperature, which by themselves can become 
</p>
<p>valuable tools in high-speed wind tunnel applications.  
</p>

<p>A simplistic description of the Rayleigh spectrum in the presence of the background flare and particle (Mie) 
</p>
<p>scattering, are schematically shown in Fig. 1. Fig 1(a) helps to recapitulate the basics of the Rayleigh spectrum: 
</p>
<p>The electric field of the incident laser light polarizes individual gas molecules, which in turn scatter a very small 
</p>
<p>part of that light. The moving gas molecules create a Doppler shift in the scattered light. Airflow, such as that in a 
</p>
<p>wind tunnel, can be thought of as a combination of a bulk motion at the measured Mach number plus a 
</p>
<p>superimposed random velocity of the individual molecules. Therefore, the Doppler-shifted scattered-light is 
</p>
<p>spectrally broadened with a distinct peak. The separation between the peak of the incident laser line and the peak 
</p>
<p>of the Rayleigh spectrum provides a measure of the bulk velocity. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) 
</p>
<p>depends on the spread of molecular motion and therefore is a measure of gas temperature, and the area under the 
</p>
<p>Rayleigh spectrum, i.e., the intensity of the scattered light is proportional to the gas density. In the presence of 
</p>
<p>background glare from the laser beam, the Rayleigh scattered light is mixed with light at the laser frequency (Fig. 
</p>
<p>1b) and can be easily identified once the laser frequency is known. Most of the naturally born particles and the 
</p>
<p>condensates are small are expected to follow the bulk motion of the flow. In fact, Mie scattering from the dust 
</p>
<p>particles clearly marks the central peak of a Rayleigh spectrum. Fig 1(c) shows the spectrum in presence of both 
</p>
<p>background glare and particles in the air flow.  
</p>

</p>
<p>temperature inside a jet, and a parametric study using progressively increasing background glare and particle 
</p>

<p>II. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 
</p>
<p>IIa. Optical setup 
</p>
<p>Because the Doppler shift is of the order of 109 Hz (2.66MHz per m/s of flow velocity for 90˚ scattering using 
</p>
<p>532nm light), which is far smaller than the optical frequency (5.6x1014 Hz for 532nm light in air), there is a need 
</p>
<p>to use a narrow linewidth laser source and a hyperfine spectroscope, such as a Fabry-Perot interferometer (FPI). 
</p>
<p>Fig 4 shows the optical arrangement around a small free jet. There are two aspects of this setup that needs to be 
</p>
<p>mentioned at the outset. In a wind tunnel application, an important question is how to deliver the laser. Passing 
</p>
<p>the beam through any window, even when coated to reduce reflection, produces excessive background flare that 
</p>
<p>may swamp the Rayleigh-Mie signal. Unlike many other optical techniques, we use a CW laser, which allows for 
</p>
<p>transmission through optical fibers. Delivery of laser light through an optical fiber will reduce the glare. So, we 
</p>
<p>decided to test such a delivery system for the present setup. The downside of using fibers is transmission loss 
</p>
<p>(~40%), and a scrambling of the polarization axis, which effectively reduces the Rayleigh scattered light towards 
</p>
<p>the collection direction by half. The second aspect is the choice between direct imaging of the probe volume 
</p>
<p>versus the use of an optical fiber for passing the collected scattered light. Our previous work [6] used direct 
</p>
<p>imaging of the probe volume through FPI on a camera. In the presence of dust particles, it becomes difficult to 
</p>
<p>separate the Rayleigh and Mie scattered signal in the direct imaging technique. Therefore, the other option of 
</p>
<p>imaging the probe volume on an optical fiber was used. The optical fiber scrambled the scattered light from 
</p>
<p>different parts of the probe volume, and presented a uniformly illuminated circular face for imaging through FPI. 
</p>
<p>Such images provided radial segregation between the Rayleigh scattered, Mie scattered, and the background flare 
</p>
<p>light (Fig 2). The downside of using an optical fiber for the transmission of the scattered light is a further loss of 
</p>
<p>photons. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> The optical layout can be divided into five groups: (a) The laser source and the delivery of light to the flow, (b) 
</p>
<p>optics to collect Rayleigh-Mie scattered light, (c) the spectroscopic arrangement, (d) optics to deliver a small part 
</p>
<p>of the incident laser light also called as the reference light, and (e) stabilization arrangement. The 532 nm, single-
</p>
<p>mode, incident beam of the first group was produced from a frequency-doubled, Nd:YVO4 solid state, Coherent 
</p>
<p>Verdi laser that emitted a maximum of 10.5W power. The vendor provided a specification of 5MHz for the 
</p>
<p>linewidth. The light was coupled to a 50micron core diameter fiber. At the output end the light was collimated by 
</p>
<p>a 10.9mm focal length aspheric lens and passed through the air stream from a small jet. To reduce the flare light 
</p>
<p>off the aspheric lens a hollow conical baffle was used to house the fiber end and the lens. This baffle was removed 
</p>
<p>to increase the flare light content in the Rayleigh-Mie scattered light. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>In the second group scattered light from 1.35mm long probe volume was collected by a pair of 75mm diameter 
</p>
<p>achromats with f/# 4 and 2.67, and focused on a 900micron core diameter fiber, which in turn, delivered light to 
</p>
<p>the spectroscopic arrangement - the third group. The spectroscopic arrangement first collimated the light delivered 
</p>
<p>by the fiber by a 50mm f/#2 achromatic lens. The collimated light was passed through air-spaced, planar, 30mm 
</p>
<p>diameter, 90 % reflectivity FPI mirrors with a free-spectral range of 8GHz. The optical cavity of the stabilized 
</p>
<p>Fabry-Perot, supplied by Lightmachinery, was actively controlled by three piezo-scanned support points and three 
</p>
<p>strain gauges that provided position sensing. Once aligned, the closed-loop control maintained the parallelism of 
</p>
<p>the two mirrors through extraneous environmental factors such as thermal drift, vibration, and noise. This was 
</p>
<p>particularly beneficial for long-duration data collection and eliminated the need for any manual adjustment. The 
</p>
<p>actively controlled interferometer simplified the day-to-day alignment so much that the laser can be turned on any 
</p>
<p>day, and nicely formed fringes with little degradation of the finesse can be readily visible. Finally, the 
</p>
<p>interferometer output was imaged through a 300mm focal length fringe-forming lens on a low-light, cooled, 
</p>
<p>EMCCD camera. Baffles and curtains were placed around the spectroscopic setup to further reduce contamination 
</p>
<p>from stray light. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>For the alignment of the spectroscopic setup and for determining the instrument function, a small part of the 
</p>
<p>incident laser light needed to be passed through the interferometer – this made the fourth group. To achieve this 
</p>
<p>goal, a small amount of light scattered by the fiber coupler was collected and passed to an Integrating sphere via </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>7 
 
</p>
<p>another optical fiber, marked as fiber carrying reference light in fig 4. The IS was mounted on a linear actuator, 
</p>
<p>which allowed placement of an opening of the IS in front of the collection fiber. When the IS was placed in front 
</p>
<p>of the collection fiber, the Rayleigh scattered light was blocked and the reference light from the incident beam 
</p>
<p>passed through the interferometric setup.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The fifth and the final group is the stabilization arrangement to correct for the slow drift of the laser frequency. 
</p>
<p>Despite the extensive use of active temperature control of the laser head some amount of slow drift caused by the 
</p>
<p>changes in the ambient temperature is inevitable. Such drifts create error in velocity measurement and makes 
</p>
<p>identification of the background glare difficult. One of the mirrors in the laser cavity was mounted on PZT stacks.

<p>The interferometric measurement was a two-step process. The first step involved characterizing the instrument 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

<p>IIIa. Effect of increasing Mie scattering from particles: 
</p>
<p>To vary the particle content in the jet stream air from two different sources were supplied to the jet. The shop air 
</p>
<p>used in our experiment was found to be cleaner than the ambient due to multiple filters both at the inlet and the 
</p>
<p>outlet of the air compressor, and an oil separator and a chiller used at the outlet. Air from this source is called as 
</p>
<p>“Cleaner air.” A second compressor without any filter was the second source of air. Fine droplets of lubrication 
</p>
<p>oil added to the naturally born particles and significantly increased the particle content in this “Dirtier air.” To 
</p>
<p>further increase the particle content to the level of condensation mists in the wind tunnel couple of incense sticks 
</p>
<p>were burnt at the inlet of the compressor without any filter. The smoke from the incense sticks farther increased 
</p>
<p>the particle counts in this dirtiest case, also referred as “Dirtier air + Incense” air, which created a visible glare on 
</p>
<p>the laser path passing through the jet.  Figure 7 shows the particle counts from all the above operations in a 
</p>
<p>tabular form and as a histogram. The jet velocity was changed for 0 to 290m/s and was measured by a small Pitot 
</p>
<p>tube placed at the location of the probe volume. The total pressure measured from the Pitot tube and the total 
</p>
<p>temperature from a Kiel probe was used in the isentropic relationship to determine the jet properties. The very 
</p>
<p>small jet diameter made the perfect juxtaposition of the Pitot tube at the laser probe volume difficult. This may be 
</p>
<p>the cause of the slight bias error in velocity and temperature seen in the data presented. The Pitot tube was placed 
</p>
<p>below the laser probe volume. The jet was mounted on a vertical traverse which moved the jet between the 
</p>
<p>location of the laser probe volume and that of the Pitot tube. 
</p>
<p>The model for the Rayleigh image (equation 19) provided a measure of the intensity of the Rayleigh light (IR), 
</p>
<p>particle scattered light (IPart) and that of the flare light ILas. These values were extracted for each data point (see the 
</p>
<p>heading of Fig. 6) and were used to quantify the respective scattering intensities. The ratio of the intensity of the 
</p>
<p>Mie scattered light to that of the Rayleigh light IPart/IRal was varied from 0.0001 to 6. For IPart/IRal &lt;1, the expected 
</p>
<p>range for naturally present dust particles, it was observed that the model function was able to extract velocity 
</p>
<p>within an accuracy of ±8m/s (our data had a bias error of 6m/s), and temperature of ±5K (our data had a bias error 
</p>
<p>of 5K). For more intense Mie scattering IPart/IRal &gt; 1, such as that would be encountered in the presence of some 
</p>
<p>condensation in the air stream, the uncertainty in velocity increased to ±12m/s – still a reasonable value, but the 
</p>
<p>temperature error increased enormously. In other words, it was found that the present spectrally measured 
</p>
<p>technique can provide good measurement of velocity in the presence of any number of particles and condensation, 
</p>
<p>but good measurement of temperature can be obtained only up to an extent of particle contamination. Further 
</p>
<p>improvements may be obtained via refinements of the model function such as by accounting for the turbulent 
</p>
<p>dispersion of the particle spectrum. In addition, the particle size may also have created uncertainty in the 
</p>
<p>measurement. </p>

<p>IV. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION  
</p>
<p>Spectrally resolved Rayleigh scattering is known to provide time-averaged measurement of velocity, temperature 
</p>
<p>and density, and spectra of turbulent fluctuations in clean, particle free flows. How to measure the same quantities 
</p>
<p>in large wind tunnels and other facilities where there are naturally born dust particles and flare light from stray 
</p>
<p>scattering is a problem. The spectrally resolved technique makes a complete measurement of the optical spectrum 
</p>
<p>of the scattered light from a probe volume inside a flow. The Mie-scattered light from the dust particles and the 
</p>
<p>flare light from the incident laser beam are simply added at distinct frequency bands to the spectrum of Rayleigh-
</p>
<p>Brillouin scattering. The extraction of velocity and temperature information is dependent upon sophistication of 
</p>
<p>the signal processing methods, and the extent of contamination. To systematically evaluate the accuracy of one 
</p>
<p>such models an optical setup was created around a small jet supplied with air with different particle loading. The 
</p>
<p>amount of flare light was adjusted by coupling different amount of background scattered light to the collection 
</p>
<p>optics. The setup used a narrow line-width CW laser that was passed through the jet. The scattered light from a 
</p>
<p>probe volume was collected by a pair of lenses and was coupled to an optical fiber. The fiber passed the light to a 
</p>
<p>Fabry-Perot interferometer for spectral analysis and final imaging by a cooled EMCCD camera. A model function 
</p>
<p>of the fringes formed by the interferometer was used to extract time averaged velocity and temperature at the 
</p>
<p>probe volume. The ratio of the intensity of the Mie scattered light to that of the Rayleigh light IPart/IRal was varied </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>14 
 
</p>
<p>from 0.001 to 6. For IPart/IRal &lt;1, expected range for naturally present dust particles, it was observed that the model 
</p>
<p>function was able to extract velocity within an accuracy of ±6m/s (our data had a bias error of 6m/s), and 
</p>
<p>temperature of ±6K (our data had a bias error of 5K). For more intense Mie scattering IPart/IRal &gt; 1, such as that 
</p>
<p>would be encountered in the presence of some condensation in the air stream, the uncertainty in velocity increased 
</p>
<p>to ±12m/s – still a reasonable value, but the temperature error increased enormously. In other words, it was found 
</p>
<p>that the present spectrally measured technique can provide good measurement of velocity in the presence of any 
</p>
<p>number of particles and condensation, but good measurement of temperature can be obtained only up to an extent 
</p>
<p>of particle contamination.  ….
