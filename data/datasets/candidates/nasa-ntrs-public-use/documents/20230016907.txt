<p>Contrails Measurement and Testing 
</p>
<p>Capabilities in NASA’s Particulate Aerosol 
</p>
<p>Laboratory  
</p>
<p>Amy F. Fagan1, Francisco J. Guzman2, Derek P. Podboy3, Martin J. 
</p>
<p>Rabinowitz4, Kevin D. Brusk5 
NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, Ohio, 44135, USA 
</p>
<p>Devin M. Podboy6, Richard C. Newport7  
HX5 Sierra, Cleveland, Ohio, 44135, USA 
</p>
<p>Brett A. Thomascik8, Kristie A. Elam9, Seth J. Caldwell10 
Jacobs Technology, Cleveland, Ohio, 44135, USA 
</p>
<p>The NASA Glenn Research Center Particulate Aerosol Laboratory (PAL) is a ground test 
</p>
<p>facility for studying emissions and contrails at upper atmospheric conditions. The facility 
</p>
<p>consists of a small-scale combustor that generates hot combustion gases and soot 
</p>
<p>nanoparticles, which flow through a transition pipe into a jet nozzle, which exhausts into an 
</p>
<p>altitude chamber. Thermodynamic conditions experienced in flight are matched in the altitude 
</p>
<p>chamber, allowing the study of ice particle formation of simulated aircraft engine exhaust 
</p>
<p>plumes. A non-intrusive optical diagnostic technique based on Mie scattering provides ice 
</p>
<p>particle size, number density, and mass density. A suite of commercial extractive particle 
</p>
<p>measurement instruments provides estimates of soot number density, size, and black carbon 
</p>
<p>mass distributions. Measurements are presented for various fuels at chamber conditions 
</p>
<p>covering an altitude range of 20,000 ft (6 km) to 45,000 ft (14 km) altitude and ambient 
</p>
<p>temperatures from -48°C to -68°C. The data presented demonstrate the effect of soot number 
</p>
<p>density, altitude, and fuel-to-air equivalence ratio on the contrail ice particle formation. Ice 
</p>
<p>mass density measurements are compared to values predicted by a thermodynamic plume 
</p>
<p>similarity solution.  
</p>
<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p> A contrail is an aircraft condensation trail composed of ice particles that form in the exhaust of the aircraft when 
</p>
<p>flying in the upper atmosphere. The physics of this formation process are still not completely understood. Contrails 
</p>
<p>are a major concern related to global warming since they may persist as cirrus clouds and increase the effective

<p>radiative forcing [1,2]. Sustainable aviation fuels (SAF) can decrease contrail formation since they produce fewer soot 
</p>
<p>particles due to their lower aromatic content [3,4]. Studying the composition and formation of contrails in flight is 
</p>
<p>difficult due to the lack of instrumentation and the high cost of flight tests. Testing contrail formation in altitude 
</p>
<p>chambers is therefore an important cost-effective alternative. To this extent, the Particulate Aerosol Laboratory (PAL) 
</p>
<p>at NASA Glenn Research Center (GRC) is a ground test facility aimed at evaluating aircraft emissions and contrail 
</p>
<p>formation. The PAL consists of a small-scale combustor that generates the hot combustion gases and soot, which flow 
</p>
<p>through a heated transition pipe to the nozzle, which exhausts into the altitude chamber. Thermodynamic conditions 
</p>
<p>experienced at upper tropospheric flight regimes are matched to study the soot and ice particulates of simulated aircraft 
</p>
<p>engine exhaust plumes. Extractive non-volatile particulate instruments along with a non-intrusive Mie scattering 
</p>
<p>diagnostic provide a suite of diagnostics to characterize the soot and ice particles in exhaust plumes in the PAL facility. 
</p>
<p>PAL has had many upgrades and improvements since it was reported on in [5,6], such as a cleaner burning combustor, 
</p>
<p>a heated spray chamber with improved atomization, and upgraded diagnostics and instrumentation having better 
</p>
<p>signal-to-noise and measurement accuracy. The goal of this paper is to provide a detailed overview of the facility and 
</p>
<p>its capabilities while presenting some representative measurements of the soot and contrail ice particulates. 
</p>
<p>II. Facility 
</p>
<p> Photographs of the Particulate Aerosol Laboratory (PAL) are shown in Fig. 1. PAL has been in continuous 
</p>
<p>operation since 2006 and was designed to study aviation emission exhaust plumes at conditions relevant to upper 
</p>
<p>tropospheric flight corridors. In this unique facility, a combustor capable of burning conventional and alternative jet 
</p>
<p>fuels exhausts into an altitude chamber that simulates the temperature and pressure conditions up to 45,000 ft (14 km). 
</p>
<p>This allows researchers to study particle evolution at altitude at a fraction of the cost associated with flight testing. 
</p>
<p>The facility is designed to be highly configurable. It utilizes a swirl-atomized combustor, a heated transition pipe, and 
</p>
<p>a small-scale jet exhaust nozzle housed in an altitude chamber. The exhaust emission flow path from the combustor 
</p>
<p>proceeds through a transition pipe (2.43 cm diameter, 160 cm length). It is then injected upwards through an adjustable 
</p>
<p>diameter scaled nozzle (generally 1.8 cm diameter) into an altitude chamber with a temperature and pressure 
</p>
<p>environment corresponding to aircraft cruising altitudes.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> The cylindrical altitude chamber is 0.6 m in diameter and 1.8 m long and can simulate a wide range of altitude 
</p>
<p>conditions anywhere from sea level to 45,000 ft [ambient pressure from 101 kPa (14.7 psia) to 14.5 kPa (2.1 psia)]. 
</p>
<p>The chamber temperature range is room temperature to -79°C and is controlled by liquid nitrogen (LN2) supplied 
</p>
<p>from a 3000-gallon dewar. The LN2 passes through a series of electric heaters, converting the working fluid of the 
</p>
<p>chamber into gaseous nitrogen (GN2). The GN2 enters the altitude chamber manifold at the bottom of the chamber 
</p>
<p>and is regulated by a diaphragm-operated control valve. It then passes through a pair of screens followed by a 
</p>
<p>honeycombed flow straightener that conditions the flow at a maximum rate of 6 lb/sec. The background GN2 and 
</p>
<p>combustion products co-flow together and exit the chamber through an exhaust duct at the top of the chamber. Since 
</p>
<p>the thermodynamic characteristics of GN2 are not different from air it is acceptable to use GN2 as the co-flow. The 
</p>
<p>exhaust duct is coupled to a high-flow vacuum source provided by GRC’s Central Process Systems altitude exhaust. 
</p>
<p>It consists of an extensive, centralized vacuum system providing continuous sub-atmospheric pressures that mimic 
</p>
<p>upper tropospheric conditions. This system can provide flow rates up to 6 lb/sec and is regulated by an electro-
</p>
<p>hydraulic actuator. The chamber incorporates four vertical ports for multiple instrumentation and diagnostic 
</p>
<p>configurations. Two 152 cm tall by 10.2 cm wide port hole plates are opposite each other and contain eleven port 
</p>
<p>holes each measuring 6.35 cm in diameter. These port holes can be used for instrumentation installations and various 
</p>
<p>port hole window glass options. The two opposing ports clocked 90° from the instrumentation port hole plates contain 
</p>
<p>Starphire® glass double-paned window assemblies, also 152 cm tall and 10.2 cm wide, to provide access for optical 
</p>
<p>diagnostic measurements. The space between the two panes of glass is evacuated to prevent condensation or ice build-
</p>
<p>up on the windows. Room temperature air is also blown on the exterior surfaces of all chamber windows for additional 
</p>
<p>protection against condensation. The optical measurement plane can be adjusted, ranging from near the nozzle exit 
</p>
<p>plane to 1.2 m from the nozzle by translation of the optical diagnostic components (lenses and fibers) along the 
</p>
<p>windows on a rail system. This allows for ice particle diagnostics, including wavelength-resolved optical absorption 
</p>
<p>and scattering, which can achieve accurate sizing and number density measurements for ice particles &gt; 50 nm diameter 
</p>
<p>and ice water content &gt; 0.04 g/m3. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> PAL’s combustor is an air-assist pressure blast atomizer simulating aircraft exhaust during cruise conditions. The 
</p>
<p>combustor produces emissions with typical particulate size distributions (10-25 nm) and number densities (~1.2x10⁷ 
</p>
<p>#/std.cm³) that resemble current jet engines. If desired, a suite of non-volatile particulate measurement equipment is </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>3 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>available to acquire particulate samples from the combustion zone. The fuel and air are supplied separately into the 
</p>
<p>injector, and mixing occurs downstream of the nozzle orifice. Air is delivered at a relatively high velocity to break up 
</p>
<p>the fuel jet and disperse the resulting spray in the combustion zone. This creates fuel atomization of tiny droplets with 
</p>
<p>a large surface area-to-volume ratio, producing effective combustion performance. The maximum temperature the 
</p>
<p>combustor can achieve is 525°C. Fuel flow rates can range from 1.8 mL/min up to 10 mL/min and are supplied by a 
</p>
<p>standard Jet-A pump or alternative fuel pump. The two fuel systems can be switched in real-time, allowing continuous 
</p>
<p>burner operation while modifying fuel composition. The standard fuel delivery system incorporates dual 500 mL 
</p>
<p>syringe pumps. These fuel pumps provide constant flow with precise accuracy for extended periods. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> A transition pipe spans the combustor to the nozzle exit. It measures 2.43 cm in diameter and 160 cm in length. It 
</p>
<p>contains configurable orifices of various sizes that drop the pressure from the combustion products near the pressure 
</p>
<p>of the altitude chamber. Several axially spaced port holes are present to introduce instrumentation, sampling probes, 
</p>
<p>chemical additives, and supplemental heat. Heat tape and insulation reduce the thermal losses between the combustor 
</p>
<p>and nozzle exit. Heated spray chambers can be added to introduce varying amounts of simulated combustion products 
</p>
<p>(e.g., water vapor). Syringe pumps control the quantity of artificially introduced combustion products into the spray 
</p>
<p>chamber via atomizers. If additional heat is required aft of the combustor, a 6kW in-line electric heater is available to 
</p>
<p>provide supplemental heat to the transition pipe up to 815°C. The heater can be configured to supplement the 
</p>
<p>combustion-heated primary flow or to replace the combustor as the primary heat source. The latter use allows for 
</p>
<p>studying homogeneous nucleation since the soot source is removed.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> The Particulate Aerosol Laboratory allows for studies of the thermodynamic state of contrail formation as a 
</p>
<p>function of fuel composition at realistic flight conditions. A calibrated thermodynamic prediction code integrated with 
</p>
<p>the facility data system enables real-time estimates of the chamber conditions. Schematics of the facility, the transition 
</p>
<p>pipe, and the combustion chamber are shown in Figs. 2-4. A color camera is mounted in a port looking into the 
</p>
<p>combustion chamber along with a fire eye. Sample images of flames observed for Jet-A and a cleaner burning 
</p>
<p>sustainable alternative fuel are shown in Fig. 5. 
</p>

<p>III. Ice Particle Diagnostics 
</p>
<p>The ice particle diagnostics consist of a Mie scattering absorption and scattering measurement system that provides 
</p>
<p>a path-integrated measurement of the mean particle diameter and number density of an assumed log-normal unimodal 
</p>
<p>size distribution. A schematic of the optical system installed on the windows of the PAL chamber is shown in Fig. 6. 
</p>
<p>The optical system components and data processing are described in this section.  
</p>
<p>A. Experimental Setup 
</p>
<p>The light source for the Mie scattering diagnostic is a six-wavelength light emitting diode (LED) source that 
</p>
<p>provides illumination over the wavelength range from 360nm to 650nm. Two different light source models are 
</p>
<p>available with slightly different LED options. The spectral output of the two options is shown in Fig. 7. Only five of 
</p>
<p>the six LEDs of source A could be used because the output of the longest wavelength LED (=780nm, not shown) 
</p>
<p>was very unsteady. LED source B provides more data at shorter wavelengths which is ideal for this diagnostic due to 
</p>
<p>its increased sensitivity to particle size at wavelengths less than 450nm. Data from both sources are presented in this 
</p>
<p>paper. The light source is mounted in a thermally controlled environmental box to keep the spectral output steady, 
</p>
<p>minimizing error in the Mie scattering measurements. A 3mm diameter liquid light guide transmits the light from the 
</p>
<p>source to a fused-silica 90-mm focal length lens that provides a 25mm collimated beam through the test section. The 
</p>
<p>light source and associated collimating optics are shown in the inset photo in Fig. 6. An identical lens on the opposite 
</p>
<p>side of the chamber collects the transmitted light and sends it via 200m diameter optical fiber to an Ocean Insight 
</p>
<p>FLAME-S model spectrometer to spectrally resolve the signal. First, a ‘reference’ signal, Iref, with no exhaust plume </p>
<p></p>

</p>

</p>
<p>transmission, T, is calculated by the following equation:

<p>Not shown in the schematic is a color camera that images the 25mm beam from a 90° observation direction. The 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>signals have only been used for rough analysis in the current work since the back-scattering signal is corrupted by 
</p>
<p>significant forward scattered signal from the reflection of the incident light beam off the two glass windows on the 
</p>
<p>opposite side of the chamber. The forward scattering is likewise corrupted by the backscattering from the reflected 
</p>
<p>beam; however, the backscatter signal is much weaker than the forward scattering and likely does not have any 
</p>
<p>significant effect. We are investigating ways to calibrate the scattering signals to account for these additional 
</p>
<p>contributions. The optical system is mounted on rails so it can easily be moved to any distance from 0 to 1.2 m 
</p>
<p>downstream of the nozzle exit.

<p>B. Mie Scattering Data Analysis 
</p>
<p>The data analysis is based on Mie scattering theory [11] and MATLAB subroutines for calculating the theoretical 
</p>
<p>Mie scattering coefficients provided by Mätzler [12]. The transmission signal is related to the absorbance, A, which is 
</p>
<p>a function of the extinction coefficient, Qext, and an integration over the particle size distribution (psd) for particles of 
</p>
<p>radius, r. Multiplying the integral by the number density of ice particles, Nice, and the path length through the exhaust 
</p>
<p>plume, L, gives the total measured absorbance. Transmission, T, is a function of the absorbance.  
</p>

<p>The extinction coefficient, Qext, and the scattering amplitudes, S1 and S2, are functions of the mean particle diameter, 
</p>
<p>Dice, the width of the size distribution, , the wavelength of the illumination, , and the refractive indices of the 
</p>
<p>particles (nice) and the surrounding medium (nmedium). The spectrally resolved transmission signals are fit to a numerical 
</p>
<p>model, which assumes a log-normal size distribution with a 30% standard deviation, to provide mean particle size, 
</p>
<p>Dice, and number density, Nice. The length L is estimated from the camera images. The ice mass density (units: g/m3) 
</p>
<p>is calculated from the measured Dice and Nice values, the width of the plume, L, and the associated log-normal size 
</p>
<p>distribution. The model function assumes a unimodal size distribution of spherical particles. The index of refraction 
</p>
<p>is assumed to be that of pure ice and does not account for changes in index of refraction with temperature, which 
</p>
<p>should be small. A 30% standard deviation in the size distribution is an acceptable value based on contrail ice 
</p>
<p>measurements in the literature and that it typically provides a good fit to the data. However, the standard deviation 
</p>
<p>can be adjusted to other values, if desired. Sample experimental transmission data for various mean ice particle sizes 
</p>

</p>
<p>40,000 ft (9 km to 12 km), ambient temperatures of -48°C to -68°C, and nozzle exit temperatures ranging from 90°C 
</p>
<p>to 150°C. A sample log-normal size distribution for 500nm particles with a 30% standard deviation is shown in the

<p>IV. Soot Diagnostics 
</p>
<p>PAL includes a suite of non-volatile particulate matter (nvPM) measurement capabilities based on the SAE 
</p>
<p>ARP6320A sampling procedure with samples taken from the combustion zone. A schematic of the sampling and 
</p>
<p>dilution system is shown in Fig. 10. A thick walled 9.5mm stainless tube cut at 45° is aligned with the middle of the 
</p>
<p>combustion zone. To minimize thermal losses, heat tape and heated hoses are used to maintain the sample as warm as 
</p>
<p>possible prior to reaching the instrumentation. A flow orifice is used such that 80% of the dynamic pressure drop is

</p>

<p>bench consists of a Siemens Ultramat 23 analyzer which measures CO and CO2 by use of non-dispersive infrared 
</p>
<p>radiation and O2 by use of an electrochemical sensor. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 11 shows a diagram of the Dekati diluter system. The Dekati diluter ensures rapid mixing of the sample 
</p>
<p>which freezes the particle state, minimizes the deposition of particles within the diluter, and keeps the number densities 
</p>
<p>below the saturation limit of the nvPM instruments. Particle and moisture free gaseous nitrogen is used as the diluent 
</p>
<p>gas and is heated to 160°C. Post dilution, the sample goes through a heated line which is controlled with a Variac 
</p>
<p>transformer such that the temperature at T4 is kept at 60°C. For the nvPM system, the pressure drop across the Dekati 
</p>
<p>diluter (P1 – P2) is kept near zero which corresponds to a dilution factor of 8. If the pressure drop across the diluter 
</p>
<p>remains constant, the dilution factor should remain fixed. The gauge pressure before the nvPM instrumentation (P3, 
</p>
<p>Fig 10.) is kept near 0.8 psig to prevent flooding or reverse flow in the condensation particle counters (CPC). Where 
</p>
<p>necessary, the bend radius of any tubing is greater than 10 times the inner diameter. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>PAL has access to numerous commercial nvPM diagnostic equipment for size, number, and mass. The suite of 
</p>
<p>instruments includes an Engine Exhaust Particle Sizer (TSI EEPS), Condensation Particle Counters (TSI CPC 
</p>
<p>3775/3776), Scanning Mobility Particle Sizers (TSI SMPS 3080/3082), Laser Induced Incandescence (Atrium LII-
</p>
<p>300), photoacoustic micro soot sensor (AVL MSS+), Cavity Attenuated Phase Shift extinction monitor (CAPS PMex), 
</p>
<p>and a Cloud Condensation Nucleus (CCN) counter. For this paper, we present a schematic of the equipment limited 
</p>
<p>to the following instruments: SMPS 3082, SMPS 3080, CPC 3775, and EEPS.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fig. 12 shows a schematic of the nvPM instruments used in this work. Efforts are made to have a single path 
</p>
<p>sampling circuit where at the end of the manifold is a pump set to 0-25 slpm controlled by a mass flow meter. The 
</p>
<p>total flow rate is set greater than or equal to 20 slpm. Stainless steel barbed wye connectors are used to split the flow 
</p>
<p>across various instruments. Conductive tubing is used at the inflow for all instrumentation to prevent electrostatic 
</p>
<p>losses and silicone tubing is used at the exhaust. All instruments exhaust to a single manifold that vents to a filtration 
</p>
<p>system prior to going to ambient. The SMPS scan times are set to 80 seconds up and 20 seconds down and use a sheath 
</p>
<p>flow of 10 slpm. The SMPS 3082 uses a long 3081 differential mobility analyzer (DMA) which measures particles 
</p>
<p>between 10 - 270 nm and the SMPS 3080 uses a 3085 nano DMA measuring between 2 – 80 nm at these sheath flows. 
</p>
<p>Both SMPS systems use a CPC 3776 as their respective counter and operate at 1.5 slpm. The standalone CPC is a 
</p>
<p>model 3775 which measures greater than 4 nm and a max particle number density of 107 #/cm3. The EEPS serves as 
</p>
<p>a redundant instrument which sizes and counts particles between 5.6 – 560 nm with coarser resolution of 32 bins (16 
</p>
<p>per decade) based on their electrical mobility.

<p>V. Thermodynamic Prediction Code 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> A custom plume modeling code designated Scaling and Characterization ALgorithm for the Particulate Aerosol 
</p>
<p>Lab (SCALPAL) was created and implemented to assist in describing contrail ice formation in the NASA PAL facility. 
</p>
<p>Using peak water vapor saturation conditions, the ice production rate is first estimated. Using this information, the 
</p>
<p>code then calculates the expected ice density at the optical measurement location in the tunnel by estimating the 
</p>
<p>dilution of the nozzle plume by chamber co-flow.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> The ice production rate is calculated by psychrometric evaluation of the nozzle plume characteristics as they mix 
</p>
<p>with chamber co-flow.  Using water vapor saturation characteristics of Parish &amp; Putnam [14] the peak over saturation 
</p>
<p>is estimated. The percentage of total humidity at the nozzle exit, which will freeze out during mixing, is then 
</p>
<p>calculated. This is a derivative of the Schmidt-Appleman [15] technique. An illustration of this technique is shown in 
</p>
<p>Fig. 13. 
</p>

</p>

<p> A custom-developed nozzle plume expansion code is used to estimate temperature and water vapor content 
</p>
<p>throughout the test tunnel. The centerline decay of the jet is based on the model of Witze [16] which was shown by 
</p>
<p>Bridges and Wernet [17] to agree well with PIV data collected at NASA GRC. We note that the figure of merit for 
</p>
<p>SCALPAL is the plume temperature profile, whereas the Witze correlation estimates a normalized velocity profile. 
</p>
<p>As an initial estimate, velocity and temperature were assumed to have similar characteristic profiles. Then, using 
</p>
<p>temperature measurements made in PAL, the model radial profile distribution was ultimately calibrated to match these 
</p>
<p>data via empirical correlation. A series of tests were conducted at the PAL facility for the purpose of surveying the 
</p>
<p>temperature profile throughout the plume. The PAL flow field is considered axisymmetric. A thermocouple was 
</p>
<p>moved to various locations in the plume to construct a two-dimensional temperature profile. The measurement 
</p>
<p>locations were concentrated in regions of the plume that is well mixed, more than 10 diameters downstream of the 
</p>
<p>nozzle exit, after the potential core has closed out. Line plots of the measured data and calibrated correlation for an 
</p>
<p>example data point are shown in Fig. 14. The deviation at y values greater than 15 cm is noted and attributed to wall 
</p>
<p>heating.  Deviation in this area is considered acceptable because, while humid flow from the nozzle does migrate into 
</p>
<p>this region, to do so it has already passed through its maximum oversaturation condition and therefore all vapor 
</p>
<p>condensation processes will have gone to completion. No new ice is condensing in that area. Figure 15 shows an 
</p>
<p>example contour plot of the resulting SCALPAL output ice density. With this result the integrated ice density across 
</p>
<p>the diameter of the test tunnel may be calculated at any designated distance from the jet exhaust plane. This provides 
</p>
<p>an estimate of ice density, based solely on thermodynamic state, suitable for comparison to the optically derived ice 
</p>
<p>density measurements described in Section III. The code also provides a prediction of the gas velocity, gas 
</p>
<p>temperature, and time-of-flight of particles throughout the entire flow field within the PAL chamber.

<p>VI. Results and Discussion 
</p>
<p>Three different experiments were conducted to illustrate the effect of soot, fuel-to-air equivalence ratio, and 
</p>
<p>simulated altitude (chamber pressure) on contrail ice particulate formation. These experiments were performed at 
</p>
<p>common aircraft cruising altitudes and provide a demonstration of the PAL diagnostic capabilities. All optical ice 
</p>
<p>particle measurements were acquired at the same axial location of 1.15 m or 64 jet diameters downstream of the 1.8 
</p>
<p>cm diameter nozzle exit. Soot measurements were sampled from inside the combustion chamber just before the 
</p>
<p>combustion products enter the transition pipe as depicted in Fig. 4. 
</p>
<p>A. Effect of soot size and number density 
</p>
<p>Various fuels were burned to alter the size and quantity of soot produced in the combustor to investigate their 
</p>
<p>effect on contrail formation. These included Jet-A, n-heptane, butanol, HEFA-SPK, and two different Fischer-Tropsch 
</p>
<p>(FT) fuels. The yield sooting index (YSI) is a property of fuels that is related to the soot formation rate; a higher YSI 
</p>
<p>indicates a greater sooting tendency relative to fuels of lower YSI [18]. The YSI values for Jet-A, n-heptane, and 
</p>
<p>butanol are 60, 36, and 22, respectively. HEFA-SPK and FT fuels are SAF fuels that produce less soot than standard 
</p>
<p>jet fuels due to their low aromatic content. One of the FT fuels is derived from coal while the other is derived from 
</p>
<p>natural gas. HEFA-SPK is a Hydro-processed Esters and Fatty Acids Synthetic Paraffinic Kerosene fuel. Their exact 
</p>
<p>YSI values are not known, but they should have lower YSI than Jet-A.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Soot and ice particle characteristics were measured for these six fuels using the extractive soot instruments and the 
</p>
<p>Mie scattering diagnostic at chamber conditions of 40,000 ft (12 km) altitude, ambient temperature of -48°C, and 
</p>
<p>nozzle exit temperature of 90°C. The total nozzle air flow was 205 slpm and the fuel flow rate was maintained constant 
</p>
<p>at 4 mL/min for all six fuels. This resulted in equivalence ratios of approximately 0.24 for Jet-A and the three SAF 
</p>
<p>fuels, 0.21 for n-heptane, and 0.18 for butanol. Figure 16 shows that the ice particle number density increases as soot 
</p>
<p>numbers increase with the exception of butanol. Although the YSI of butanol is on the lower end of the tested fuels, 
</p>
<p>the effects of oxygen on soot precursor chemistry are complex. Nativel et al. showed that acetylene and propargyl 
</p>
<p>radicals increased with the addition of butanol to toluene mixture [19]. Acetylene and propargyl are well known soot 
</p>
<p>precursors species, with the formation of propargyl being a key pathway in the formation of benzene. The conditions 
</p>
<p>within the PAL combustor could therefore promote nvPM particle formation via excess acetylene and propargyl 
</p>
<p>production relative to the other fuels tested. If we consider the non-butanol data points, Jet-A produces the largest 
</p>
<p>amount of soot, followed by the three SAF fuels, while n-heptane produced the least amount of soot.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>With the exception of butanol, Fig. 17 shows that ice particle size decreases with increasing soot numbers since 
</p>
<p>there are more soot nucleation sites, resulting in smaller and more numerous ice particles for the same water content. 
</p>
<p>Again, the chemistry of oxygenated species is complex, and products produced in the combustion of butanol could 
</p>
<p>also change the hygroscopic and surface properties of the soot particles produced. Butanol could therefore lead to 
</p>
<p>more hygroscopic soot particles leading to larger mean ice particle diameter. Figure 18 shows that there is not an 
</p>
<p>obvious relationship between soot geometric mean size and ice particle size. The combustion process for each of these 
</p>
<p>fuels produces a slightly different amount of water but the difference is less than 3%. The predicted ice densities from 
</p>
<p>the thermodynamic prediction code are in the range 0.104-0.107 g/m3 for all six fuels. The measured ice densities are 
</p>
<p>plotted in Fig. 19 and most of the values fall slightly above this predicted range on average by about 8%. The ice 
</p>
<p>density is very sensitive to the ambient gas temperature; small deviations from the predicted ice concentration is not 
</p>
<p>surprising since the temperature of the chamber could easily vary by several degrees from that predicted by the plume 
</p>
<p>model. If the ambient temperature was 1°C colder than the temperature measured by the chamber thermocouple, the 
</p>
<p>predicted ice density for Jet-A would be 0.117 g/m3 instead of 0.107 g/m3. </p>

<p>B. Effect of fuel-to-air equivalence ratio 
</p>
<p> The fuel flow rate of three fuels (Jet-A, n-heptane, and butanol) was varied to provide an equivalence ratio () 
</p>
<p>range from 0.24 to 0.36 to study the effect of this parameter on the contrail ice particle formation. Ice particle 
</p>
<p>characteristics were measured using the Mie scattering diagnostic at chamber conditions for an altitude of 35,000 ft 
</p>
<p>(10.7 km), ambient temperature of -54°C, and nozzle exit temperature nominally 150°C (this temperature changed 
</p>
<p>slightly with changes in ). The total nozzle air flow was 160 slpm. The soot diagnostic equipment was unavailable 
</p>
<p>at the time this experiment was conducted; therefore, only ice particle measurements are presented. The ice particle 
</p>
<p>number density increases with increasing equivalence ratio while the mean diameter falls off slightly as shown in Figs. 
</p>
<p>20 and 21, respectively. The size and number density of the ice particles formed in the contrail for n-heptane and 
</p>
<p>butanol are nearly identical, while the Jet-A contrail ice particles tend to be smaller and more numerous. There is a 
</p>
<p>large amount of scatter in the Jet-A data at  = 0.27 but less scatter at the higher  values. Upon further inspection of 
</p>
<p>the facility data, it was observed that the burner temperature increased from 410°C to 435°C and the nozzle exit 
</p>
<p>temperature increased from 145°C to 159°C from the first to the last of the data points at  = 0.27. The burner and 
</p>
<p>nozzle temperature did not vary as much for the remaining data. This shows that the contrail particle development is 
</p>
<p>sensitive to these two values. The burner temperature likely influences the soot production while the nozzle 
</p>
<p>temperature affects the thermodynamic state in the flow field. Figure 22 shows the measured and predicted ice density 
</p>
<p>as a function of equivalence ratio. The measured values match the predictions within 6% deviation or less.  
</p>

<p>C. Effect of altitude 
</p>
<p> The altitude of the PAL chamber was varied from 20,000 ft to 45,000 ft (6 km to 14 km) while maintaining a 
</p>
<p>constant ambient temperature of -68°C to study the effect of altitude on the contrail ice particle formation. Jet-A fuel 
</p>
<p>was maintained at a flow rate of 4mL/min for this experiment. The nozzle exit temperature was 95°C and the total 
</p>
<p>nozzle air flow was 300 slpm. The ice particle characteristics were measured with the Mie scattering diagnostics; 
</p>
<p>however, soot measurements were not acquired for this experiment. The ice particle number density shown in Fig. 23 
</p>
<p>stays roughly constant at approximately 1.7x1013/m3 over this range of altitudes. The combustion and the soot are 
</p>
<p>unaffected by changes in the chamber pressure since the combustion chamber is isolated from the altitude chamber by 
</p>
<p>two orifices. This is probably why the ice number density also does not change. However, ice mass density increases 
</p>
<p>by a factor of four when going from 45,000 ft down to 20,000 ft as shown in Fig. 24. The higher ice density is 
</p>
<p>accounted for by an increase in particle size. The ice density thermodynamic prediction is shown by the dashed line. 
</p>
<p>The measured values match the prediction very well for all altitudes except the lowest one. The thermodynamic code 
</p>
<p>was calibrated to the PAL chamber via thermocouple sweeps over a limited range of conditions which never went 
</p>
<p>below an altitude of 35,000 ft (10.7 km). The deviation between the data and prediction may indicate that the code 
</p>
<p>needs to be refined with additional calibration data at lower altitudes.   
</p>
<p> </p>
<p></p>
</div>

</p>
<p>results for conditions both inside and outside of the calibrated range. Ice particulate data were gathered from 
</p>
<p>experiments spanning a one-year period and covering the full range of altitudes covered in this experiment, and as 
</p>
<p>many different temperatures and ice density values as possible. The measured versus predicted ice density for this 
</p>
<p>large sampling of data points is shown in Fig. 25 where the dashed line indicates perfect correlation. Surprisingly, the 
</p>
<p>points with the largest deviation did not show a correlation with low altitude conditions. The spread of the data 
</p>
<p>increases with increasing water concentration, but overall, there is good correlation between the data and prediction. 
</p>
<p>It is still a useful exercise to refine the prediction code calibration with additional thermocouple measurements at more 
</p>
<p>chamber conditions.

<p>VII. Conclusion 
</p>
<p>The Particulate Aerosol Laboratory is a contrails research facility at the NASA Glenn Research Center. This paper 
</p>
<p>provides details about the PAL, including the available controls, equipment, and diagnostics in the facility. An optical 
</p>
<p>Mie scattering diagnostic measures mean ice particle size, number density, and ice mass density at an axial plane 
</p>
<p>downstream of the nozzle exit. It enables measurement of ice particulates in the size range and concentrations typically </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>17 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>found in upper troposphere flight corridors (diameter &gt; 50nm and ice density &gt; 0.04 g/m3). A suite of non-volatile 
</p>
<p>particulates diagnostic equipment provides soot measurements at the exit of the burner. A custom thermodynamic 
</p>
<p>plume modeling code is available to assist in estimating the flow field and gas mix out in the NASA PAL facility. 
</p>
<p>This model provides an estimate of ice density, based solely on thermodynamic state, suitable for comparison to the 
</p>
<p>optically derived ice density measurements. The non-intrusive Mie scattering diagnostic plus extractive particulate 
</p>
<p>instruments provide a suite of diagnostics to characterize exhaust plumes in the PAL facility. These diagnostics were 
</p>
<p>used to study the effect of soot, fuel-to-air equivalence ratio, and altitude on contrail formation at a range of typical 
</p>
<p>aircraft flight conditions using a series of fuels that produce varying amounts of soot. Increasing the soot number 
</p>
<p>density while keeping the ice mass density constant results in smaller contrail ice particles at higher concentrations. 
</p>
<p>The diameter of the ice particles was shown to have no correlation with the size of the soot particles. Increasing 
</p>
<p>equivalence ratio produces more soot and water from the richer combustion conditions; therefore, the ice particle 
</p>
<p>number density increases as well. The ice mean diameter decreased slightly with increasing equivalence ratio. 
</p>
<p>Increasing altitude results in higher ice mass density in the contrail due to the lower ambient pressure. The main 
</p>
<p>parameter that changes to accommodate the additional ice density is the particle size; the ice number density was 
</p>
<p>mostly unaffected by a change in altitude. The combustion process, and therefore the soot, are not affected by a change 
</p>
<p>in altitude so it is no surprise that the ice particle number density remains unaffected as well. These three experiments 
</p>
<p>appear to support the conclusion that ice particle number density is affected most significantly by the soot number 
</p>
<p>density, while the ice particle size is governed mostly by the ice density present in the plume. Comparisons between 
</p>
<p>the measured ice density and the thermodynamic prediction agreed very well (within 6% or better) for most of the 
</p>
<p>data in these three experiments. Observing data from a one-year period covering a wider range of conditions showed 
</p>
<p>that the ice density data correlates well with the model prediction, although the spread in the data increases with 
</p>
<p>increasing ice water content.
