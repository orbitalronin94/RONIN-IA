<p>As part of the 2023 strategic analysis cycle to explore the trade space, the NASA Mars 
</p>
<p>Architecture Team wanted to explore what it takes to produce in situ on Mars many hundreds 
</p>
<p>of tons of propellants for a large all-chemical transportation system. The conceptual 
</p>
<p>operations and design of the LO2/LCH4 in situ resource utilization (ISRU) water acquisition, 
</p>
<p>propellant production and liquification system was assigned to the NASA Compass 
</p>
<p>concurrent engineering team with support from various NASA ISRU, cryogenic fluid 
</p>
<p>management, and surface power experts. The conceptual point design examined one case 
</p>
<p>producing 300 t of LO2/LCH4 from the Mars atmosphere and delivered water in 20 months 
</p>
<p>and storing the liquified propellants in a to-be-reused lander. Several of these large single-
</p>
<p>stage, all-chemical class large vertical landers would deliver the required ISRU equipment. 
</p>
<p>The required 150 t of water stock for the ISRU system was traded between three options: 
</p>
<p>delivered, pumped from subsurface ice deposits or extracted from surface soils. The large 
</p>
<p>propellant production systems consist of atmospheric CO2 collection scroll pumps, a combined 
</p>
<p>solid oxide electrolysis and methanation system to convert the CO2 and water into gaseous O2 
</p>
<p>and CH4, and various dryers, scrubbers, and separators to remove the excess water, CO2 and 
</p>
<p>H2. The liquefaction system consisted of 90 K cryocoolers to provide cold Ne to the launch 
</p>
<p>vehicle tanks to liquify these CH4 and O2 gases and store them as rocket propellants. The 
</p>
<p>systems are deployed using a 6 t (payload) capable chassis derived from conceptual 
</p>
<p>pressurized rover designs. In total, the propellant production and liquefaction systems 
</p>
<p>required three propellant production pallets, two liquefaction pallets, two water tankers, and 
</p>
<p>six 40 kW-fission surface power systems (FSPS) with cabling. All this equipment was found to 
</p>
<p>notionally fit inside two- 75 metric ton payload capacity Mars ascent and landing vehicles 
</p>
<p>(MALV). For the case where 150 t of water delivered from Earth, four cargo MALVs are 
</p>
<p>required for the full system. The same is true when the 150 t of water is extracted through 
</p>
<p>surface mining. For the borehole system, only 3 cargo MALVs are necessary. A comparison 
</p>
<p>of approaches in terms of number of landers, number and type of elements, power and time 
</p>
<p>is made. 
</p>
<p>I.Introduction 
</p>
<p>As part of the 2023 strategic analysis cycle, the NASA Mars Architecture Team wanted to explore what it takes to 
</p>
<p>produce in situ on Mars many hundreds of tons of propellants for a Mars ascent vehicle. This was one of many Mars 
</p>
<p>architectures under study including nuclear electric propulsion combined with chemical, solar electric propulsion 
</p>
<p>combined with chemical, and nuclear thermal propulsion. This ISRU architecture assumed a 75 t payload class, single 
</p>
<p>stage, MALV that could enter Mars hyperbolically, perform aero-deceleration, and land on the Mars surface. This 
</p>
<p>vehicle, if refueled using in-situ propellants could then return the crew to at least a 5-sol Mars orbit. The conceptual 
</p>
<p>MALV was designed by NASA Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC)/Advanced Concepts Office (ACO) and is 
</p>
<p>shown in Fig. 1 [1] 
</p>

<p>The conceptual operations and element designs of 
</p>
<p>the water collection, propellant production, and 
</p>
<p>liquefaction systems for this LO2/LCH4 rocket were 
</p>
<p>assigned to the NASA Compass concurrent 
</p>
<p>engineering team with support from various NASA 
</p>
<p>ISRU, cryogenic fluid management, and surface 
</p>
<p>power experts. The conceptual point designs examined 
</p>
<p>one scenario producing 300 t of LO2/LCH4 from Mars 
</p>
<p>atmosphere and water delivered from Earth in 20 
</p>
<p>months and storing the liquified propellants in the 
</p>
<p>MALV to later be used by the crew for launch to orbit. 
</p>
<p>Two additional design options evaluated use two 
</p>
<p>potential in-situ water sources in lieu of bringing the 
</p>
<p>water from Earth: subsurface ice and surface soil 
</p>
<p>mining. 
</p>
<p>These efforts focused on the water collection, 
</p>
<p>processing, conversion to O2 and CH4 gases and 
</p>
<p>liquefaction systems sizing, deployment, operations, 
</p>
<p>technologies, and risks. Figures of merit included: 
</p>
<p>performance (production rate), mass, robustness, cost, 
</p>
<p>and life. The designs were made to be single fault 
</p>
<p>tolerant. Due to known Mars dust storm events of 
</p>
<p>many months, a nuclear power source was assumed 
</p>
<p>instead of solar. The recent government conceptual 
</p>
<p>design Lunar 40 kW FSPS was modified for use on 
</p>
<p>Mars to power the ISRU systems [2]. For this study 
</p>
<p>the required 150 t of water for the ISRU system was 
</p>
<p>assumed delivered from Earth or mined on Mars. 
</p>
<p>NASA Kennedy Space Center (KSC) designed the 
</p>
<p>surface water transportation pallet (water tanker) and 
</p>
<p>water storage [3] in parallel to the Compass water 
</p>
<p>extraction options, propellant production pallet, and 
</p>
<p>liquefaction pallet designs.  
</p>
<p>For each element of the architecture, a discussion of the concept of operations of the elements follows along with 
</p>
<p>descriptions of the support systems. This paper presents layouts, element components/processes, and notional MALV 
</p>
<p>manifesting of the ISRU ‘pallets.’ A mass breakout is included. Also discussed are the advantages and limitations of 
</p>
<p>each element, along with technology readiness levels (TRL) and potential risks.  
</p>
<p>The concept of operations for the core of the Mars ISRU system (propellant production, liquefaction, and storage) 
</p>
<p>was found to be quite involved and is shown in Fig. 2. The user of the ISRU systems is the large single stage, all 
</p>
<p>chemical MALV, that, after delivering some of the ISRU equipment, is loaded with ISRU propellants and used by the 
</p>
<p>crew to reach a 5-sol Mars parking orbit. Several of these MALVs would deliver the rest of the required ISRU 
</p>
<p>equipment, including power and surface transportation. The initial option looked at having the 150 t of water (to 
</p>
<p>process with Mars CO2 atmosphere to make the O2 and CH4 ascent propellants) delivered by two MALVs at -104 and 
</p>
<p>-78 months (before crew ascent). Another study assumption was that only one MALV could be delivered every Mars 
</p>
<p>opportunity. The assumed power systems (multiple 40 kW lunar FSPs) would be delivered at the next Mars 
</p>
<p>opportunity (-49 months), then would be deployed from the MALV at least 1 km from lander, to reduce risk from 
</p>
<p>potentially damaging debris caused by subsequent MALV landings. These FSPs could share most of the design 
</p>
<p>attributes of the lunar version but would need to be positioned side by side for proper shielding operation and throttled 
</p>
<p>down once the propellants were produced and the crew arrive to minimize radiation impacts on the crew during their 
</p>
<p>Mars stay.  
</p>

<p>At the next Mars opportunity (-26 months) the 
</p>
<p>propellant production and liquefaction systems would 
</p>
<p>be delivered, deployed and begin operation. Two 
</p>
<p>surface water transportation pallets carried by the 
</p>
<p>autonomous chassis (capable of ~ 5 t water each) will 
</p>
<p>shuttle the water from the delivery MALVs in 22, ~ 1 
</p>
<p>km trips to the ISRU propellant production plant. The 
</p>
<p>300 t of gaseous O2 and CH4 is produced and then 
</p>
<p>transferred to the crew ascent MALV for liquefaction 
</p>
<p>and storage before the crew descends on the next 
</p>
<p>opposition opportunity, 26 months later. With the crew 
</p>
<p>ascent MALV refueled, the crew board and ascend to 
</p>
<p>5-sol orbit for rendezvous with an Earth return vehicle 
</p>
<p>that could use various propulsion technologies. The 
</p>
<p>large propellant production system consists of 
</p>
<p>atmospheric CO2 collection scroll pumps, electrolysis, 
</p>
<p>Sabatier, and dryers. The chassis to move the large 
</p>
<p>power, ISRU and liquefaction pallets was scaled from 
</p>
<p>conceptual pressurized rover chassis designs. These 
</p>
<p>pallets were used to carry all the elements and could 
</p>
<p>be picked up and slid off multiple times by a common 
</p>
<p>chassis. The pallets are about 4.5 m long and about 1.5 
</p>
<p>m wide.  
</p>
<p>In an effort to simplify the transportation of 
</p>
<p>propellants, it was decided to transport the water from 
</p>
<p>the source location (i.e., water delivered from Earth 
</p>
<p>and stored in MALV payload tanks or Martian water 
</p>
<p>feedstock) to the propellant production and 
</p>
<p>liquefaction pallets which themselves could be placed 
</p>
<p>within 10 meters of the MALV to be refueled. This 
</p>
<p>eliminated the need for transport and pumping of </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>5 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>cryogenic propellants. Instead, only water is needed to be pumped into the propellant production pallet, the produced 
</p>
<p>O2 and CH4 gases are sent to the MALV tanks where cold (90 K gaseous Ne) would be pumped from the liquefaction 
</p>
<p>pallet, also located near the MALV, to liquify the O2 and CH4 gases in the MALV tanks. Thus, only liquid water and 
</p>
<p>gaseous O2, CH4, and Ne need to be pumped between elements. 
</p>
<p>Producing and storing 300 t of LO2/LCH4 in 20 months requires multiple elements. Three ~ 5.4 t, ISRU pallets 
</p>
<p>using a total of ~190 kW continuous (5+1 ISRU strings) performs the production of the O2 and CH4. Two liquefaction 
</p>
<p>pallets are needed to liquify the ISRU produced GO2 and GCH4 using the broad area cooling built into the MALV 
</p>
<p>main propulsion tanks, using a total of ~45 kW. In total roughly 230 kW of continuous power (~ 11.5 MWhr/t 
</p>
<p>propellant) is required for the propellant production and liquification. 
</p>
<p>A. ISRU Propellant Production Pallet  
</p>
<p>The propellant production pallet is shown in Fig. 3. Due to volume requirements, three pallets are needed to 
</p>
<p>produce the 300 t of GO2 and GCH4. The full system can process water and atmospheric CO2 into gaseous O2 (~18.5 
</p>
<p>kg/hr) and CH4 (~4.6 kg/hr).  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fig. 4 ISRU Pallet Components and Processes 
</p>
<p>Each 5.4 t pallet uses a total of ~62 kW continuous. Fig. 4 shows the CO2 collection (atm pumps), electrolysis, 
</p>
<p>Sabatier reactor, condensers, and dryer subcomponents. These systems were based on past work, albeit at a smaller 
</p>
<p>scale (ref Kleinhenz). The core of the conversion process from CO2 and water is the electrolysis unit which, by using 
</p>
<p>alternating plates, can produce both O2 and CH4 using H2O and CO2 [3]. Each ISRU pallet can process ~ 75 kW of 
</p>
<p>power each from FSPSs and each requires a switchgear for high power loads. The thermal system requires multiple 
</p>
<p>radiators for the electrolyzer, methanators, condensers, and support equipment for a total of ~38 square meters 
</p>
<p>effective area. The mechanical system uses a cage for the ISRU components and integrates it into a common pallet to 
</p>
<p>be moved by the 6-wheel common chassis. Control strings for the plant operations communicate by either an orbiter 
</p>
<p>ultra high frequency (UHF) or a surface Wi-Fi system.  
</p>
<p>B. Liquefaction Pallets 
</p>
<p>The system to liquify the 300 t of GO2/GCH4 in 20 months on Mars is broken into two pallets due to the limits of 
</p>
<p>the 6-wheel autonomous chassis. The liquefaction pallets’ only job is to produce cold Ne to liquify and maintain the 
</p>
<p>O2 and CH4 propellants on the crew ascent MALV. The core of the system is the 90K cryocoolers producing 90K Ne 
</p>
<p>gas. The cryocooler systems in this design are based on current development and testing [4] [5]. The liquification 
</p>
<p>pallet is shown in Fig. 5. Each pallet has twelve, 90 K cryocoolers to process a combined system total of gaseous O2 
</p>
<p>(~15 kg/hr) and CH4 (~4 kg/hr) into liquid. Each 3.5 t liquefaction pallet uses a total of ~21 kW providing 90K Ne to 
</p>
<p>broad area cooling systems on the main propulsion tanks on the MALV. The thermal system design specified multiple 
</p>
<p>radiators, each offering an effective area of about 40 square meters, to serve the needs of the cryocoolers and 
</p>
<p>switchgear. Similar mechanical, power and command/communication systems are used as in the propellant production 
</p>
<p>pallet.  </p>

<p>needed to deliver the necessary components. A first order, sample packaging for one of the landers is shown in Fig.

<p>are arranged vertically to make better use of the space. A notional ‘attic’ is assumed to carry some of the smaller 
</p>

<p>G. In-situ Water from Subsurface Ice Deposits 
</p>
<p>In lieu of bringing the water from Earth, sources of in-situ water have been found a few meters just beneath Mars’s 
</p>
<p>surface. While early radar attempts to find large liquid water reservoirs deep under the surface failed, significant 
</p>
<p>amounts of water ice were found just below the surface at higher latitudes. Indeed, the higher the latitude the closer to 
</p>
<p>the surface the ice was found (Fig. 7). In fact, large ‘cliffs’ of water ice, over 100 meters high, have been observed 
</p>
<p>from orbit facing away from the summer sun [7].  
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>Several authors have suggested drilling into these, melting the ice and pumping up the water—similar to how arctic 
</p>
<p>and Antarctic research bases mine water for base operations [8] [9]. Use of this approach on Mars would have 
</p>
<p>additional challenges of landing near the ice deposits and drilling through the several meters of mineral overburden 
</p>
<p>before reaching them, and it should be noted that significant research and operational assessments would be needed 
</p>
<p>to ensure compliance with applicable planetary protection constraints. 
</p>
<p>The Compass Team performed a conceptual design to evaluate what such a mining system might look like and 
</p>
<p>how it would perform. These borehole or water drill rigs would replace the tanks of water brought from Earth. Their 
</p>
<p>operation is shown in Fig. 8.

<p>The Borehole pallet design is based heavily on the Small Business Innovation Research design and testing 
</p>
<p>performed by Honeybee [10] [11] and, in turn, terrestrial tube mining processes [12]. The design is shown in Fig. 9 
</p>
<p>and Fig. 10. It combines the tube process with a cutting head to penetrate the expected ~ 2m of mineral overburden. 
</p>
<p>It then transitions to the use of ~10 kW of heaters all along the tube to melt the water into a 15 m reservoir to generate 
</p>
<p>the required 150 t of water. This design stores the water in this cavity until the water tanker can deliver the water in 5 
</p>
<p>t portions to the propellant production pallets. This also keeps the water mine as shallow as possible. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>While this design requires a continuous 10 kW of power to keep the melted water from refreezing, it is deemed 
</p>
<p>simpler than either constructing large water storage tanks on the surface or using the MALV’s former LO2 tanks for 
</p>
<p>storage, requiring that the water be moved by the tanker twice. The operational steps of the borehole rig are shown in 
</p>
<p>Fig. 11.

<p>Fig. 11 Borehole Rig Operations

<p>A 500 W hammer drill would cut through the overburden soil. The design adds a motor to push a coil of tube down 
</p>
<p>into the borehole. Once the ice is reached, the 10 kW of heaters are used to continue boring by melting and, once at 
</p>
<p>depth, to melt the storage reservoir. An additional 10 kW is allocated for water processing, mainly boiling the water 
</p>
<p>for planetary protection concerns. Factoring in a 30 percent growth in power needs, the total peak power is projected 
</p>
<p>to be around 27.5 kW. For the thermal design, heaters and thermocouples are distributed along roughly 20 meters of 
</p>
<p>coiled stainless-steel tubing and the probe, enabling temperature control throughout the system. This allows for turning 
</p>
<p>off the heating at the top of the water storage chamber so that the top of the water cavity can freeze and seal the </p>
<p></p>

<p>chamber. This in turn allows the build-up of a few pounds per square inch (PSI) to prevent sublimation of the 
</p>
<p>subsurface water. 
</p>
<p>At the surface a cooling mechanism is facilitated by multiple radiators with a collective effective area of about 17 
</p>
<p>square meters, designed to cool the equipment and condense the extracted water vapor. 
</p>
<p>The fluid systems utilize CO2 compressed gas (gathered from the Martian atmosphere) to clear the cuttings from 
</p>
<p>the drilling process. Additionally, a water pump is employed to elevate the ullage gas of the water to the necessary 
</p>
<p>pressure for condensation and subsequent transportation. From a mechanical standpoint, the drilling system 
</p>
<p>incorporates coiled tubing attached to a cutter probe, all of which is mounted on a common pallet. This pallet is 
</p>
<p>transported by a six-wheel common chassis, ensuring mobility and stability across the Martian terrain. Command and 
</p>
<p>data handling (C&amp;DH) operations are centralized in a control rig, which is in turn managed via UHF and Wi-Fi 
</p>
<p>communications, allowing for remote controlling of the entire system.  
</p>
<p>The borehole system assumes 27.5 kW of electrical power from an FSPS delivered on the same MALV with the 
</p>
<p>borehole system. While it would be possible to utilize waste heat from the reactor to assist in melting the ice, the 
</p>
<p>interface challenges of joining a reactor with the borehole unit were left as an alternative for later evaluation.  
</p>

</p>
<p>pilot plant to both find the water ice and demonstrate the tube drilling/melting approach. The borehole rig approach 
</p>
<p>was found to be beneficial compared to delivering the water from Earth from a landed mass and timeline perspective 
</p>
<p>as it saved both a MALV cargo lander as well as 26 months. It did add complexity in the form of planetary protection 
</p>

<p>I. Borehole Mining Lessons Learned and TRLs 
</p>
<p>Extracting water from Martian sub-surface ice appears to be a practical option when employing a combined soil/ice 
</p>
<p>drilling apparatus, with the stipulation that landings occur at latitudes greater than 30 degrees to ensure accessibility 
</p>
<p>to the ice. The borehole rig integrates a drill head capable of penetrating the soil and electric heaters to thaw the ice, 
</p>
<p>thereby avoiding the potential freezing of the coiled tubing to the wall of the borehole. This configuration also 
</p>
<p>facilitates the creation of a seal within the well, providing additional assurance that the subsurface water pool remains 
</p>
<p>in a liquid state across a wider range of water temperatures. 
</p>
<p>Several of the subsystems need more technology development. In addition to some of the support systems (Wi-Fi, 
</p>
<p>AMPS, and DTAU from the propellant production and liquefaction pallets), several technologies unique to the 
</p>
<p>borehole need development. The Centrifugal Separator, essential for separating particulates from fluids without the 
</p>
<p>need for filtration media, is assessed between TRL 5 and 6, denoting advanced developmental stages with significant 
</p>
<p>demonstration in relevant environments. The boiler system, which is crucial for heating and vaporizing substances, 
</p>
<p>shares a comparable readiness at TRL 5/6. 
</p>
<p>A deployable probe harness, which is part of the instrumentation suite, is currently at TRL 4. The coiled tube 
</p>
<p>system, which includes a cutter head and an electric melter for penetrating Martian dirt and ice, respectively, is 
</p>
<p>evaluated at TRL 4/5 following subscale tests by Honeybee Robotics. The entire thermal system, involving heat 
</p>
<p>distribution and management technology, as well as the purification system is at TRL 4. 
</p>
<p>The main risks for the borehole mining system include the coiled pipe/cutter head getting stuck in the overburden 
</p>
<p>or the subsurface water chamber cannot be sealed, gets too warm, and the water begins to sublimate. Both valve seat 
</p>
<p>and ice contaminates are a concern as well as the impacts of dust on the above ground systems. Planetary protection 
</p>
<p>in both directions is of a major concern and has only been addressed in the borehole system with UV lights to sterilize 
</p>
<p>the probe head and tubing before it penetrates the soil and boiling the ice water to purify it. Although the borehole 
</p>
<p>approach may offer an efficient engineering solution, more thorough analysis is warranted to understand how planetary 
</p>
<p>protection constraints will add to the operational complexity and landed mass needs for this approach. 
</p>

<p>J. In-situ Water from Surface Soils 
</p>
<p>An alternative to the borehole for gathering in-situ water on Mars is to extract it from the surface soils. Previous 
</p>
<p>studies on the availability and potential extraction of water from soils showed that certain areas contained water rich 
</p>
<p>(~ 10 percent) minerals on the surface (e.g. gypsum) but these were only in special locations [13]. In order to provide 
</p>
<p>a Mars-wide in-situ water availability that would eliminate the need to land the crew at certain sites (like the borehole 
</p>
<p>approach), normal or ‘garden’ variety soil was assumed as the ore for the water extraction process. Because ‘garden’ 
</p>
<p>soils on Mars have been found to only have ~1 percent water it would be the most challenging in-situ approach 
</p>
<p>requiring the most soil excavated (e.g., a about 29,000 t of soil ‘ore’ to extract the required 150 t of water) and the 
</p>
<p>most equipment. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fig. 12 Potential Surface Mining ISRU Layout 
</p>
<p>Fig. 12 shows a notional surface mining approach, along with the other elements and landers (each separated by 
</p>
<p>the 1km landing keep out zone). Four, 220 m diameter surface mines would be required, each with a processing plant 
</p>
<p>at the middle and each having a dedicated excavator. The excavator was based on the current Regolith Advanced 
</p>
<p>Surface Systems Operations Robot (RASSOR) design [14] under consideration for the moon but scaled up by 2.5 
</p>
<p>times in each physical dimension to allow for carrying larger loads and reduce the estimated large number of smaller 
</p>
<p>RASSORs required otherwise. This excavator was termed the RASSORX and is shown in Fig. 13 with Table 6 giving 
</p>
<p>its performance parameters.  
</p>
<p>Table 6 Estimated RASSORX Performance Parameters 
</p>
<p> 
 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>

</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>It was found that a loader, like a conveyor belt, will also be needed to lift the ore from the RASSORXs to the 
</p>
<p>Water Extraction Rigs (WERs). This design could not be completed in the allotted time, but a representative mass and 
</p>
<p>volume was assumed. Not only would the RASSORX supply the loader with ore, but it would also be responsible for 
</p>
<p>carrying the tailings (waste soil) for disposal on the way back to the mine. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fig. 13 RASSOR and RASSORX 
</p>
<p>The conceptual design study for water extraction on Mars involved a comprehensive mining operation, estimated 
</p>
<p>to span 78 months with staged power levels of 25 percent, 50 percent, and 100 percent. The system was sized to 
</p>
<p>accumulate 150 t of water within 44 months under full power operations. Approximately 29,000 t of regolith would 
</p>
<p>need to be excavated from an area covering around 220,000 square meters, equating to over 50 acres. This operation 
</p>
<p>would be distributed across four surface mines, each with a diameter of 220 meters and a depth of 12.5 centimeters in 
</p>
<p>a single pass. 
</p>
<p>In this scenario, a single RASSORX at each mine was conceptualized to collect about 1333 kilograms of regolith 
</p>
<p>per trip, with a round trip time of 20 minutes. To meet the target, four trips per day would be necessary, with each 
</p>
<p>RASSORX operating for four hours on a single battery charge capable of 12 trips, utilizing a battery with a 3.5 kWhr 
</p>
<p>capacity (assuming an 80 percent Depth of Discharge, equating to 2.8 kWhr). 
</p>
<p>The RASSORX units were envisioned to deposit the gathered ore into a loader, which remained to be designed. 
</p>
<p>This loader would include a grizzly rock screen to filter out large stones and facilitate loading the ore into the WER 
</p>
<p>at a minimum rate of 4 kilograms per minute. The hopper's dimensions were projected to be around 1.1 by 1 meter, 
</p>
<p>with its top situated 3.15 meters above the surface for ease of access from a notional loader. 
</p>
<p>Recharging of the RASSORX units was anticipated to occur over a 2-hour period at the WER station. 
</p>
<p>Concurrently, over the 78-month operation, tailings from the mining process would be collected by the RASSORX 
</p>
<p>after ore deposition and transported back to the mining area. Options for the disposal of these tailings included 
</p>
<p>spreading them across the already mined surface or forming piles. The same RASSORX units used for ore collection 
</p>
<p>were also considered for tailings transport, maintaining the system's efficiency. 
</p>
<p>The WER water extraction conceptual design is shown in Fig. 14. The water extraction process begins with water 
</p>
<p>vapor generated by auger dryers, which is then pressurized to 7 PSI and heated to 575K using a multi-stage 
</p>
<p>compressor. This compressor is in turn cooled by an electronics radiator. The water vapor is subsequently condensed 
</p>
<p>within a roughly 2 square meter radiator, bringing the temperature down to 353K under the same pressure. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fig. 14 Water Extraction Rig </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>16 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Following condensation, undissolved dust particles are removed in a drop-out tank. The filtered water is then 
</p>
<p>transferred to a storage tank with a capacity of approximately 600 kilograms. This tank is designed to accumulate 
</p>
<p>three weeks' worth of water production, estimated at 29 kilograms per day, whose ullage gas is then pumped up to 14 
</p>
<p>PSI to be compatible with the water tanker. This tanker is scheduled to off-load the water every three weeks, filling 
</p>
<p>sequentially eight times at the mining sites (two times per site) before delivering the water to the first MALV whose 
</p>
<p>LO2 tanks are capable of storing water. Once the water is all produced and stored in the MALV and the ISRU 
</p>
<p>production plant arrives, the surface water transportation pallet will remove the water and transport it to the propellant 
</p>
<p>production pallets. 
</p>
<p>For power requirements, the WER operates with peak power demands of around 35 kW for heaters and 
</p>
<p>approximately 7 kW for water processing; with an added 30 percent for power growth, the total power requirement is 
</p>
<p>estimated at about 55 kW. Thermally, the system employs heaters with a capacity of around 35 kW, integral to the 
</p>
<p>auger mechanism. These heaters heat the solid regolith to approximately 575K to facilitate the removal of water as 
</p>
<p>vapor. Cooling and condensation within the system are managed by multiple radiators with a collective effective area 
</p>
<p>of about 11 square meters. Fluid dynamics within the WER include water pumps that raise the water pressure to 1 
</p>
<p>atmosphere, necessary for condensation and transport. Mechanical components include auger tubes and leg stabilizers. 
</p>
<p>The rig's operations are managed via a C&amp;DH unit, incorporating UHF and Wi-Fi systems for control and 
</p>
<p>communication. 
</p>

<p>L. Surface Mining Lessons Learned and TRLs 
</p>
<p>Mining soil with a 1 percent water content on Mars, termed 'garden soil,' necessitates considerable infrastructure 
</p>
<p>and duration to yield the requisite 150 t of water. The garden soil approach, in terms of time and MALVs required, is 
</p>
<p>on par with simply transporting the water directly. It demands the introduction of at least three new surface elements: 
</p>
<p>RASSORXs, Loaders, and WERs. 
</p>
<p>The viability of these mining elements for reuse in subsequent missions at the same Martian site could potentially 
</p>
<p>render them a more favorable option over sending additional water. However, the type and extent of maintenance 
</p>
<p>needed to enable these systems to gather and process an estimated 29,000 t of soil for a second mission warrant careful 
</p>
<p>assessment. 
</p>
<p>The mining operation would occupy an area of approximately 50 acres, equivalent to the size of 50 football fields, 
</p>
<p>and is expected to produce a volume of ore that would fill around 300 train coal cars. The scale of this operation 
</p>
<p>highlights the substantial logistical considerations involved in utilizing indigenous Martian resources for water 
</p>
<p>extraction. 
</p>
<p>The TRLs for various components of a Martian resource processing system were assessed, highlighting their stages 
</p>
<p>of development. The Auger Dryer system, which is used to remove moisture from Martian soil, is at TRL 3-4, 
</p>
<p>indicating that it is in the experimental proof-of-concept stage. The Power Management and Distribution Harness 
</p>
<p>(PMAD/Harness) capable of handling 3000 VDC also reaches TRL 5. A Multi-stage compressor essential for 
</p>
<p>pressurizing gases has been developed to a similar stage, achieving TRL 5. RASSORX, the loader, and the high-
</p>
<p>temperature motors to run the water extraction augers, have been developed to TRL 4-5. 
</p>
<p>The main risks of the surface mining approach are clogging, jamming, and just plain wearing out of the equipment 
</p>
<p>to process ~ 29,000 t of soil. Other risks include reuse of the LO2 tank for water storage and the ever-present dust of

<p>M. Comparison of Approaches 
</p>
<p>The various conceptual designs of the ISRU equipment allows for a comparison of the required mass, power, time 
</p>
<p>and indeed, number and type of elements needed to produce LO2/LCH4 on Mars. Several options are summarized 
</p>
<p>comparing these needs for the 300 t of LO2/LCH4 not only including the ISRU approaches (with the sub-trade of water 
</p>
<p>source) but the simpler approaches of delivering just the CH4 and producing just the LO2 from the atmosphere as well 
</p>
<p>as just delivering the 300 t LO2/LCH4 propellant. Table 9 shows the options side by side.  
</p>
<p>Table 9 Mars 300 t ISRU Options Compared 
</p>
<p> 
 
</p>
<p>If the figure of merit is number of launches the borehole mining approach shows that it can save a MALV as well 
</p>
<p>as propellant production time at the expense of an additional FSP. The borehole approach is limited to where on the 
</p>
<p>surface of Mars the crew can go which might not match all exploration needs, including planetary protection risks. 
</p>
<p>The surface mining approach, assuming global application with only 1 percent water in the soil takes as much time 
</p>
<p>and launches as taking the water to Mars from Earth and requires new elements and technologies and more water 
</p>
<p>tanker trips. Applying the approach to certain 10 percent water rich soil areas could make it more attractive but would 
</p>
<p>be as location limiting as the borehole approach, albeit with different location limitations. 
</p>
<p>Two options not evaluated in these concept studies would be to send either all the cryogenic propellant or just the 
</p>
<p>liquid CH4 and make the LO2 using ISRU methods. The latter has been suggested before for smaller (10’s of tons) 
</p>
<p>cryogenic propellant loads. Taking all the propellant would take the most LVLs and time (due to the single LVL 
</p>
<p>launch per opportunity). Taking just the liquid CH4 might be as good at the borehole approach in terms of time and 
</p>
<p>launchers but needs further assessment at this propellant level. An added complexity would be the need to replace the 
</p>
<p>relatively simple water tankers with cryogenic tankers to fuel up the MALV. 
</p>
<p>Indeed, just taking the water seems to be the easiest approach in the near term. The great promise of ISRU is 
</p>
<p>reusing the systems to produce water for the next mission, saving resending the ISRU equipment. This has at least 
</p>
<p>two challenges. First it would require revisiting the same landing site and would not be appropriate for crew visiting 
</p>
<p>different locations on Mars since the ISRU equipment most probably could not be transported to the new site. Second 
</p>
<p>and just as important is the lifetime of the ISRU components. The mining of 150 t of water and the subsequent 
</p>
<p>production of 300 t of cryogenic propellants requires many subsystems which might not have the lifetime needed to 
</p>
<p>do a repeat performance. Developers just don’t know lifetimes of their systems yet. Perhaps robotic or crew 
</p>
<p>replacement units of worn-out parts (pumps, electrolysis units, augers, motors) could be implemented. 
</p>
<p>This study only produced enough propellant to get the crew in an MALV back to a Mars 5 sol parking orbit, to be 
</p>
<p>picked up by an interplanetary vehicle to return the crew to Earth. In order to send the MALV all the way back to 
</p>
<p>Earth might require four to five times more propellant. To the first order the 300 t ISRU system could be scaled up to 
</p>
<p>the 1200 t or 1500 t case. A linear scaling seems appropriate for the overall architecture parameters. Sending just the </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>20 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>water would require eight or more MALVs. Sending all the cryogenic propellant would require 16-20 MALVs (each 
</p>
<p>of which needs multiple refuelings in Earth orbit before journeying to Mars). Only sending all the cryogens would 
</p>
<p>keep the landing power needs low, perhaps only 40 kW. The borehole option might only require 12 – 15 landers for 
</p>
<p>the first landing. Reuse of the ISRU for subsequent landings (assumed in the same location) is key. Any approaches 
</p>
<p>involving ISRU would easily require over 1 MWe of power and a new nuclear power plant since sending and operating 
</p>
<p>25 FSPs might become unwieldy.  
</p>
<p>In summary, Compass has assessed several options for implementing ISRU on Mars to make propellant – an often-
</p>
<p>mentioned feature of sustained human Mars missions. The concepts begin to show the magnitude of the surface 
</p>
<p>infrastructure needed to implement any of these approaches. It also indicates a significantly complex robotic operation 
</p>
<p>to both set up and operate this infrastructure before the crew arrives, introducing a significant challenge to ensure that 
</p>
<p>all of these robotic operations operate successfully over a period of time measured in years for this human mission to 
</p>
<p>succeed. Finally, these approaches likely add additional site selection criteria that may be in conflict with desirable 
</p>
<p>scientific/exploration mission objectives. Finally, it was shown that the Compass process can reveal changes that 
</p>
<p>should be considered in certain element design features (e.g., MALV payload volume) and operations. 
</p>
