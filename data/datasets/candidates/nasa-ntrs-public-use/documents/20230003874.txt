<p>APRES Prototype Mission Planner 
</p>
<p>System Demonstration 
</p>
<p>John L. Bresina1, Vijayakumar Baskaran2, J Benton3, Hassan Eslami4, Elif Kurklu5, 
</p>
<p>David E. Smith6, Deep Tailor7, and Ramazan Ushpayev8

<p>Abstract 
</p>
<p>Activity Planning with Resources for the Exploration of 
Space (APRES) is a mixed-initiative mission planning 
system for ground operations. APRES has been designed to 
support multi-spacecraft missions. The APRES Interface is 
browser-based and includes a plan editor, a timeline plan 
display, a temporal constraint editor, display of the state and 
numeric chronicles, and a violation resolution manager.  
Automation support is supplied by the APRES Service, 
which includes components that provide the following 
capabilities:(1) plan simulation, which determines the state 
and numeric chronicles (values of the model variables over 
time) and determines when "processes" are triggered and 
terminated based on world states in the execution trace, (2) 
violation detection of constraints and flight rules encoded in 
the domain model, and of the temporal constraints created 
by the user, (3) violation resolution suggestions as to how to 
fix the plan's violations via rescheduling. The user controls 
when and how to utilize the automation support. 
</p>
<p>Introduction 
</p>
<p>The APRES (Activity Planning with Resources for the 
</p>
<p>Exploration of Space) Prototype system is a ground-based, 
</p>
<p>mixed-initiative mission planner, which can be employed 
</p>
<p>for human space missions and robotic missions. The 
</p>
<p>support of multi-spacecraft missions was one of the key 
</p>
<p>design drivers. The resulting features facilitate the creation 
</p>
<p>of multi-spacecraft domain models, enabling an order of 
</p>
<p>magnitude reduction in the model size, likewise for the 
</p>
<p>creation of the UI configuration. 
</p>
<p> The APRES Prototype design draws primarily from the 
</p>
<p>design of, and operational experience with, the LASS 
</p>
<p>planner (based on SPIFe) deployed on the Lunar 
</p>
<p>Atmosphere Dust Experiment Explorer (LADEE). The 
</p>
<p>primary differences between APRES and LASS are the 
</p>
<p>interface framework, the domain modelling language, and 
</p>
<p>the automated reasoning components. LASS used an 
</p>
<p>Eclipse-based interface, a simpler Activity Dictionary, and 
</p>
<p>a reasoning component based on EUROPA (Frank and 
</p>
<p>Jonsson, 2003). 
</p>
<p>APRES Architecture Components 
</p>
<p> The APRES Prototype architecture consists of the 
</p>
<p>following key components (Figure 1). 
</p>
<p>• APRES Interface: browser-based front-end, 
built on top of the OpenMCT (Mission Control 
Technologies) ground operations software system 
(https://nasa.github.io/openmct/). 
</p>
<p>• APRES Service: back-end suite of file 
management and automated reasoning 
components 
</p>
<p>• ANML Editor: browser-based smart editor for 
domain models, specified in the ANML language 
</p>
<p>• APRES Data Store: file-based local storage for 
all files used in the planning process; accessed by 
both APRES and the ANML Editor 
</p>
<p> The APRES Interface includes the following GUI 
</p>
<p>components: Activity Dictionary, Activity Editor, 
</p>
<p>Timeline Viewer, Temporal Constraint Editor, and Tables.  
</p>
<p>The Timeline Viewer includes the UTC Time Zone, 
</p>
<p>Action/Process Timelines, and State and Numeric 
</p>
<p>Chronicles.  There are tables for: Initial Assignments, 
</p>
<p>Violations, Temporal Constraints, and Resolutions. From 
</p>
<p>the interface, the user can invoke the Validate Plan 
</p>
<p>operation and the Resolve Violations operation, both of 
</p>
<p>which are performed by the APRES Service. 
</p>
<p> The APRES Service components are based on the 
</p>
<p>ANML language (Smith, Frank, and Cushing, 2008), 
</p>
<p>which is a highly expressive language for specification of 
</p>
<p>models. ANML enables the creation of more accurate 
</p>
<p>models and more accurate plans. In addition to action 
</p>
<p>definitions, ANML models can include definitions of 
</p>
<p>"processes", which are not under the control of the agent, 
</p>
<p>e.g., the operation of a survival heater that is powered on 
</p>
<p>and off based on the current temperature.  
</p>
<p> The closest modelling language to ANML is PDDL, 
</p>
<p>which has many variants (for a summary see 
</p>
<p>https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_Domain_Definitio
</p>
<p>n_Language). ANML has strong notions of action and </p>

<p>state, much like in PDDL; however, ANML uses a 
</p>
<p>variable/value representation and one can specify a richer 
</p>
<p>set of possible conditions and effects than allowed by 
</p>
<p>PDDL. In particular, one can specify conditions at times 
</p>
<p>other than the start and end of an action, and also over 
</p>
<p>arbitrary intervals within the action. Similarly, one can 
</p>
<p>specify effects at times other than the start and end of an 
</p>
<p>action. The same primitives that are used to specify these 
</p>
<p>richer temporal constraints in ANML are also used to 
</p>
<p>specify exogenous conditions, as is done with timed initial 
</p>
<p>literals in PDDL 2.2. In contrast to PPDL+, ANML does 
</p>
<p>not distinguish between events and processes – processes 
</p>
<p>are allowed to include both continuous and discrete effects 
</p>
<p>at arbitrary times and over arbitrary windows. 
</p>
<p> We have also developed a web based ANML Editor for 
</p>
<p>creating domain models. The ANML Editor interacts with 
</p>
<p>the ANML Parser to detect errors and interacts with the 
</p>
<p>APRES Data Store for file management. 
</p>
<p>The APRES Planning Process 
</p>
<p>The human planner selects which action instances to insert 
</p>
<p>into the plan and schedules them.  The automated reasoning 
</p>
<p>components in the APRES Service provide support to the 
</p>
<p>user. Typically, APRES is used to incrementally develop a 
</p>
<p>plan; the user alternates between adding actions to extend 
</p>
<p>the plan and invoking the simulator and violation detection 
</p>
<p>process. In order to handle partial plans, the Episodic Plan 
</p>
<p>Simulator (EpSim) is permissive and continues the 
</p>
<p>simulation in the face of violations by making enabling 
</p>
<p>assumptions about missing preconditions, conflicting 
</p>
<p>effects, and bounds violations.  This gives the user a more 
</p>
<p>complete status of the partial plan and facilitates the 
</p>
<p>violation resolution process.  
</p>
<p>The simulation determines the chronicles for each fluent 
</p>
<p>(variable) in the domain model, specifying the fluent’s 
</p>
<p>value over time. Secondly, the simulation also inserts into 
</p>
<p>the plan the process instances that are triggered by 
</p>
<p>simulation episodes.  Thirdly, the plan validation detects 
</p>
<p>plan violations based on the domain model and the user-
</p>
<p>created temporal constraints. The following are the types 
</p>
<p>of violations detected: Unsatisfied Condition, Violated 
</p>
<p>Condition, Inconsistent Effects, Variable Bounds, 
</p>
<p>Temporal Constraint, and Inconsistent Constraints. The 
</p>
<p>first four violation types are detected by EpSim, and the 
</p>
<p>last two types are detected by the Temporal Constraint 
</p>
<p>Checker. 
</p>
<p> Upon request from the user, suggestions for resolving 
</p>
<p>the existing plan violations are automatically generated, 
</p>
<p>which specify changes to the schedule of actions. The user 
</p>
<p>has control over which if any of these resolution 
</p>
<p>suggestions to perform and which violations to resolve 
</p>
<p>manually via action rescheduling, addition, modification, 
</p>
<p>or deletion. 
</p>
<p>Any subset of the recommendations can be selected and 
</p>
<p>previewed, showing what the plan would look like and 
</p>
<p>what violations would remain. The user can then select 
</p>
<p>another subset to preview or accept the current resolved 
</p>
<p>plan to replace the original plan or reject all resolution 
</p>
<p>suggestions and manually fix the violations. 
</p>
