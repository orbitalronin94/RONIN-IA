<p>Since 2019, a group out of NASA Ames Research Center (ARC) has been focused on 
</p>
<p>implementing systematic updates to make unsteady pressure-sensitive paint (uPSP) a more 
</p>
<p>viable capability for production wind tunnel testing. Focusing on the general categories of data 
</p>
<p>acquisition, data transfer, data processing, and data visualization, the uPSP Development 
</p>
<p>Team has made several improvements to increase data quality and innovate a more workable 
</p>
<p>system that would provide valuable surface pressure data to customers. As a result of the 
</p>
<p>COVID-19 pandemic and several initial demonstration tests prior to the formal start of the 
</p>
<p>development effort, the decision was made to focus on launch vehicles as the test article. To-
</p>
<p>date this development has focused on implementation in the ARC Unitary Plan Wind Tunnel 
</p>
<p>11-by 11-foot Transonic Wind Tunnel due to the large optical access of the test section and 
</p>
<p>that the NASA Advanced Supercomputer is located at ARC. This paper summarizes the 
</p>
<p>project origins and the results of four years of development effort, which will all culminate in 
</p>
<p>a final demonstration test in the first half of 2024.   
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>Starting in October 2019 (fiscal year [FY] 2020), a concentrated effort at NASA Ames Research Center (ARC) 
</p>
<p>began to make unsteady Pressure-Sensitive Paint (uPSP) a turnkey capability at NASA facilities. This effort is 
</p>
<p>sponsored by the Aerosciences Evaluation Test Capability (AETC) Portfolio Office under the Aeronautics Research 
</p>
<p>Mission Directorate (ARMD) within NASA as the uPSP Test Technology Capability Challenge. AETC oversees many 
</p>
<p>of the large ground test facilities across NASA. Scaling up uPSP to be compatible with production wind tunnels 
</p>
<p>involves redesign of data acquisition, data transfer, data storage, data processing, and data visualization. From October 
</p>
<p>2019 – September 2023, the uPSP Development Team at ARC worked to meet this challenge. AETC’s goals are to 
</p>
<p>provide the tools to deliver the technology innovations and breakthroughs necessary to address increasingly complex 
</p>
<p>research and development challenges in wind tunnel testing facilities at NASA. Prior to the formal start of the 
</p>
<p>capability challenge, two demonstrations of the uPSP system at the NASA Ames Unitary Plan Wind Tunnel (UPWT) 
</p>
<p>11-by 11-ft Transonic Wind Tunnel (TWT) using two different NASA Space Launch System (SLS) models were 
</p>
<p>performed. In 2017, the first multi-camera test was conducted during SLS’s Ascent Unsteady Aerodynamics Test </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>2 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>(AUAT)[1]-[3] and in 2019, SLS’s Ascent Transient Aerodynamics Test (ATAT)[4],[5] was conducted. The close 
</p>
<p>collaboration with the SLS program resulted in these two “piggyback” tests to demonstrate the improvements to the 
</p>
<p>uPSP system, while traditional dynamic pressure transducers were the primary diagnostic acquiring surface pressure 
</p>
<p>measurements. 
</p>
<p>Phase I of the uPSP Test Technology Capability Challenge will conclude with a full-system Launch Vehicle 
</p>
<p>Demonstration Test (LVDT) at NASA ARC in the UPWT 11-by 11-ft TWT, currently scheduled for the first half of 
</p>
<p>2024. To date, the primary focus of the system development has been on launch vehicles, and this test will consist of 
</p>
<p>a generic launch vehicle. This will be the first time several updated and integrated components of the overall system 
</p>
<p>will be in place and tested. For example, eight high-speed cameras will be used to acquire the data. In the 2019 SLS 
</p>
<p>ATAT demonstration test, Project: Red Rover[1],[6] was implemented as a connection between the UPWT and a high-
</p>
<p>performance computational facility, the NASA Advanced Supercomputing Division (NAS), also located at NASA 
</p>
<p>ARC. This Red Rover pipeline has been improved and this future demonstration will include the transfer, processing, 
</p>
<p>and visualization of the data in near-real time. Finally, the processing software will be run as each test data point is 
</p>
<p>completed with data products available in near-real time for those with an active NAS account, or for anyone on site 
</p>
<p>to visualize on a local computer or on the Hyperwall within the NAS facility1. The Hyperwall provides the display of 
</p>
<p>image data over a matrix of computer monitors, allowing detailed representation of these data for three-dimensional 
</p>
<p>environments. We invite the community to participate in this demonstration test by either accessing the data from the 
</p>
<p>NAS from a remote location, or by travelling to Ames during the test and interacting with the system on site. The 
</p>
<p>uPSP processing software is also now open source2 and available for anyone to apply to their own data sets.  
</p>
<p>This paper serves as a summary and overview of Phase I of the uPSP Test Technology Capability Challenge at 
</p>
<p>NASA Ames Research Center, covering obstacles, improvements, and the state of the art in uPSP as a diagnostic for 
</p>
<p>production wind tunnel experiments from October 2019 (FY 2020) - December 2023 (FY 2023). It motivates the 
</p>
<p>necessity of uPSP in the study of aerosciences, the challenges with leveraging the uPSP technology, and how the uPSP 
</p>
<p>Development Team at NASA ARC produced a system to increase the functionality of uPSP at NASA wind tunnels. 
</p>
<p>This paper also provides details about the uPSP processing software and how certain errors and corrections are 
</p>
<p>implemented to increase the data quality. Finally, this paper describes future work for uPSP development, with the 
</p>
<p>assumption the capability challenge will continue with a Phase II. Some additional prior technical work published by 
</p>
<p>the uPSP Development Team may be found in Refs. [7]-[13].  
</p>

<p>II. Unsteady Pressure-Sensitive Paint 
</p>
<p>Pressure-sensitive paint (PSP) has been in development in earnest at NASA since the 1990s[14],[15], however, it has 
</p>
<p>primarily focused on the steady-state acquisition as camera technology to acquire images at fast sample rates precluded 
</p>
<p>the usefulness of having a fast-responding paint[16],[17]. The paint formula used for this development work was the 
</p>
<p>porous, fast-response PSP purchased from Innovative Scientific Solutions Inc3. In the theme of making as much of 
</p>
<p>the results of development as accessible as possible, the uPSP Development Team consistently used commercially 
</p>
<p>available resources. The paint has response rates up to 20 kHz with a polymer ceramic base coat with a platinum tetra 
</p>
<p>(pentafluorophenyl) porphine (PtTFPP) pressure-sensitive component that is a permeable biner with luminescent 
</p>
<p>molecules that sit on top of the base coat. The absorption of 400-nm light energy by the luminophore excites the 
</p>
<p>molecules so they either emit a photon or return to the ground state through oxygen quenching. Given that quenching 
</p>
<p>due to oxygen competes with relaxation via emission of a photon, the resulting light intensity measurements collected 
</p>
<p>by the camera at 650-nm are inversely proportional to the partial pressure of oxygen in the flow and can be converted 
</p>
<p>into quantitative pressure values given a calibration curve. The calibration curves are generated through a combination 
</p>
<p>of laboratory benchtop and in-situ data collection[18]. A more refined methodology for in-situ data calibration will be 
</p>
<p>implemented as a part of the LVDT in Spring 2024. 
</p>
<p>III. Project Origins 
</p>
<p>In 2013, the Department of Energy published an Advancing Scientific Knowledge Discovery Working Group 
</p>
<p>report[19] emphasizing that to accelerate scientific discovery, data must be processed as quickly as it is acquired. Often 
</p>
<p>this is a challenge for optical data sets due to the system complexity requiring input from other instrumentation systems 
</p>
<p>and traditionally large data sets, which require human interaction for processing. The deployment of uPSP for the 
</p>

<p>2017 SLS AUAT (described below) demonstrated the operational capability of the data acquisition system but also 
</p>
<p>highlighted the enormous challenge large data sets would present experimental facilities. During the 2019 SLS ATAT, 
</p>
<p>the operational connection between the UPWT and the NAS was demonstrated with the transfer, processing,

</p>
<p>demonstration are also provided below.  
</p>
<p>Characterizing unsteady, separated flow will continue to be a focus in the advancement of aerosciences. In a 2017 
</p>
<p>Future of NASA’s Aerosciences Capability Report[20], Schuster and D’Agnostino highlighted unsteady flows as the 
</p>
<p>“performance limiting factor for many flight vehicles”. To understand unsteady aero-physics, the unsteady surface 
</p>
<p>pressure must be measured. The ability to measure and compute these flows is a challenge. The future is also optical. 
</p>
<p>Optical measurement technologies are being developed and employed to address challenges in astrophysics, medicine, 
</p>
<p>and autonomous vehicles. In addition, cross-disciplinary teams will be needed to leverage skills to acquire and process 
</p>
<p>large data sets, develop robust processing routines, and use and create open-sourced applications to share data and 
</p>
<p>efficiently meet customer requirements[8]. All of this motivated the uPSP Test Technology Capability Challenge 
</p>
<p>development effort, which had three primary objectives: 
</p>
<p>1. Reduce the noise floor of the system to ≤ 128 dB 
</p>
<p>2. Reduce processing time to &lt; 24 hours for a uPSP test conducted at ARC UPWT TWT 
</p>
<p>3. Show decrease in required Kulites by ~25% in uPSP highly visible areas 
</p>
<p>With the successful completion of the planned LVDT and post-processing of the data, the uPSP system will 
</p>
<p>demonstrate that it meets all these requirements, proving the project objectives were achieved.  
</p>

<p>A. SLS AUAT 2017 Demonstration Test 
</p>
<p>In 2017, the SLS AUAT was conducted at the NASA Ames UPWT[2]-[3]. This test served as a major launching 
</p>
<p>point in evaluating the feasibility of uPSP being implemented for a production-level wind tunnel facility as a 
</p>
<p>“piggyback” demonstration test. A photo of the AUAT model installed in the NASA ARC UPWT is provided in 
</p>
<p>Figure 1. This test was the first time multiple high-speed cameras were used to collect uPSP data, one in each of the 
</p>
<p>four sides of the test section. This test highlighted the conservative nature of traditional unsteady pressure load analysis 
</p>
<p>when using pressure transducers (Kulites™). When looking at the impact of the wake from the launch abort nozzles, 
</p>
<p>for example, the pressures between each Kulite needs to be interpolated as opposed to directly measured over the 
</p>
<p>entire surface in uPSP data, as shown in Figure 2.

<p>IV. Data Acquisition and Calibration 
</p>
<p>With the incorporation of eight high-speed cameras (Phantom v2512) as a part of the uPSP data acquisition leg 
</p>
<p>of the system, and with a new process to acquire uPSP lifetime data with high-speed cameras[10], significant upgrades 
</p>
<p>have been made to the acquisition of pressure-sensitive paint data points. In addition, better camera and paint 
</p>
<p>calibration procedures have been introduced that not only reduce the uncertainty of projecting the uPSP data onto a 
</p>
<p>surface grid, but also allow for the calibration and incorporation of additional optical diagnostics in conjunction with 
</p>
<p>uPSP data products.  
</p>
<p>Internal and external calibrations of the high-speed cameras allow for a point on the 3D model surface to be 
</p>
<p>associated with a pixel on the 2D camera image. These calibrations ensure that flow features are associated with the 
</p>
<p>correct physical location on the test model. Improvements have been made to the camera setup procedure using more 
</p>
<p>robust calibration algorithms, operational changes to the measurement of the 3D targets, and a new internal calibration 
</p>
<p>board4. These improvements have reduced noise-to-pixel error to less than 0.1 pixels while additionally reducing the 
</p>
<p>test setup complexity[13]. 
</p>
<p>The incorporation of four additional cameras creates a trade-off between higher overall resolution of the model 
</p>
<p>surface and better camera coverage/overlap of the model surface[22]. Analysis was performed to determine the benefits 
</p>
<p>and drawbacks of different camera placement options in the UPWT so that the uPSP Development Team could make 
</p>
<p>timely, informed decisions on said trade-off. There are several calibration-related updates that have been incorporated 
</p>
<p>into the data acquisition and included as a part of the processing software. For example, camera-to-tunnel calibration 
</p>
<p>has been accounted for and automated. The flatfield calibrations of the camera sensors to account for any non-linear 
</p>
<p>or non-uniform pixel response has been included. Finally, methodologies for improving the overall focus of each 
</p>
<p>camera were also developed to improve the resolution compared to prior experiments and make better use of the full 
</p>
<p>range of model motion (alpha-beta angles) during a test.[22] 
</p>
<p>Future improvements that will be investigated and implemented after the planned LVDT event include using new 
</p>
<p>optical filters on the high-speed cameras. Initial tests had &gt;30% illumination on the camera sensor compared to filters 
</p>
<p>purchase in 2016. This will require further development to address excess signal leakage from unwanted sources. We 
</p>

<p>V. Project: Red Rover 
</p>
<p>The original Red Rover project supported a proof-of-concept demonstration of near-real time processing and 
</p>
<p>visualization of uPSP data for the September 2019 SLS ATAT[4],[5] wind tunnel test. The feasibility of this approach 
</p>
<p>was established, and the scope of work required for a follow-on production level system was determined. Red Rover 
</p>
<p>2.0 project builds upon previous work to create a production-ready capability through additional fiberoptic cabling 
</p>
<p>and data transfer procedures have been implemented to ensure an even faster and more secure data transfer from the 
</p>
<p>high-speed cameras in the UPWT test section to the NAS. Among the wind tunnel data systems at the UPWT, uPSP 
</p>
<p>is unique in the quantity of data that it produces and the computational processing power required. Data sets from 
</p>
<p>other wind tunnel systems are typically managed by small servers located locally and easily backed up on a small 
</p>
<p>number of external hard drives. This method was barely workable for earlier proof-of-concept tests and was quickly 
</p>
<p>realized to not be scalable to production-level uPSP. The approach to this problem was to connect the UPWT to the 
</p>
<p>NAS, which operates NASA’s supercomputers, and is a few blocks down the street from the UPWT. This idea of 
</p>
<p>connecting Ames’ wind tunnels and supercomputers was explored in the late 90’s through the DARWIN project[23], 
</p>
<p>and the uPSP project was able to reuse the fiber optic infrastructure that had already been installed. This connection 
</p>
<p>is called “Red Rover”, mnemonically named because it ‘sends data on over’. Through this connection, the uPSP team 
</p>
<p>is able to leverage the world-class supercomputing capabilities of the NAS and produce processed results as the wind 
</p>
<p>tunnel test is occurring, in near-real time. Because the data is hosted on the NAS and not stored on external drives, it 
</p>
<p>is also much more accessible to remote customers (by logging in to the NAS). The NAS also hosts a customer facility 
</p>
<p>called the Hyperwall, which allows customers to interactively visualize data, as shown in Figure 4. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 4. Engineers discuss the preliminary data transferred from the 11-by 11-foot TWT of the UPWT for processing 
</p>
<p>at the NAS facility and visualized at the NAS Hyperwall facility in near real-time [4]. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The overall data flow through the system is described in Figure 5, below. Test data from the air-gapped wind 
</p>
<p>tunnel systems, including tunnel conditions and other experimental diagnostics such as shadowgraph or infrared 
</p>
<p>thermography imaging, and uPSP data acquisition systems are transferred into Artemis 2.0. Artemis 2.0 is a pair of 
</p>
<p>servers (like a ‘bucket’) connected to a Network Attached Storage system with over a petabyte of storage. The system 
</p>
<p>offloads data from the high-speed cameras and acts as a buffer for transferring data to the NAS and has enough 
</p>
<p>capacity to locally store all data from a typical wind tunnel test if data transfer to the NAS is unavailable or not desired. 
</p>
<p>Upload to the NAS occurs over a fiber-optic connection, and data is accessed and processed using NAS user accounts. 
</p>
<p>A full demonstration of Red Rover 2.0 is included in the LVDT. 
</p>

<p>VI. uPSP Data Processing Software 
</p>
<p>In January of 2023, the uPSP Data Processing Software was released to the general public under the NASA Open-
</p>
<p>Source Agreement Version 1.3 (https://github.com/nasa/upsp-processing). The software, in development since 
</p>
<p>2015[24], has enabled uPSP technology to operate in a production wind tunnel environment and efficiently incorporates 
</p>
<p>necessary data acquisition improvements, such as camera calibration routines described above. The latest software 
</p>
<p>comprises a suite of mixed Python/C++ applications that operate on raw, high-speed video to produce surface pressure 
</p>
<p>time series useful for engineering analysis. The data flow is outlined in Figure 6. The highest-fidelity output is the 
</p>
<p>measured fluctuating surface pressure at each point on a 3D surface grid model of the wind tunnel test subject, which 
</p>
<p>can then be post-processed into more useful engineering data such as surface patch time series and dominant surface 
</p>
<p>pressure spectral modes. The software accounts for small frame-to-frame motion of the test subject and is most suited 
</p>
<p>for rigid-body test models. Active development effort is focused on upgrades to support deformation test models (such 
</p>
<p>as aircraft wings), enhanced signal processing for measurement noise suppression, and tailored data products to fit 
</p>
<p>end-user engineering workflows.  
</p>

<p>VII. Data Products and Visualization 
</p>
<p>The most basic output from the processing software is the pressure-time-history on the model surface grid. 
</p>
<p>However, this data product is often not useful in and of itself when considering the aero and vibro-acoustic analysis 
</p>
<p>possibilities of high spatio-temporal surface pressure data.  
</p>
<p>The reader is directed to Ref. [8] for a more detailed discussion of some of the outputs and visualization tools, 
</p>
<p>but some highlights are provided here. For example, dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) has been used to analyze 
</p>
<p>aeroacoustics associated with the two SLS demonstration tests that heavily relied on the visualization aspects this 
</p>
<p>technique provides.[7],[25] DMD was used to further investigate the vortex shedding tone generated by the forward 
</p>
<p>attachment of the solid rocket boosters (SRBs) to estimate the shedding frequency, the wavelengths, and propagation 
</p>
<p>speeds. DMD also verified the propagation direction, proving that one component of the aeroacoustics is moving 
</p>

<p>VIII. Conclusion &amp; Future Work 
</p>
<p>For the past four years, the uPSP Development Team at NASA Ames Research Center has worked to make 
</p>
<p>unsteady pressure-sensitive paint (uPSP) technology a turnkey capability that is accessible to production-level wind 
</p>
<p>tunnel facilities at NASA. As a by-product of this effort, an open-source processing code has been released that is not 
</p>
<p>limited by the uPSP formula or facility the data is acquired. The final demonstration of the Phase I development effort 
</p>
<p>will occur in Spring 2024 in the Ames Unitary Plan Wind Tunnel 11-by 11-ft Transonic Wind Tunnel as a Launch 
</p>
<p>Vehicle Demonstration Test (LVDT) that highlights each of the components of the system outlined in this work.  
</p>
<p>The LVDT will use a test model previously used in 2015[27] as a part of the series of uPSP demonstrations at 
</p>
<p>NASA Ames that was designed to generate buffet loads around a hammerhead payload nose based on a launch vehicle 
</p>
<p>designed by Charles Coe[28]. An image from that test is provided in Figure 10. With all the system development since 
</p>
<p>previous demonstration tests, the operational capability will be a key factor of LVDT in addition to the technical and 
</p>
<p>data quality improvements highlighted in this work. For example, given that PSP is applied after the model is fully 
</p>
<p>installed on the support strut, any model changes that need to occur will damage the paint surface, this test seeks to 
</p>
<p>find ways to minimize that damage. To streamline this and establish the proper procedure to ensure continued high 
</p>
<p>data quality with any configuration changes during testing, a new ogive nose to replace the existing hammerhead nose 
</p>
<p>will also be tested. Furthermore, to highlight the greater versatility additional high-speed cameras provide, an optics 
</p>
<p>change where two cameras on the same test section sidewall will occur to magnify (thus increasing the resolution) on 
</p>
<p>a smaller region of the model. This test will also be the first production-scale test of the high-speed lifetime 
</p>
<p>methodology where steady-state PSP data is collected using the unsteady paint formula and the high-speed cameras 
</p>
<p>as a follow on from Ref. [10]. Simultaneous shadowgraph and infrared (IR) thermography imaging will be conducted 
</p>
<p>to integrate already turnkey capabilities at the UPWT into the uPSP analysis. Finally, this model in both its 
</p>
<p>configurations is completely open-source and it is our aim to share as much as the data as possible with the wider 
</p>
<p>community to foster continued and new collaboration in using uPSP for wind tunnel testing. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 10. Image of Coe model with hammerhead nose installed in ARC UPWT 11-by 11-ft TWT in 2015 [27]. 
</p>
<p> </p>
<p></p>

<p> 
</p>
<p>There is the intention for a Phase II of development, which will focus on the greater integration of data products 
</p>
<p>and visualization into the open-source version of the processing software, integration of additional data collection 
</p>
<p>systems (such as shadowgraph and IR thermography[12] as described with LVDT), and transition to deployment at 
</p>
<p>other NASA wind tunnels other than the ARC UPWT. Future use of the system will also leverage cloud computing 
</p>
<p>and visualization tools in conjunction with parallel AETC efforts. Finally, future work will account for those added 
</p>
<p>complexities of testing aircraft in wind tunnels that were of little concern when evaluating launch vehicles. 
</p>
