



































Efficient Parametric Uncertainty Analysis of an Earth Entry
Vehicle Concept using Least Angle Regression

Thomas K. West IV∗ and Christopher O. Johnston†

NASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, VA, 23681

The objective of this work was to outline and apply an efficient and accurate parametric un-
certainty propagation approach to the analysis of convective heating on an Earth entry vehicle
concept. The described approach was based on Least Angle Regression used to solve a sparse
and underdetermined linear system in the point-collocation non-intrusive polynomial chaos
surrogate method. This approach involved an iterative process to computing the non-zero
terms of the underlying polynomial chaos model using only enough samples to converge uncer-
tainty interval predictions and Sobol index values based global nonlinear sensitivity estimates.
The Earth entry vehicle was analyzed at three points along a representative trajectory for a
Mars return mission. 329 sources of uncertainty were identified in the computational fluid
dynamics model used to predict the forebody convective heating. These included uncertainty
in flow field chemical rates, collision integrals, heats of formation, surface finite rate char model
reaction rates, wall roughness height, and the turbulent Schmidt number. Results from this
study showed that convective heating uncertainty as high as±50% of the nominal was predicted
with only about 50 evaluations of the computational model. This was far fewer than would be
required for a sampling-based approach or a full basis polynomial chaos model, which would
have required over 50,000 samples. Additionally, results showed that over 90% of the total
convective heating uncertainty was due to uncertainty in the N2 catalytic rate on the surface,
while the remainder of the uncertainty was attributed to the turbulent Schmidt number and
the wall roughness uncertainties.

I. Introduction
As NASA begins the development of a Mars sample return mission, uncertainty quantification (UQ) will serve

as a key capability for estimating risk and identifying key sources of uncertainty that drive margins. While an Earth
entry vehicle (EEV) system as a whole is subject to significant uncertainty, the thermal protection system (TPS) is one
subsystem that is plagued by numerous sources of uncertainty due to the complex physics associated with both the
aerothermodyamics modeling and the material response. A reliable TPS design is absolutely critical to ensuring not
only the survival of the collected samples but also to avoid potentially contaminating the Earth due to the uncontrolled
release of the samples by a compromised entry vehicle.

Previous work by Johnston et al. [1] investigated the impact of uncertainty on stagnation point convective and
radiative heating predictions during hyperbolic Earth entry, similar to that of a Mars sample return mission. Their UQ
approach was to assume the impact of uncertainty sources were independent from one another and that heating was
monotonic with the uncertainty. This approach significantly reduced the number of simulations needed to obtain an
overall heating uncertainty prediction but required assumptions with unknown impact. Additionally, Johnston and
Kleb [2] performed a detailed uncertainty analysis of forebody radiative heating during lunar-return Earth entry using
fuzzy set logic. This approach used a Cauchy deviates method, which required approximately 10,000 evaluations of the
computational model.

Following these previous works on UQ of planetary entry vehicle heating, West et al. [3] leveraged a technique
based on compressed sensing [4] and polynomial chaos. This optimization-based approach was shown to drastically
reduce the computational cost of propagating numerous sources of uncertainty through an expensive computational
model. West et al. [5] applied the compressed sensing-based polynomial chaos approach to the analysis of radiative
heating uncertainty on the Stardust sample return capsule backshell. While these past works both investigated the
impact of uncertainty on heating predictions and reduced the computational cost of doing so, there is a need for less
computationally complex techniques and additional computational savings.

The overall objective of this work is to outline a methodology for propagating numerous sources of uncertainty
through a computationally expensive model. A technique termed least angle regression (LAR) [6] is used in conjunction
with point-collocation based non-intrusive polynomial chaos (NIPC) [7] to construct a sparse polynomial chaos
representation of the uncertainty. Previous work by Blatman and Sudret [8] explored using LAR in conjunction with
NIPC. They showed that the technique was viable for the problems considered, demonstrating accurate statistical
moment predictions when compared to Monte Carlo sampling. The LAR-NIPC iterative approach enables potentially
significant computational savings over both traditional sampling-based approaches and full basis regression models. In
addition to outlining this approach and proposed convergence techniques, this paper provides comparisons between other
uncertainty propagation methods, such as compressed sensing, to highlight LAR-NIPC accuracy and computational
savings.

The LAR-NIPC approach is applied to a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model of convective heating on the
surface of an EEV returning from Mars. Hundreds of parametric uncertainty sources are considered in this study. In
the flow field, these include uncertainties in the chemical rate model for each reaction and species heats of formation.
Uncertainty in binary collision integrals used to approximate transport properties, the turbulent Schmidt number, and
wall roughness height are also considered, as well as uncertainties associated with the finite rate char process used to
model the TPS surface chemistry. Key uncertainty sources are identified that may serve as indicators for prioritizing
future investments to reduce overall parametric uncertainty.

In the next section, the UQ approach used in this study is described in detail. Section III then outlines the CFD
model used to simulate the EEV during entry into Earth’s atmosphere. Section IV presents the results of this study
including a description of the baseline forebody heating predictions, followed by details of the uncertainty analysis
study. The last section highlights key findings from this work.

II. Uncertainty Quantification Approach
In this section the uncertainty quantification approach is described in detail. First, an overview of polynomial chaos

is given, followed by an description of the least angle regression technique. Lastly, a discussion of convergence measures
is given along with details of performing variance deposition algebraically with a polynomial chaos expansion.

B. Point-Collocation, Non-Intrusive Polynomial Chaos
The objective with any PCE method is to determine the expansion coefficients, 𝛼𝑖 . To do this, polynomial chaos

methods can be implemented using an intrusive or a non-intrusive approach. While an intrusive method may appear
straightforward in theory, for complex problems this process may be computationally expensive and difficult to implement
as changing the deterministic model is required [13]. In contrast, the non-intrusive approach can be easily implemented
to construct a surrogate model that represents a complex computational simulation because no modification to the
deterministic model is required. The non-intrusive methods require only the response (or sensitivity [14–16]) values at
selected sample points to approximate the stochastic response surface.

Several methods have been developed for NIPC. Of these, the point-collocation NIPC method has been used
extensively in many aerospace simulation and CFD problems [7, 14, 17, 18] for improved computational efficiency
and tractability for high-dimension problems over other spectral-projection-based approaches. The point-collocation
method starts with replacing a stochastic response or random function with its PCE by using Eq. (1). 𝑁𝑠 sample vectors
are chosen in random space, and the deterministic code is evaluated at these points, which is the left hand side of Eq. (1).
Following this, a linear system of 𝑁𝑠 equations can be formulated and solved for the expansion coefficients of the PCE.

C. Sparse Approximation via Least Angle Regression
One approach to obtaining a unique solution to an underdetermined linear system of equations is to use a technique

called LAR. LAR was proposed by Efron et al. [6] as a means of efficiently obtaining the solution of a linear system
with the minimum number of nonzero terms, similar to compressed sensing [3, 4]. However, the LAR approach does
not require the solution of an optimization problem, which may reduce complexity and minimize potential error.

Starting with the active set A empty, the stagewise process outlined in Eqs. (6) through (10) is repeated, adding one
term to the active set at each iteration. This is done until convergence of the predicted responses is observed, or all of the
available training data are included in the active set. Note that the active set length is limited by the number of available
training data. As the active set length approaches that of a full polynomial model, the LAR procedure tends to a least
squares result, and no additional error is incurred by including more data. The final LAR predicted response, 𝒓, can then
be used to estimate the PCE coefficients, 𝜶, by solving the linear system in Eq. (3) with only the terms in the active set.

For computationally expensive problems, monitoring convergence of the PCE model as the LAR approach progresses
is the key to reducing computational cost. Stopping the procedure when an acceptable level of error is achieved may
limit the number of times a computational model needs to be executed. With the LAR approach, even one training
point will return a result. However, enough data should be included to reach a desired level of polynomial chaos model
convergence. Note that adaptive sampling approaches are the subject of future work. In this study, a large sample set of
points are chosen from a Latin hypercube design, and points are randomly added to the set of training data.

The accuracy of the Sobol indices depends highly on the PCE coefficients, making them an ideal measure of PCE
convergence. Also, because the number of total Sobol indices is the same as the number of uncertain parameters, 𝑛,

particularly useful for dimension reduction strategies [3, 14] and to help guide investments into effectively reducing
uncertainty with future efforts.

An additional approach to tracking convergence of the LAR-NIPC approach is to track statistics of interest to a
given analysis. This may include statistical moments or a desired uncertainty interval. Tracking convergence based on
the first moment alone should be cautiously considered as the mean is known to converge rapidly, while other moments,
such as the variance, often require much more information. Convergence of the variance, defined back in Eq. (11),
is part of the Sobol indices approach described above. Convergence of an uncertainty interval may be useful as it is
generally a desired output from an uncertainty analysis. However, uncertainty measures are determined after propagating
uncertainty sources through the NIPC model, which makes them subject to sampling error. Enough samples are needed
to minimize this error.

III. Computational Modeling Approach and Sources of Uncertainty
This section describes the CFD model used to predict the convective heating on the forebody of an EEV concept of

interest. The first section details the geometry and computational domain used in this study. The next section describes
the CFD model, followed by a detailed description of the uncertainty sources considered in this study.

A. Geometry and Computational Domain
The aeroshell geometry was a 45 deg. sphere-cone with a 0.3 meter nose radius and a base diameter of 1.3 meters.

Images of the geometry used in this study are given in Fig. 1. The computational domain was axisymmetric about the
centerline with 106 nodes in the streamwise direction, up to the shoulder, and 141 nodes normal to the wall. The grid
for the final solution is shock-aligned with 80% of the grid normal to the wall lying inside the shock layer. Wall normal
grid spacing was achieved via grid adaption with a target cell Reynolds number of 1.0.

B. Computational Fluid Dynamics Model
The flow field was modeled using the Langley Aerothermodynamic Upwind Relaxation Algorithm (LAURA)

software, which is a finite-volume, Navier-Stokes flow solver [20]. LAURA uses a second-order, upwind, discretization
scheme with Roe’s flux-difference splitting and Yee’s Symmetric Total Variation Diminishing (STVD) formulation of
the inviscid flux. The flow field was assumed to be steady state, and the boundary layer was modeled as fully-turbulent
employing the Baldwin-Lomax algebraic turbulence model [21]. Additionally, a roughness augmentation model
described by Krogstad [22] was used to model a mean wall roughness height of 0.7 millimeters. A coupled ablation
wall boundary condition is modeled on the vehicle forebody surface.

The gas properties were modeled with a thermochemical nonequilibrium assumption using the two temperature
model [23, 24]. Freestream mass fractions were assumed to be 76% N2 and 24% O2 by mass. The coupled ablation
flowfield was modeled using the following 30 species: N2, O2, N, N+, NO, NO+, N+

C. Parametric Sources of Uncertainty
Numerous parametric uncertainty sources were considered in this study. In the flow field, uncertainties in the

chemical rate model for each reaction (see Tables 4, 5, and 6 for uncertainty ranges and references) were considered.
Transport properties (diffusion, thermal conductivity, and viscosity) were approximated using binary collision integrals
given by Gupta et al. [33]. Available higher order curve fits provided by Wright et al. [34, 35], who also estimated
collision pair specific uncertainties, were used when possible. Uncertainty for all collision pairs not identified by Wright
et al. was assumed to be ±30%. In total, 210 collision pair uncertainties were considered.

The heats of formation of many ablation products have uncertainty that may impact surface chemistry. Specifically,
heat of formation uncertainty in C2, C3, and C2H have been previously investigated in detail as these are common
products produced from sublimation of primarily carbon ablators [36–38]. These uncertainties are listed in Table 1.
This uncertainty may impact the species compositions within the ablation layer, which in turn impacts the heat flux on
the surface, as shown by Moss and Simmonds [39]. The uncertainties used for these three species were given by Gurvich
et al. [36]. These are also in agreement with uncertainties identified more recently by Sharma et al. [38]. Johnston et
al. [1] used these same uncertainties for a Mars return Earth entry scenario but did not find significant impact due to
relatively low ablation rates. Heats of formation for the other species modeled in the flowfield were assumed to be ±3%.

The exact value of the turbulent Schmidt number, 𝑆𝑐𝑇 , especially for high enthalpy flows, is a subject of debate. The
turbulent Schmidt number is effectively the diffusion rate of mass to the wall. Edquist et al. [40] found that published
values of the turbulent Schmidt number typically fall in the range of 0.5 ≤ 𝑆𝑐𝑇 ≤ 1.0. Recent work by Erb et al. [41] on
Galileo probe forebody recession reconstruction suggested that the lower bound on the turbulent Schmidt number should
be 0.8. However, the massive blowing by the Galileo probe TPS makes it unique and may not be representative of the
Mars return environments considered in this study. While the current baseline solution employs a value of 𝑆𝑐𝑇 = 0.9,
the full range of 0.5 ≤ 𝑆𝑐𝑇 ≤ 1.0 is considered in this study. Additionally, a ±30% roughness height uncertainty was
assumed in this study.

IV. Results and Discussion

or nominal solution of the EEV forebody flow field is described. Then, the parametric uncertainty in the predicted
convective heating is given, based on the LAR-NIPC approach outlined in section II. Key sources of uncertainty are
identified, and a comparison with alternative uncertainty propagation approaches is provided.

B. Uncertainty Analysis
To assess the impact of the 329 uncertainty sources identified above, the LAR-NIPC approach was applied directly

to the full dimension problem. A second order PCE with 329 uncertainty sources has nearly 55,000 terms, determined
using Eq. (2). Construction of a full order expansion would require at least 55,000 CFD model evaluations, which is not
feasible. Using the LAR-NIPC iterative approach, a sample size of only 50 was determined to be sufficient for accurate
prediction of both the uncertainty bounds and the Sobol sensitivity indices. The resulting convective heating uncertainty
bounds along the surface of the EEV forebody are shown in Fig. 3 for each of the three freestream conditions. For case
1, the uncertainty ranges from about ±30-35% along the entire surface. At case 2 conditions, the uncertainty can range
from nearly ± 40% on the spherical portion of the vehicle to about [+50%, -40%] near the shoulder. These percentages
are similar for case 3. Recall that these results do not include the impact of uncertainty in the material response, which
is the subject of future work. This would likely push the uncertainty intervals wider, particularly at the stagnation point
where blowing is the highest.

Convergence of the above convective heating uncertainty bounds is shown in Fig. 4 as a function of the number
of samples used in the LAR-NIPC routine. This figure shows the convergence for three points on the surface: the

The top contributors to the total uncertainty along the surface are shown in Fig. 5. For each of the three cases, the
N2 catalytic rate uncertainty constitutes over 90% of the convective heating uncertainty at the stagnation point. This
percentage decreases to between 70-75% approaching the shoulder where the turbulent Schmidt number and roughness
height uncertainties begin to contribute more significantly due to the impact of turbulent augmentation. Near the
shoulder, the turbulent Schmidt number contributes about 15% of the total uncertainty with the roughness reaching
between 5-10% of the uncertainty. All together these three parameters make up 92-98% of the total uncertainty on
the surface, at each of the three trajectory points investigated. The other 326 uncertainty sources are found to each
contribute less than 1% to the convective heating uncertainty.

Similar to the uncertainty intervals, convergence of the above total Sobol indices can also be used to verify sample
independence and convergence of PCE. Plots of total Sobol index convergence for case 4 are shown in Fig. 6 for
the stagnation point and a point near the shoulder. Each of these convergence plots contains 329 lines for each of
the uncertain parameters, yet only one parameter at the stagnation point and three parameters near the shoulder are
distinguishable. Notice that convergence of the percent parameter contributions is achieved rapidly for both locations.
The low level of samples required to rank the key parameters is attributed to the sparse nature of EEV convective heating
uncertainty problem.

V. Conclusions
Efficient parametric uncertainty analysis for planetary entry systems is critical for informing vehicle design and

assessing thermal protection system margins. The large number of uncertainty sources and associated computational
expense of the modeling can make obtaining accurate heating uncertainty predictions challenging. The least angle
regression based non-intrusive polynomial chaos expansion (LAR-NIPC) described in this work was shown to provide
accurate heating uncertainty estimates and global nonlinear sensitivities at significantly less computational cost when
compared to sampling or other surrogate-based approaches. Key uncertainties found to impact convective heating
included N2 surface catalysis, the turbulent Schmidt number, and the surface roughness height. Future investments
could be made to further understand and perhaps reduce these uncertainties. Results from the LAR-NIPC approach
were compared to those produced by a compressed sensing technique, which is generally accepted as the benchmark
