<p>Navigation Sensor Technology Assessment 
</p>
<p>Capability for Data-Driven Systems Analysis 
</p>
<p>Esther Lee1, Paul V. Tartabini2, Brett R. Starr3, Paul D. Friz4, 
</p>
<p>Christopher D. Karlgaard5 and Jamshid Samareh6 
NASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, Virginia, 23681, USA 
</p>
<p> The capability to assess the mission performance of novel navigation technologies from a 
</p>
<p>systems-level approach and to provide quantitative results is crucial. With growing interest 
</p>
<p>and innovations from NASA’s commercial partners, data-driven results that quantify the 
</p>
<p>technological impact will guide research developments and facilitate stakeholder decision-
</p>
<p>making while requiring less time and resources. This paper presents a method to evaluate 
</p>
<p>various sensor combinations and their impact on the overall system performance using an 
</p>
<p>existing six degrees-of-freedom, physics-based engineering simulation for a government 
</p>
<p>reference lunar lander as a testbed. Selected navigation technologies over various technology 
</p>
<p>readiness levels, including Inertial Measurement Units, Navigation Doppler Lidar, and radar 
</p>
<p>altimeter-radar velocimeter, were studied in this paper. Results using this testbed to perform 
</p>
<p>sensitivity studies and to provide quantitative assessments are reported. One key finding is 
</p>
<p>that there are diminishing returns for reducing sensor errors. The most influential sensor 
</p>
<p>parameter to landing success are vehicle configuration and mission dependent.  Results from 
</p>
<p>this method could be used by stakeholders to make data-driven systems-based decisions and 
</p>
<p>by technology developers as guidance for the specific parameter improvements that will have 
</p>
<p>the most significant impact on mission success. This capability could be further used to assess 
</p>
<p>alternative scenarios should one type of technology become unavailable or to re-assess initial 
</p>
<p>assumptions and identify appropriate requirements from a system perspective.  
</p>
<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>NASA is planning to return to the Moon through the Artemis program, with goals of landing the first woman and 
</p>
<p>first person of color on the Moon and establishing the first long-term presence on the Moon. NASA will use the 
</p>
<p>Human Landing System (HLS) as a mode of transportation to land astronauts on the lunar surface. Ultimately, the 
</p>
<p>lessons learned on and around the Moon will support future human deep space missions to Mars.  
</p>
<p>Landing humans or payloads on the Moon, and subsequently Mars, will require collaborating with commercial 
</p>
<p>and international partners to develop and build a lander with novel technologies. The capability to quantitatively 
</p>
<p>assess the mission performance of these novel technologies from a systems-level approach is crucial, especially as 
</p>
<p>more commercial partners innovate new technologies. These quantitative results can identify specific areas of 
</p>
<p>research that will have the most impact on mission success and facilitate stakeholder decision-making. This 
</p>
<p>capability to perform a technology assessment that considers mission performance metrics as success criteria is an 
</p>
<p>important tool in characterizing the impact of different technology parameters.  
</p>
<p>NASA has surveyed and identified ten groups of technologies that could benefit HLS [1-10]. The Position 
</p>
<p>Navigation and Timing (PNT) technology group is one of the critical groups that could have significant impact on 
</p>
<p>the landing system. The focus of this study was to identify, assess, and quantify the impact of PNT technologies on 
</p>

<p>HLS system design, risks, and operations. Through this study, PNT critical parameters, trends in performance, and 
</p>
<p>their potential improvements have been identified and quantified. The outcome of this study provides guidance to 
</p>
<p>stakeholders (NASA &amp; HLS providers) and informs technology companies on ways to improve their PNT 
</p>
<p>technologies. Short-term benefits of this capability are the evaluations of individual technologies. Long-term 
</p>
<p>impacts of this capability are the ability to identify ways to use the technologies for specific mission requirements 
</p>
<p>and the ability to pinpoint ways to improve the technologies for better mission success outcomes.

<p>II. Method  
</p>
<p>A technology survey was performed to identify a variety of navigation sensors and related parameters for the 
</p>
<p>assessment. In order to perform assessments, a testbed is needed. To that end, the team has leveraged an existing six 
</p>
<p>degrees-of-freedom (6-DOF) lunar lander simulation developed by the Safe and Precise Landing Integrated 
</p>
<p>Capabilities Evolution (SPLICE) Project [11]. This physics-based engineering simulation is used to evaluate the 
</p>
<p>sensor suite from an integrated performance perspective and to determine its effect on the overall system 
</p>
<p>performance. A rapid assessment capability was also developed and used to quickly evaluate sensor trade studies 
</p>
<p>and to explore navigation filter sensitivities.  
</p>
<p>A. Overview of Simulation Model 
</p>
<p> The vehicle configuration modeled in this lunar lander simulation is a government reference design from the 
</p>
<p>abbreviated NASA Design Analysis Cycle (mini-DAC) [12] that uses a storable propellant system and does not rely 
</p>
<p>on cryogenics. The vehicle consists of two elements: a Descent Element and an Ascent Element. Figure 1 shows an 
</p>
<p>overview of the trajectory concept of operations. The simulation begins with the “Loiter” phase, in which the vehicle 
</p>
<p>loiters in a 100 km circular parking orbit. A De-Orbit Insertion (DOI) burn places the vehicle in an intermediate orbit, 
</p>
<p>representing the “Coast” phase. Power Descent Initiation (PDI) begins at a distance from the landing target. A braking 
</p>
<p>burn signifies the beginning of the “Braking” phase. The burn is initiated at the periapsis of the transfer orbit to reduce 
</p>
<p>the vehicle's velocity and begin its descent toward the landing target. As the vehicle descends, it adjusts its orientation 
</p>
<p>to facilitate the use of onboard navigation sensors and navigates its way to the landing target. As it nears the landing 
</p>
<p>target, the vehicle enters the “Approach” phase and transitions into a terminal descent orientation to safely land on the 
</p>
<p>surface at the last “Vertical Descent.” At each phase during the Deorbit, Descent, and Landing (DDL) trajectory, the 
</p>
<p>vehicle uses a different set of navigation sensors to provide information on the vehicle’s state to its navigation solution. 
</p>
<p>The concept of operations relating to navigation sensors is shown in Fig. 2. The Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) 
</p>
<p>provides vehicle position, velocity, and attitude measurements throughout the DDL trajectory. The Deep Space 
</p>
<p>Network (DSN) provides measurements in the “Loiter” phase, and the star tracker provides additional vehicle attitude 
</p>
<p>measurements during the “Loiter” and “Coast” phases. Additional vehicle position and velocity measurements during 
</p>
<p>the “Braking” phase and “Approach” phase are provided by the Terrain Relative Navigation (TRN) and Navigation 
</p>
<p>Doppler Lidar (NDL), respectively. Further details on the framework and simulation modelling can be found in Lugo 
</p>
<p>et al. [11]. 
</p>

<p>B. Caveats and Assumptions 
</p>
<p> A caveat to using a detailed 6-DOF simulation for technology assessments is that the results are only applicable to 
</p>
<p>this simulation and sensor suite. A different vehicle or trajectory design may require a different set of sensors and may 
</p>
<p>have different error sensitivities. The preliminary results presented in this paper will require verification with the 
</p>
<p>trajectory designed for a different sensor suite and landing mission. This paper explains the method used and how it 
</p>
<p>could be used for alternative configurations. Specific results for alternative configurations may differ from this 
</p>
<p>government reference design. 
</p>
<p> For this study, the focus was on technology hardware and no attempt was made to alter guidance software to 
</p>
<p>account for sensor uncertainties. It is possible to use software to mitigate and compensate for some sensor uncertainties 
</p>
<p>to improve sensor performance, but software cannot eliminate the effects all types of uncertainties. Therefore, it is 
</p>
<p>valuable to consider the impact of hardware technology first when selecting navigation sensors for specific missions.  
</p>
<p>C. Technology Survey  
</p>
<p> A wide variety of navigation sensors were surveyed in this study, including IMUs, radar velocimeters, radar 
</p>
<p>altimeters, TRN, and NDL. This paper reports results for IMUs, with ongoing progress reported for radar velocimeter, 
</p>
<p>radar altimeters, and NDL. Critical parameters for these sensors were gathered by collecting publicly available data 
</p>
<p>from online sources. Parameters not publicly available were obtained from the vendors who create those products. To 
</p>
<p>avoid sharing any sensitive information about specific products, data has been genericized and anonymized.  
</p>
<p>1) IMUs 
</p>
<p> One of the focuses of this study is to investigate how IMU error parameters affect the landing performance of a 
</p>
<p>spacecraft landing on the Moon. The three baseline IMUs used in this study were defined by the SPLICE project as 
</p>
<p>high-, medium-, and low-quality, representing the state-of-the-art in current IMU technology based on performance. 
</p>
<p>These were initially compared to the anticipated performance of IMUs in development. IMUs in development were 
</p>
<p>labeled as “technology pull” and represented by creating two IMUs that are representative of the equivalent quality 
</p>
<p>available in the near future. The two sets of composite parameters for the technology pull IMUs are listed in Table 1. 
</p>
<p>The first set uses the best or lowest value of each error parameter from the entire collection of all the IMUs identified 
</p>
<p>in the technology survey. The second uses the median value of each error parameter from the entire collection. The 
</p>
<p>error characteristics of the individual technology pull IMUs were generally between these two bounds. None of the 
</p>
<p>IMUs under development had higher sensor parameters than the high-quality IMU identified as the state-of-the-art. 
</p>
<p>This is because the majority of new IMUs are focused on reducing mass, power, and cost rather than improving 
</p>
<p>performance.  
</p>
<p> The exception to this is the Quantum Positioning System (QPS). There are two types of QPS, active and passive 
</p>
<p>[13]. The active QPS operates similar to GPS, requiring signals from multiple satellites to determine the location of a 
</p>
<p>spacecraft. The passive form of QPS is similar to an ultra-sensitive IMU. Only the passive form of QPS was included 
</p>
<p>in this study. QPS is a low technology readiness level (TRL) technology and there are no prototypes with published 
</p>
<p>performance parameters. It is theorized that QPS could be 1000 times as sensitive as traditional IMUs [13]. To 
</p>
<p>compare with traditional IMUs, three placeholder QPS units were used with theoretical performance parameters 10, 
</p>
<p>100, and 1000 times better than the high-quality state-of-the-art IMU used in this study.  
</p>
<p> Table 1 shows a comparison of all the IMUs and error parameters used in this study, including the high-, medium-
</p>
<p>, and low-quality state-of-the-art IMUs, the best and median values of the IMUs currently under development, and the 
</p>
<p>1000, 100, and 10 times QPS units.

<p>2) NDL and Radar Altimeter-Radar Velocimeter Combination 
</p>
<p> A different approach was taken for the NDL and radar altimeter-radar velocimeter combination technology survey. 
</p>
<p>NDL [14] is a technology being developed by NASA Langley Research Center and is not available from multiple 
</p>
<p>commercial manufacturers like other sensors. As a result, this assessment used the currently available third generation 
</p>
<p>(Gen-3) NDL parameters. The Gen-3 NDL parameters are shown in Table 2. There are a variety of radar altimeter 
</p>
<p>and radar velocimeter sensors commercially available with a range of accuracy and precision. They are approximately 
</p>
<p>an order of magnitude less precise than the NDL. Rather than evaluate commercially available altimeter-velocimeter 
</p>
<p>combinations, the altimeter-velocimeter combination was assumed to have measurement errors ranging from 1 to 100 
</p>
<p>times that of the NDL measurement accuracy to facilitate expressing landing performance in terms of the NDL 
</p>
<p>performance.  
</p>

</p>

D. Approach 
</p>
<p> The simulation framework was set up such that the sensor models were parametrized. This enables parametric 
</p>
<p>studies of the sensor parameters. The sensor parameter values identified in the technology survey were input to the 
</p>
<p>simulation framework. Multiple series of Monte Carlo simulations of 8000 runs were used to evaluate sensor </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  
</p>
<p>5 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>sensitivities and performance. Performance metrics (e.g., landing accuracy and safe landing velocity) were identified 
</p>
<p>and used to evaluate mission success. The following performance metrics were identified for this study to determine 
</p>
<p>landing success. All requirements must be met to be considered a “success.” 
</p>
<p>1) Landing precision: range to the target at touchdown is 100 m. 
2) Horizontal velocity at touchdown is less than 1 m/s.  
3) Vehicle angle is less than 3 degrees off vertical at touchdown. 
4) Maximum vehicle angular rates about all axes are less than 0.5 deg/s. 
</p>
<p>III. Results  
</p>
<p> In this section, results using this technology assessment capability are presented. Each assessment example 
</p>
<p>showcases how this capability identifies improvements for low to high TRL technologies. Ongoing progress is also 
</p>
<p>documented. The preliminary results presented will require verification with the trajectory designed for a different 
</p>
<p>sensor suite and landing mission. Specific results for alternative configurations may differ from this government 
</p>
<p>reference design.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>A. Evaluating Existing IMUs 
</p>
<p> In this assessment, a series of Monte Carlo analyses was performed to determine the sensitivity of landing 
</p>
<p>performance to the quality of existing IMUs. Characteristics of the IMUs from the SPLICE project were used as 
</p>
<p>representative values of existing IMUs that have been used in aerospace applications. These sets were categorized as 
</p>
<p>low-, medium-, and high-quality based on the overall performance of each IMU, as shown in Table 1. For this 
</p>
<p>comparison, three separate Monte Carlo analyses were performed using the same vehicle configuration, with the only 
</p>
<p>difference being the IMU sensor model, which was generic in nature and accounted for typical IMU errors in a 
</p>
<p>parametrized fashion. The accelerometer and gyroscope bias errors listed in Table 1 were reduced by a factor of 10 to 
</p>
<p>account for assumed calibration. Each analysis included 8,000 dispersed DDL trajectories beginning at deorbit from 
</p>
<p>the initial 100 x 100 km parking orbit and ending at touchdown. The IMU is active for the entire duration of the DDL 
</p>
<p>trajectory and other sensors are activated depending upon the specific flight phase as shown in Fig. 2. For each Monte 
</p>
<p>Carlo analysis there were 88 dispersed input parameters: 34 parameters related to vehicle design and initial state 
</p>
<p>uncertainties and 54 sensor-related error uncertainties (24 related to IMU and 30 related to the non-inertial sensors). 
</p>
<p>The same dispersions were used in each Monte Carlo analysis, with the exception of the IMU-related error 
</p>
<p>uncertainties.  
</p>
<p> The landing success metrics from Section II.D were computed for each of the three Monte Carlo analyses and used 
</p>
<p>to quantify the effect that IMU quality had on overall landing performance. For the high-quality IMU, only 0.8% of 
</p>
<p>cases failed to meet the safe landing requirements. There was a moderate decrease in performance for the medium-
</p>
<p>quality IMU (4.5% failure rate) and significant drop-off for the low-quality IMU (15.2% failure rate).  </p>

<p> Additional Monte Carlo studies were performed to determine the sensitivity of landing performance to each of the 
</p>
<p>individual accelerometer and gyro error parameters listed in Table 1 (i.e., misalignment, scale factor, bias, and random 
</p>
<p>walk). For the three IMUs considered, the X-accelerometer bias error was the most influential parameter by a 
</p>
<p>significant margin. Figure 3 shows the results of a follow-on study where additional Monte Carlo analyses were 
</p>
<p>performed using all of the low-quality IMU error parameters except for X-accelerometer bias, which was varied from 
</p>
<p>the low-quality level (900 micro-g) to the high-quality level (100 micro-g). The results show that the landing failure 
</p>
<p>rate for this configuration decreased as this one error parameter was lowered. When the error fell below 500 micro-g, 
</p>
<p>the overall landing performance of the configuration with the low-quality IMU actually surpassed the configuration 
</p>
<p>with the medium-quality IMU. When the error was reduced to 100 micro-g, landing performance nearly matched that 
</p>
<p>of the high-quality IMU. This assessment highlights the capability to evaluate how individual error parameters affect 
</p>
<p>specific landing metrics. Results can be relayed to developers to indicate where further investment would be most 
</p>
<p>beneficial

<p>B. Evaluating IMUs Currently Under Development 
</p>
<p>Another Monte Carlo study was performed to assess the performance of the “technology pull” IMUs described 
</p>
<p>in Section II.C. Table 1 lists representative values of the error parameters associated with the technology pull IMUs. 
</p>
<p>These IMUs are currently under development at several vendors, with a focus on reducing mass, power, and cost 
</p>
<p>rather than improving performance. The goal of this study was to determine if these future IMUs, which are 
</p>
<p>currently at mid to high TRL, could be used for precision landing on the Moon, and if not, what improvements 
</p>
<p>would make them suitable for this purpose.

</p>
<p>To assess performance, two 8000-case Monte Carlo analyses were conducted; one was conducted with each 
</p>
<p>error parameter set to Median and the other with each parameter set to Best. The landing success metrics were 
</p>
<p>computed (see Section II.D). As was done previously, accelerometer and bias terms were reduced by a factor of 10 
</p>
<p>to account for on-orbit calibration. For the representative technology pull IMU composed of median error values, the 
</p>
<p>overall successful landing percentage (shown in Table 3) was only 12.6%, which is not sufficient to support a lunar 
</p>
<p>landing mission. When all of the best (i.e., lowest) error characteristics were assumed, the success rate approached 
</p>
<p>95%, which is comparable to the existing medium-quality IMU considered in Section III.A. Additional Monte Carlo 
</p>
<p>analyses were performed to evaluate sensitivities and identify which specific error parameters were most influential 
</p>
<p>on the overall landing success rate. Initially, individual Monte Carlo analyses were performed by changing one error 
</p>
<p>parameter at a time from the median value to the best value. For example, the “Best Scale Factor” case assumed 
</p>
<p>median values for accelerometer/gyroscope misalignment, random walk, and bias errors but assumed the best value 
</p>
<p>for scale factor errors. Results for each of these sensitivity analyses are shown in Fig. 4, where it is seen that landing 
</p>
<p>performance was most sensitive to misalignment errors and random walk.

<p>However, even when the best value for misalignment was used, the landing success rate was still under 50%. 
</p>
<p>Additional cases considered using the best values for more than one error parameter and it was found that the largest 
</p>
<p>improvement occurred with best values for misalignment and random walk errors. For that case, the landing success 
</p>
<p>rate exceeded 90%, and the improvement over the baseline median IMU was larger than the sum of the individual 
</p>
<p>improvements when each error was increased one at a time.  
</p>
<p>The high sensitivity to misalignment and random walk errors for the technology pull IMUs differed from the 
</p>
<p>previous study of existing IMUs, where accelerometer bias was found to be most influential error parameter. This 
</p>
<p>difference was likely due to the smaller bias and the much larger misalignment errors associated with the technology </p>

</p>
<p>showcases the capability to identify sensitive parameters for an IMU with median values and the parameters to 
</p>
<p>improve for better landing success for lunar missions. For vehicles that use a median value IMU, improvements in 
</p>
<p>misalignment and random walk errors have the potential to improve landing success.

<p>C. Evaluating QPS 
</p>
<p> This assessment focused on identifying the level of improvements necessary for a passive QPS that has the 
</p>
<p>characteristics of an ultra-sensitive IMU. Specific parameters tied to a physical prototype were not used because the 
</p>
<p>technology survey did not find prototypes for this specific technology. However, sources claim 1000 times 
</p>
<p>improvement over existing IMUs [12]. Parameters of the high-quality IMU were scaled to represent three quality 
</p>
<p>levels of QPS, shown in Table 1, by reducing the sensor error parameters by 10, 100, and 1000 times, which is 
</p>
<p>equivalent to reducing the parameters by 90%, 99%, and 99.9%, respectively. Three Monte Carlo analyses were 
</p>
<p>performed using the DDL trajectory with the IMU parameters for each QPS quality level. Landing success rates were 
</p>
<p>calculated using the criteria listed in Section II.D and compared. The results showed that the landing success rate for 
</p>
<p>all three exceeded 99%, and that the differences in landing success rate were within 0.1%.  
</p>
<p> Because the success rates were very high, a follow-on study was performed to explore the threshold where the 
</p>
<p>landing success rate approached 99%. Additional Monte Carlo runs were performed by reducing the error parameters 
</p>
<p>of the high-quality IMU by 50% and 80%. Results in Fig. 5 show that reducing IMU errors by 50% reduces failed 
</p>
<p>landings significantly from the current state-of-the-art high-quality IMU. Results further show that reducing error past 
</p>
<p>50% has diminishing returns. Landing success rates for the representative IMUs with more than 50% error reduction 
</p>
<p>oscillate within 0.1% due to Monte Carlo dispersions. This assessment is an example of the capability’s potential to 
</p>
<p>identify the level of improvements technology companies need to develop in IMUs for successful lunar landing. 
</p>
<p>Instead of improving the parameters to 1000 times better than the current state-of-the-art IMU, equivalent to reducing 
</p>
<p>the errors by 99.9%, resources can be allotted to improving the parameters by 50% for successful lunar landing 
</p>
<p>missions. The level of improvements needed may vary depending on the vehicle and trajectory design.  
</p>

<p>D. Ongoing Work: Evaluating Radar Altimeter and Radar Velocimeter Combination as an Alternative for 
</p>
<p>NDL 
</p>
<p>In the government reference design, the descent element uses a NDL sensor to measure altitude and velocity during 
</p>
<p>the “Approach” and “Vertical Descent” phases of its descent trajectory shown in Fig. 1. The mini-DAC simulation 
</p>
<p>incorporates a high-fidelity model of the third generation NDL (Gen-3 NDL) that begins measuring altitude and 
</p>
<p>velocity at an altitude of 3 km above ground and continues measurements down to an altitude of approximately 30 m 
</p>
<p>above ground.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
Fig. 6 Gen-3 NDL. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The Gen-3 NDL simulation model accounts for the effects of range to ground and the vehicle’s velocity on its 
</p>
<p>measurements, thus its measurement uncertainty is a function of line-of-sight (LOS) range to ground and the vehicle’s 
</p>
<p>velocity. The Gen-3 NDL hardware is shown in Fig. 6 and its performance specifications are shown in Table 2. A 
</p>
<p>fourth generation NDL (Gen-4 NDL) design has been released that increases the NDL’s maximum line-of-sight range 
</p>
<p>to 7 km and improves the precision of its velocity and altitude measurements. However, the Gen-4 NDL simulation 
</p>
<p>model had not been released for integration into the government reference simulation at the time of this study. 
</p>
<p>Due to the high-fidelity nature of the NDL model, it was not possible to represent the Gen-4 NDL measurement 
</p>
<p>precision in the mini-DAC simulation by adjusting simulation inputs, thus the effect of the Gen-4 NDL advancements 
</p>
<p>on landing performance could not be quantified without the Gen-4 NDL simulation model. However, a qualitative 
</p>
<p>assessment of increasing the NDL’s maximum operational altitude was made by starting NDL measurements in the 
</p>
<p>mini-DAC simulation at an altitude of 5 km above ground, instead of an altitude of 3 km in the original simulation. 
</p>
<p>With the 5 km start altitude, the percentage of fully successful landing cases increased by 5%. An improved estimate 
</p>
<p>of the lander’s altitude and velocity earlier in the descent trajectory has a significant effect on the government reference 
</p>
<p>mini-DAC lander making a successful landing. However, the landing performance improvement using the Gen-4 NDL 
</p>
<p>model may be different than the qualitative 5% estimate. 
</p>
<p>The combination of a radar altimeter and a radar velocimeter as an alternative or redundant system for the NDL 
</p>
<p>was also evaluated to determine the landing performance. The government reference mini-DAC simulation had low-
</p>
<p>fidelity radar altimeter and radar velocimeter models available for use in the navigation filter. The simulation was 
</p>
<p>modified to use a radar altimeter sensor and a radar velocimeter sensor in place of the NDL. Currently available radar 
</p>
<p>altimeter and radar velocimeter sensor measurements are approximately 10 to 25 times less precise than the NDL 
</p>
<p>sensor and radar altimeters have a lower operational altitude than the NDL. Rather than simulate a particular radar 
</p>
<p>altimeter or radar velocimeter sensor, a generic approach was used where the precision of the altitude and velocity 
</p>
<p>measurements were varied from the Gen-3 NDL specifications to 100 times less precise than the Gen-3 NDL 
</p>
<p>specifications. The measurement precision was varied in the simulation by applying 1, 10, 25, 50, and 100 multipliers 
</p>
<p>on the Gen-3 NDL standard deviation of the sensor measurements to simulate an altitude and velocity measurement 
</p>
<p>system over a range of quality. The altitudes above ground at which the radar velocimeter and radar altimeter begin 
</p>
<p>taking measurements were also changed in the simulation to be representative of state-of-the-art radar altimeter and 
</p>
<p>radar velocimeter sensors. This approach quantified the effect on landing performance of using more precise, state-
</p>
<p>of-the-art, and less precise radar altimeter-radar velocimeter sensor combinations. Figure 7 shows the percentage of 
</p>
<p>successful landings normalized to the radar altimeter and radar velocimeter quality equivalent to the Gen-3 NDL 
</p>
<p>measurement precision. The results indicate that there is little degradation in the landing performance for radar </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  
</p>
<p>10 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>altimeters and radar velocimeters with up to 10 times less precise measurements. However, the landing performance 
</p>
<p>does begin to degrade nearly linearly as the radar altimeter and radar velocimeter precision decreases more than 10 
</p>
<p>times that of the Gen-3 NDL, with a 3% degradation at 100 times less precise measurements. The altimeter and 
</p>
<p>velocimeter models in the government reference mini-DAC simulation were low fidelity. Thus, the trend in landing 
</p>
<p>performance may change with higher fidelity models.  
</p>
<p>This ongoing assessment evaluates the impact of an altitude and velocity measurement unit and its effects on the 
</p>
<p>overall success of lunar landing missions. The trends revealed an error threshold the altitude and velocity measurement 
</p>
<p>system must meet to ensure landing success.  
</p>

<p>IV. Enhancing the Technology Assessment Capability using NewSTEP 
</p>
<p> The Monte Carlo approach of assessing sensor performance can be costly in terms of the time needed to run 
</p>
<p>thousands of high fidelity 6-DOF simulations. New Statistical Trajectory Estimation Program (NewSTEP) is a Kalman 
</p>
<p>filter code specifically formulated for solving trajectory reconstruction problems [15]. NewSTEP can be configured 
</p>
<p>to emulate navigation system performance and can produce uncertainty estimates from linear covariance analysis [16]. 
</p>
<p>Linear covariance analysis enables rapid trade studies as it does not require Monte Carlo techniques for uncertainty 
</p>
<p>quantification. This rapid assessment capability can be used to explore sensor capabilities or other navigation filter 
</p>
<p>assumptions, which includes filter-tuning parameters to ensure filter consistency. Filter consistency is a measure of 
</p>
<p>how well the filter's estimate of its navigation errors match with actual error. Inconsistent filters can cause issues: for 
</p>
<p>instance, if the vehicle cannot make accurate estimation of its position above ground, it could engage its landing 
</p>
<p>sequence too early or too late. However, NewSTEP’s linear covariance analysis mode does not capture dispersions as 
</p>
<p>it uses a nominal trajectory to propagate uncertainty estimates. Therefore, it is complimentary to the Monte Carlo 
</p>
<p>approach. 
</p>
<p>A. NewSTEP Results 
</p>
<p> The trajectory reconstruction tool was used in its linear covariance analysis mode to assess various IMUs for the 
</p>
<p>government reference vehicle. An initial linear covariance analysis was performed using the SPLICE-defined IMU </p>

</p>
<p>Table 1. The results of the linear covariance analysis are shown in Fig. 8, comparing the 3-sigma values of vehicle 
</p>
<p>position error (Fig. 8a), vehicle velocity error (Fig. 8b), and vehicle attitude error (Fig. 8c) measured from the IMUs 
</p>
<p>over the DDL trajectory simulation. These results are for pure inertial navigation based on DSN initialization and do 
</p>
<p>not consider other sensors such as TRN. It is expected that a better IMU quality would lead to smaller measurement 
</p>
<p>errors. The results indicate nearly identical navigation performance between the medium- and high-quality IMUs for

</p>
<p>uncertainty predictions are increased.  The second approach is known as Lear’s method [19, 20], which is a form of 
</p>

</p>

<p>V. Summary and Future Work 
</p>
<p> Assessment examples presented in this paper show the short- and long-term impact of the technology assessment 
</p>
<p>capability. Assessment results can be used to evaluate individual technologies and their impact on lunar landing 
</p>
<p>missions and to identify sensitive parameters and the improvements necessary to increase landing success rates. Trends 
</p>
<p>identified can impact technology-to-mission infusion and technology development decisions. It is important to 
</p>
<p>reassess the results for specific mission and vehicle configurations as factors such as sensor coupling may affect 
</p>
<p>results. This capability could be further used to assess alternative scenarios should one type of technology become 
</p>
<p>unavailable or to reassess initial assumptions and identify appropriate requirements from a system perspective. 
</p>
<p>Furthermore, the capability can also be used to assess the impact of a technology to reduce mission risks. 
</p>
<p> The current study has focused on large landers. Future study will be expanded in four areas. First, the fidelity of 
</p>
<p>the technology assessment will be improved and will include additional figures of merits such as propellant usage, g-
</p>
<p>loads, and ride quality. Second, the simulation model will be expanded to include smaller landers such as Commercial 
</p>
<p>Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) landers. The study will explore the importance of PNT parameters and their thresholds 
</p>
<p>for smaller landers. Third, the simulation will be instrumented to assess the impact of PNT on Human Lander System 
</p>
<p>risk posture. Because the current simulation assessment approach was computationally intensive, the fourth area is to 
</p>
<p>continue the development of the NewSTEP rapid assessment capability to compliment the Monte Carlo analysis 
</p>
<p>approach. 
</p>
