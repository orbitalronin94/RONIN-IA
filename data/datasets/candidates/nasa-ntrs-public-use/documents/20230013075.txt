<p>2.d.8 Groundwater and terrestrial water storage—M. Rodell and D. N. Wiese 
</p>
<p>Terrestrial Water Storage (TWS; the sum of groundwater, soil moisture, surface water, snow, 
</p>
<p>and ice) reached 20-year highs or lows in several regions in 2022. Changes in mean annual TWS 
</p>
<p>between 2022 and 2021 are plotted in Plate 2.1 as equivalent heights of water (cm). Drought and 
</p>
<p>heat continued to affect southern Europe in 2022, with TWS reaching 20-year lows in the 
</p>
<p>Danube River basin and in the Alps. Drought in western Iran also worsened with another year of 
</p>
<p>large TWS losses (some &gt;9 cm). Above-normal precipitation provided some relief to western 
</p>
<p>Russia, but TWS remained below average. Large increases in TWS (some &gt;12 cm) were seen in 
</p>
<p>Southeast Asia, due in part to the heaviest rain in 60 years falling in southern China in June and 
</p>
<p>Typhoon Noru causing flooding in Vietnam and Laos in September (see section 7g for details). 
</p>
<p>TWS in central and southern India approached 20-year highs as well. Extreme drought struck the 
</p>
<p>Yangtze River basin in the summer of 2022, causing the river to reach record low levels and 
</p>
<p>severely depressing TWS in the region. Southeastern Australia had a very wet year, with 
</p>
<p>multiple episodes of extreme rain and flooding contributing to 20-year high TWS in the Murray-
</p>
<p>Darling River basin by the end of the year. Conversely, north-central Australia became notably 
</p>
<p>drier (section 7h4). Wet weather caused TWS increases in the northern portion of sub-Saharan 
</p>
<p>Africa and in South Africa, with the latter experiencing record-breaking rainfall in April. 
</p>
<p>Drought affected a large area of south-central Africa centered on Lake Tanganyika, diminishing 
</p>
<p>TWS, yet TWS remained above normal in almost all of sub-Saharan Africa. In North America, 
</p>
<p>dry weather caused TWS declines up and down the U.S. Central Plains, while previously 
</p>
<p>elevated TWS dropped closer to normal levels in the eastern United States. As a result, TWS in 
</p>
<p>the Mississippi River basin dropped to a 20-year low, as water levels in October in the lower 
</p>
<p>Mississippi River itself were the lowest since at least 1988. TWS in the southwestern United </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>States was nearly unchanged from the low levels in that region in 2021. TWS increased in south-
</p>
<p>central Canada and decreased in north-central Canada. In South America, eastern Brazil gained a 
</p>
<p>huge amount of water, exceeding 12 cm over a large area, while northern Brazil added to already 
</p>
<p>elevated TWS levels. Much of the western half of the continent’s TWS experienced declines due 
</p>
<p>to subpar rainfall.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figures 2.d.8.1 and 2.d.8.2 depict zonal mean and global mean TWS anomalies, respectively, 
</p>
<p>since April 2002 after removing the seasonal cycle. Data gaps occur during the interim between 
</p>
<p>the GRACE and GRACE-FO missions and when onboard instruments were shut down for 
</p>
<p>various reasons. In addition to excluding from these averages TWS declines associated with ice 
</p>
<p>sheet and glacier losses in Antarctica, Greenland, the Gulf Coast of Alaska, and polar islands (as 
</p>
<p>per Rodell and Wiese 2022), more areas where glacier ablation caused long-term trends in High 
</p>
<p>Mountain Asia, western Canada, and in the southern Andes were also excluded this year. Most 
</p>
<p>notably, 2022 witnessed intensification of both a dry zone between about 25°N and 45°N and a 
</p>
<p>wet zone between about 8°S and 15°N (Fig. 2.d.8.1). The former is attributable to TWS declines 
</p>
<p>in the central and eastern United States, southern Europe, the Caspian Sea and adjacent lands, 
</p>
<p>and eastern China. The latter was caused by TWS gains in eastern Brazil, northern sub-Saharan 
</p>
<p>Africa, southern India, and southeastern Asia. South of that zone of wetness, dryness that began 
</p>
<p>in 2019 or earlier appears to be abating, also owing to TWS gains in eastern Brazil. At the global 
</p>
<p>scale (Fig. 2.d.8.2), TWS, excluding ice sheets and glaciers, fluctuated by about 1 cm throughout 
</p>
<p>the year, remaining stable with respect to 2021 and somewhat low overall. 
</p>
<p> </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>TWS is a useful indicator of hydroclimatic variability because it reflects the integrated effects of 
</p>
<p>weather over months to years. Groundwater and terrestrial water storage are not well monitored 
</p>
<p>at regional and larger scales using conventional approaches, but the Gravity Recovery and 
</p>
<p>Climate Experiment (GRACE) and GRACE Follow-On (GRACE-FO) satellite missions have 
</p>
<p>provided global, monthly time series of TWS anomalies (departures from the long-term mean) 
</p>
<p>since 2002 (Tapley et al. 2004; Landerer et al. 2020). On a monthly scale, uncertainties are 
</p>
<p>typically around 1 cm–2 cm equivalent height of water over a 500,000 km2 region at midlatitudes 
</p>
<p>(Wiese et al. 2016). Groundwater typically dominates the interannual variations in TWS except 
</p>
<p>in the wet tropics (dominated by surface water) and high latitude and alpine regions (dominated 
</p>
<p>by ice and snow; Getirana et al. 2017). 
</p>
