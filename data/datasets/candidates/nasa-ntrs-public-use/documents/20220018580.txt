



































Assessment of Mars 2020 Forebody Heating Predictions with
Coupled Material Response

Thomas K. West IV∗ and Christopher O. Johnston†

NASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, VA, 23681

The objective of this work was to assess the impact of flow field - material response cou-
pling on reproducing temperatures within the Mars 2020 forebody thermal protection system.
Both equilibrium and nonequilibrium coupled material response approaches were considered.
Turbulent transition estimates were included in the flow field modeling based on transition
times inferred from flight measurements. A coupled equilibrium ablation model was found
to overpredict the in-depth temperatures near the surface by over 100 K in the stagnation
region and by about 200 K near the leeside shoulder, which was subject to turbulent heating
augmentation. For nonequilibrium ablation, two finite rate surface chemistry models were
used to model the chemical process on the vehicle surface. The first was a model consisting
of N2 catalysis and CO2 recombination model, and the second was a model consisting of only
N2 and O2 catalysis. Near the stagnation point, the N2 and CO2 model was found to be within
about 10 K from the peak in-depth temperature near the surface; however, overprediction
of the in-depth temperatures was observed early in the trajectory, similar to the equilibrium
ablation model. The N2 and O2 catalysis model was found to significantly underpredict near
surface in-depth temperatures.

I. Introduction
Designing and sizing thermal protection systems (TPS) on entry vehicles historically employs the assumption

that flowfield heating predictions and material response analyses are uncoupled. This assumption typically yields
a conservative (high) estimate of the in-depth heating resulting in a thicker TPS being required to satisfy bondline
temperature constraints. For an uncoupled analysis, there is no explicit impact of the material response on the flow field,
including blowing of pyrolysis and char gases into the boundary layer. Additionally, the wall temperature is not fed
back to the flow field, which impacts the predicted convective heat rate. Instead, an approximate blowing correction is
applied to the non-ablating convective heating solutions to account for a reduction in heating due to blowing from the
surface. While assuming an uncoupled material response typically leads to a more reliable TPS, the trade-off is that, in
many cases, the TPS is over-designed which translates to a vehicle weight penalty. Aside from design, comparison
of simulation tools with flight data requires an accurate modeling of the physics without simplifying or conservative
assumptions. Otherwise, a true assessment of model predictive capabilities cannot be accurately determined.

The Mars 2020 vehicle was instrumented with the Mars Entry, Descent, and Landing Instrumentation 2 (MEDLI2)
sensor package, which included in-depth thermocouples (TCs), pressure transducers, heat flux gauges, and a radiometer
distributed on both the forebody and backshell of the vehicle. Instrumentation on the forebody include MEDLI2
Instrumented Sensor plugs (MISP) and MEDLI2 Entry Atmospheric Data System (MEADS) suite of pressure transducers.
The layout of the sensors is shown in Fig. 1. Each MISP on the forebody is identified as MEDLI2 Thermal on Heatshield
(MTH), and each MEADS pressure transducer on the forebody is labeled as MEDLI2 Pressure on Heatshield (MPH).
Further details of the MEDLI2 instrumentation and measured flight data are given by White et al. [1].

The MEDLI2 flight data provides an opportunity to measure predictive capabilities and assess the impact of modeling
key physical phenomenon. Previous work by Johnston et al. [2] showed the impact of modeling a coupled ablation
wall boundary on a Mars 2020 like vehicle surface. While the condition studied in that previous work was at a higher
energy than what was experienced by Mars 2020, it showed that convective heating and the surface char rate were a
factor of two lower than an uncoupled ablation model. In the present study, the primary objective is to investigate the
impact of modeling the Mars 2020 TPS performance with a coupled flow field and material response analysis. Both
equilibrium and finite-rate char models are assessed in this coupled material response approach. A comparison is made
to uncoupled material response predictions, which were used for design and sizing of the flown TPS. To-date, this work

II. Flow Field and Material Response Models
In this section, details of the geometry and computational grid are provided. Then, a description of the material

response model and surface chemistry models considered is provided.

A. Geometry, Computational Domain, and Flight Trajectory
The Mars 2020 forebody was a 4.5 meter diameter, 70 degree sphere-cone. The computational domain was

three-dimensional with symmetry along the centerline as side-slip angles are assumed to be negligibly small. The
forebody grid was decomposed into 26, 11 x 11 blocks with 128 cells normal to the wall. Final grids for each trajectory
point were shock-aligned with 80% of the grid normal to the wall lying inside the shock layer. Wall normal grid
spacing was achieved via grid adaption with a target cell Reynolds number of 1.0. Further details of the geometry and
computational domain are given by Wise et al. [3] Thirteen trajectory points were chosen to model the continuum
portion of the vehicle entry. These cases are listed in Table 1. These conditions are taken from the latest best estimated
trajectory based on post-flight reconstruction of the entry [4].

B. Computational Fluid Dynamics and Material Response Models

software, which is a finite-volume, Navier-Stokes flow solver [5]. LAURA uses a second-order, upwind discretization

the inviscid flux. Flow fields were assumed to be steady state, and the boundary layer was modeled as laminar until a
time in which transition was observed in the MEDLI2 data. As accurate modeling of transition is beyond the scope of
the present study, transition times and streamwise locations are taken from Tang et al. [6] and are listed in Table 2.

was modeled just upstream of MTH02. Then, transition was set just upstream of MTH03 in the 76.76 sec case. Both
of these locations were chosen to be the TPS tile seams upstream of each MTH plug as these seams are believed to
have a propensity to trip the boundary layer. Finally, turbulent flow was modeled from the stagnation point in 82.00

The gas properties were modeled with a thermochemical nonequilibrium assumption using the two temperature

absorption are negligible at the conditions experienced by Mars 2020 [3]. A ray-tracing approach [14] was applied for
computing the radiative heating at the wall, while the tangent-slab approach was used to compute the divergence of the
radiative flux.

The elemental mass balance at the surface is solved algebraically by assuming the mass transfer coefficient is equal to
the heat transfer coefficient. This is often referred to as the 𝐵′ approach in practice.

For a coupled material response analysis, there are two approaches to achieve the coupling: equilibrium ablation and
a nonequilibrium ablation model. With equilibrium ablation, the same initial process is conducted as for the uncoupled
case, but now the solutions are restarted with the new wall temperatures and blowing rates. The flow field solution is then
reconverged using these updated boundary conditions. Subsequent updates of the wall temperature and blowing rates
are achieved by rerunning the material response analysis and forcing equilibrium chemistry on the surface between the
pyrolysis gases, char gases, and boundary layer edge species. With the flow field and material response coupled, species
are treated explicitly in the flow field and are allowed to diffuse through the boundary layer. Therefore, the blowing
correction in Eq. (4) is no longer used. This process is repeated for each trajectory point until the wall temperature,
blowing rates and heat fluxes are converged.

For nonequilibrium ablation modeling, a surface reaction rate model is used for finite rate catalysis and surface
chemistry that result in char blowing from the surface. The material response model returns the pyrolysis rate and gas
composition, along with the heat conduction rate needed for the surface energy balance. Note that here, the pyrolysis gas
is assumed to be in equilibrium before reaching the surface. The flow field is then converged while an energy balance at
the wall is iteratively solved to update the wall temperature. Similar to the equilibrium ablation approach, the process of
updating the material response is repeated until the wall temperature, blowing rates and heat fluxes are converged. More
details on the solution procedures and the mathematical formulations of coupled material response modeling are left to
literature [2, 15, 17].

C. TPS Material Model and Surface Chemistry Models
The heatshield of Mars 2020 was composed primarily of tiled Phenolic Impregnated Carbon Ablator (PICA). During

manufacturing and assembly of the spacecraft, the heatshield was sprayed with a silicon-based coating called NuSil
to control dust from the PICA heatshield. This same process was used for Mars Science Laboratory (MSL). NuSil
was believed to have a negligible impact on the heatshield performance but was actually found to alter the material
response significantly. No recession of the MSL heatshield was observed in the flight data as none of the TCs burned

4



out, which was expected to occur as a result of recession. The same absence of recession was seen for Mars 2020 [1],
even though MEDLI2 had TCs that were less than two millimeters from the surface. Later in section III, both PICA and
NuSil coated PICA (PICA-N) were investigated to show the impact of the NuSil coating on in-depth material response
performance. Note that the room-temperature-vulcanizing (RTV) silicone material between the tiles is not modeled in
this work. Other than its potential impact on boundary-layer transition, this is expected to have minimal impact on
heating at the TC-plug locations. Details of the behavior and surface chemistry of NuSil on the surface of PICA in a
Mars entry environment are still being investigated. Similar to the flight data, limited arc-jet test data showed limited or
no recession of NuSil coated test articles. In the absence of a detailed surface chemistry model, the char blowing rate
was assumed negligible throughout this work. Therefore, in the cases of equilibrium ablation, only the pyrolysis gases
and boundary layer edge species are equilibrated on the surface.

III. Uncoupled Material Response
The typical design approach for a TPS is to use an uncoupled material response analysis where the fluid dynamics

are solved independently of the material model. After obtaining converged flow field solutions at each trajectory point
listed in Table 1, an uncoupled material response prediction of the in-depth temperatures was performed with the
blowing correction in Eq.(4) applied to surface convective heating. Resulting temperature profiles versus time are
shown in Fig. 2 at the MTH01 (near the stagnation point) location in comparison to the measured flight data. To show
the impact of assuming no char blowing with the coating, an uncoated PICA model was also employed. Notice first that
the bare PICA material model yields a poor comparison near the surface (TC-1), underpredicting the peak temperature
by over 100 K. The temperature is also noticeably offset from the flight data in time. This is due to excess char blowing
from the surface. At the deepest TC location, TC-3, however, the agreement is much better as the temperature tracks
only a few degrees from the measured data. Note also that with char blowing, recession of the TPS would occur, which
was not modeled here. However, little or no recession was observed in the flight data, so the bare PICA model is not an
adequate representation of the TPS.

The PICA-N model offered an improved prediction of the near surface temperature compared to bare PICA. Peak
temperatures are overpredicted by about 70 K and was much more aligned with the flight data peak temperature time.
Moving down into the material, the PICA-N model begins to disagree more with the flight measurements compared to
the PICA model. From here forward in this work, only the zero char, PICA-N material model is used.

Moving down the forebody, towards the shoulder to MTH02, the heatshield was subject to turbulent heating, which
was observed in the flight data. In the case of MTH02, Tang et al. [6] deduced that the boundary layer transitioned
at 71.4s after entry interface. Here, instantaneous transition of the boundary layer was modeled from the PICA tile
seam upstream of MTH02 starting from the 73.99s trajectory point. Compared to the stagnation region, a similar
overprediction of the in-depth temperature was observed and is shown in Fig. 3. However, now the equilibrium ablation
model overshoots the peak near-surface temperature by about 200 K. Notice that when turbulent flow is switched on, the
same gradient change in the in-depth heating is observed, as in the flight data. Figure 3 also includes an analysis with
fully laminar flow throughout the trajectory which results in obvious underprediction of the near surface heating. This
highlights the importance of modeling turbulence. Note that modeling of transition is subject to significant model-form
uncertainty. The transition location used here is based on engineering judgment and is subject to future analysis and
investigation.

In the case of both MTH01 and MTH02, as well as the other TC locations, there was a noticeable underprediction of
the in-depth temperatures early in the trajectory, followed by an overshoot between the 42.69s and 60.61s trajectory
points. With an uncoupled material response (and equilibrium ablation discussed in the next section), equilibrium
chemistry is assumed between the boundary layer edge species and the pyrolysis gases (char gases as well, but that
is assumed zero here). At the 42.69s trajectory point, CO2 nearly fully dissociates on the windside forebody. An
equilibrium chemistry model at the wall yields an almost full recovery of the freestream CO2 mass fraction which causes

7



much higher temperatures compared to a less reactive wall. This phenomenon was observed on Orion, which is exposed
to far less CO2 constituents than Mars entry vehicles.

The impact of equilibrium was also observed by comparing the temperature overshoot between MTH01 and MTH02.
Near the shoulder, the actual surface chemistry is further from equilibrium compared to the stagnation point where
the surface may be closer to equilibrium. This leads to the larger disagreement between the model and flight data at
MTH02. Given the low pressures and temperatures early in the trajectory, an assumption of equilibrium on the surface
is likely poor.

IV. Coupled Material Response
In this section, coupled material response modeling is compared to the MEDLI2 flight data. First, the equilibrium

ablation approach is assessed. Then, nonequilibrium ablation with finite rate surface chemistry using the two different
rate models are compared to the measured in-depth TC data.

A. Equilibrium Ablation
In the case of equilibrium ablation, ablation products are treated explicitly in the flow field solution. Therefore, the

blowing correction applied to the heat transfer coefficient is no longer needed. Species mass fractions on the vehicle
surface are determined from an equilibrium analysis of the boundary layer edge species and pyrolysis gases. Species are
then allowed to diffuse through the boundary layer. A comparison of the equilibrium ablation and uncoupled ablation
in-depth temperatures is shown in Fig. 4 at the MTH01 location.

Fig. 4 MTH01 measurements compared to uncoupled material response and equilibrium ablation.

Notice that the two ablation approaches yield similar results. There is only a slight temperature difference of about
10 K. This result is in contrast to previous work by Johnston [2] where the difference between coupled and uncoupled
ablation was shown to be significant. However, the primary difference here is the assumption of no char blowing due to
the NuSil coating. This drastically reduces blowing at the surface. With less blowing, there are fewer species to diffuse
between the wall and the boundary layer, which is a key assumption of the uncoupled approach. Overall, the blowing
correction to the uncoupled cases was found to be a reasonable approximation of equilibrium ablation for Mars 2020.

A summary of the predicted in-depth temperatures compared to the MEDLI2 flight data is shown in Fig. 5. The
results in this figure include the imposed transition locations from Table 2. With equilibrium ablation, agreement
with the flight data was improved closer to the stagnation region. MTH01, MTH03, MTH09, and MTH11 yield the
best comparison. The agreement is seen to degrade away from the stagnation region largely due to the heating being
overpredicted early in the trajectory as a result of the surface chemistry being further from equilibrium.

was not unexpected because the Mitcheltree model enforces a high-level of CO2 formation at the wall, which is a highly

missing mechanisms in the surface chemistry model. Early in the trajectory, there is too much heating, followed by an
underprediction in the heating after peak convective heating at 80s. The N2 and O2 catalysis model does well early

models through the trajectory may yield a more agreeable comparison in time. One or both of these models may also
be calibrated to match the flight data. However, both of these possible approaches to improve the agreement with the
MEDLI2 flight data are currently not known to reproduce the true surface chemistry. The above result indicates that a
detailed surface chemistry model is needed to resolve the discrepancy with the flight data.

A note should be made about the material response before 60s. Regardless of coupling approach, the 42s case
consistently underpredicts the in-depth temperature. In the current study, non-continuum heating was not considered.
Additionally, complex processes that occur during initial heating, such as H2O vaporization and likely crystallization of
the silicone surface, are also not considered. However, ignoring these phenomena were found to have little impact on
the in-depth temperature predictions at later times. This is shown in Fig. 7.

Here, the original line includes the 42.69s case in the material response. The approximate line does not include
this point, and an exponential approximation to the surface heating is used to model heating before the first analyzed
trajectory point at 60.61s. The approximation results in an improved agreement prior to 60s in the trajectory; however,
the temperatures afterwards are only 10-15 K higher than the original predictions. While correctly modeling these early
processes is necessary to reduce overall uncertainty, they do not have a significant impact on the in-depth temperature
profiles for Mars 2020.

V. Conclusions
Using an equilibrium ablation model, significant overprediction of the heating was observed. This was found to be a

result of excess heating early in the trajectory during Mars 2020 entry into Mars. To alleviate the chemical equilibrium
assumption on the vehicle surface, two nonequilibrium rate models were considered. A rate model consisting of N2
catalysis and CO2 recombination yielded an improved agreement over the equilibrium ablation model, though an
overprediction of the heating early in the entry was still observed. A rate model with only N2 and O2 catalysis was
found to underpredict in-depth temperatures compared to the measured flight data. A detailed uncertainty analysis of
