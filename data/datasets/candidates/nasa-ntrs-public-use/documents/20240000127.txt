<p>Fiber Bragg Grating (FBG) as sensor
</p>
<p>Principle
• Fiber Reflector that reflects a 
</p>
<p>particular wavelength and 
transmit all others
</p>
<p>• Bragg Wavelength: 𝜆𝜆𝐵𝐵=2𝑛𝑛𝑒𝑒Λ
</p>
<p>Measuring Strain(ε) or 
Temperature (∆T) via FBG sensor
</p>
<p>∆𝜆𝜆𝐵𝐵
𝜆𝜆𝐵𝐵
</p>
<p>= 1 − 𝑝𝑝𝑒𝑒 𝜀𝜀 + (𝛼𝛼Λ + 𝛼𝛼𝑛𝑛)Δ𝑇𝑇
</p>
<p>• Δ𝜆𝜆𝐵𝐵= change in Bragg wavelength due 
to environmental change
</p>
<p>• 𝜆𝜆𝐵𝐵= Initial Bragg wavelength of FBG
• 𝑝𝑝𝑒𝑒= strain-optics coefficient
• 𝛼𝛼Λ = Thermal expansion coefficient
• 𝛼𝛼𝑛𝑛 = thermo-optic coefficient</p>

<p>NASA’s Unique FBG Interrogation Technique: OFDR
</p>
<p>6
</p>
<p>• Optical Frequency Domain 
Reflectometry (OFDR): 
</p>
<p>• Based on laser interferometry
• Single Longitudinal mode laser needed
</p>
<p>• Involves signal processing
• Fourier Transform/inverse Fourier 
</p>
<p>Transform
• Use weak reflectivity FBG
</p>
<p>• Typical WDM FBG’s R=80%
• Typical OFDR FBG’s R=0.05%
</p>
<p>• So why use OFDR for sensing 
instead?
</p>
<p>• Thousands of sensors in 1 single fiber
• High spatial density (sensor every ¼” 
</p>
<p>increment), see LOFTID results.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Optical Frequency Domain Reflectometry 
Principle
• Combine 2 coherent waves to generate a beat 
</p>
<p>frequency
• This is an unique beat frequency based on the length 
</p>
<p>difference ∆L
</p>
<p>• Multiple sensors with unique beat frequencies 
(∆Lfbg) are captured
</p>
<p>• In Fourier Domain each sensor with unique 
frequency is separated, and iFFT to obtain its design 
wavelength (𝜆𝜆𝐵𝐵)
</p>
<p>7
</p>
<p>• All FBGs are written at the same wavelength 
(λB), instead of each having a unique 
wavelength (WDM)
</p>
<p>– Multiplexing of hundreds of sensor in single fiber
</p>
<p>• A narrowband wavelength tunable laser 
source is used to interrogate multiple sensors.
</p>
<p>• Each FBG sensor is only ½ inch long
</p>
<p>Combined / 
Modulated light with 
beat frequency ∆L</p>

<p>LOFTID 
Background
</p>
<p>A New Kind of Heat Shield 
</p>
<p>• After more than a decade of 
development of Hypersonic 
Inflatable Aerodynamic 
Decelerator (HIAD) technology, 
including two suborbital flight 
tests, the LOFTID orbital flight test 
is the next step. This return from 
orbit demonstration provides an 
entry environment relevant to 
many potential applications, 
paving the way for its use on 
future missions. The LOFTID re-
entry vehicle, at 19.7 feet (6 
meters) diameter, is the largest 
blunt body aeroshell to ever go 
through atmospheric entry.</p>
<p></p>
<div class="annotation"><div class="annotationTitle">Presenter</div>
<div class="annotationSubject">Presentation Notes</div>
<div class="annotationContents">The goal of the LOFTID project is to test a new type of inflatable heat shield that could one day be used to safely enter payloads into Earth’s atmosphere or any destination with an atmosphere, from space. The inflatable decelerator is made up of a flexible heat shield that can be folded up and stored inside a spacecraft during launch. Once the spacecraft reaches the atmosphere, the heat shield is inflated to slow down the vehicle and protect it from the intense heat of re-entry. LOFTID is part of the Technology Demonstration Missions (TDM) under the Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD).On November 10, 2022, the LOFTID reentry vehicle was launched at Vandenberg Space Force Base as a secondary payload with the Joint Polar Satellite System-2 (JPSS-2), which is a weather satellite in polar orbit, using a United Launch Alliance Atlas V. Once JPSS-2 reached orbit, LOFTID was put on a reentry trajectory from low-Earth orbit to demonstrate the inflatable aeroshell capability to decelerate and withstand re-entry temperatures. At a speed of 18,000 mph, the vehicle entered earth’s atmosphere and the aeroshell was able to decelerate it to 80 mph at which point onboard parachutes were deployed, providing a smooth splashdown in the Pacific Ocean.</div>
</div>

<div class="annotation"><div class="annotationTitle">Presenter</div>
<div class="annotationSubject">Presentation Notes</div>
<div class="annotationContents">Armstrong Flight Research Center (AFRC) was approached to provide FOSS high spatial density temperature measurements in three strategic locations on the vehicle. FOSS provided 1,110 measurands just behind the nose cone, 114 measurands along the side of the vehicle, and 193 measurands on an internal structure. The main measurement of interest for the LOFTID project was just behind the nose cone. At this location, AFRC FOSS technology was able to measure temperature data in a decaying spiral pattern (23ft long) with a spatial density of 0.25”, with measurements taken at 20 samples per second. This data was recorded locally onboard the Armstrong system as well as transmitted to LOFTID’s avionics data recorder via ethernet. FOSS successfully captured data throughout the entire reentry profile, splashdown, and during vehicle recovery.</div>

<p>Conclusion
• The ruggedized FOSS system successfully acquired multiple 
</p>
<p>measurements contiguously at 20 samples per second 
</p>
<p>• The fiber placed behind the FTPS rigid aeroshell provided 1,110 
temperature data. 
</p>
<p>• On the rigid aeroshell, co-located FOSS FBGs and thermocouples 
were in agreement with reasonable tolerance for difference in 
locational protections. 
</p>
<p>• Fibers from the mid and aft sections of the payload confirmed 
that the overall temperature is steady, proving the effectiveness 
of the heat shield.  
</p>
<p>• The system has seawater residue from the splashdown but 
proved to be fully operational due to its hermetic design.
</p>
<p>• PS – FOSS system still functions as of today!</p>
