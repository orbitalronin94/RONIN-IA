NASA HR-1: A HEE Resistant Fe-Ni Superalloy


Hydrogen Environment Embrittlement (HEE) is the degradation of mechanical properties while a material is under stress in a gaseous hydrogen environment 


Material susceptibility influenced by alloy composition and microstructure


NASA HR-1 derived from JBK-75 and designed to be hydrogen resistant


Developed at NASA MSFC M&P Lab in 1990-1992


NASA HR-1 is 15 - 25% stronger than JBK-75


Higher Ni/Ti, additions of Co & W, lead to higher strength 


W retards  precipitation and increase HEE resistance 


It has good weldability, castability, and cold-workability


NASA HR-1 was identified as a candidate material for rocket engine applications operating in extreme H2 pressures


Requirements include yield strength, ductility, low cycle fatigue life, thermal conductivity, and HEE resistance


Additive manufacturing has enabled the use of NASA HR-1 for rocket engine applications

NASA HR-1 Composition Optimization for AM


Initial adjustments to NASA HR-1 composition were made to attempt to make the material more suitable to AM


Changes from the wrought composition are expected to lead to drops in strengths


During the early characterization, it was observed that the LP-DED process promoted significant Ti segregation and grain boundary -phase precipitation more than observed in wrought


-phase is detrimental to the ductility of the material as well as to the hydrogen environment embrittlement (HEE) resistance of the material


Composition of NASA HR-1 was modified using PHACOMP analysis to help avoid -phase formation in the AM material


Adjustments in composition are expected to drop the yield strength of the material while improving ductility and LCF life.


HEE resistance and thermal conductivity are expected to remain similar or slightly improve

As-Built Microstructure as a Function of Laser Power


Different laser powers were examined in the as-built condition. Single-pass walls at the 350W laser power was examined for comparison to thin wall applications. 2620W parameter examined for faster deposition for large bulk parts





With increasing laser power, as-built grain size, gas porosity, pore size all tend to increase





2620W exhibits very large pores, particularly near the top, and some micro-cracks have been observed near the top layer. Pore sizes were much smaller and no micro-cracking was observed in lower laser powers

Challenges for DED NASA HR-1 development: Chemical and microstructural inhomogeneities 


The as-deposited alloys exhibit varying degree of chemical segregation and microstructural gradient. 


Wrought alloys can be optimized by hot/cold rolling (or forging) and heat treatments to remove chemical segregation and microstructural inhomogeneity, but the microstructure of AM-materials can only be optimized by heat treatment


Therefore, development of appropriate heat treatment is critical to the success of AM alloy development. 


Development for LP-DED NASA HR-1 aimed to optimize the heat treatment for microstructure and practicality

Mitigation of -phase Through Solution 
and Aging Treatments


A time-temperature-precipitation (TTP) diagram was developed for -phase in both initial composition and HT, and checked after composition and HT optimization


The standard 718C/16h aging treatment was unable to reduce the brittle -phase to an acceptable level for LP-DED NASA HR-1. Reduction of temperature to 704°C or 690°C with two step aging cycles were examined to avoid -phase formation


Minimal -phase formation was observed with the two step aging cycles that were developed and no grain boundary -phase was observed to be continuous around the grains


690°C/16h furnace cooled to 648°C/16h or to 621°C/16h were examined


While two step aging cycles minimized -phase formation, a moderate drop in yield strength (5-15%) was expected to result

LP-DED NASA HR-1 Tensile Results


All the samples were 1070W round bars subjected to the same stress relief, homogenization, and solution treatment previously baselined


Two aging cycles were tensile tested in temperatures ranging from -195°C to 982°C: 718°C/16h (1-Step) and 690°C/16h furnace cooled to 621°C/16h (2-Step)


The two sets of data had similar trends to each other and to the wrought material with the tensile strengths significantly dropping around 648°C which is considered the maximum recommended operating temperature for the alloy


Powder reuse was examined at RT for 1x and 2x reused powder and at 648°C for 1x reused powder. No significant effects of powder reuse were observed











12











LP-DED NASA HR-1 Parameter Study Tensile


A test series looking at the effects of different LP-DED parameters was done in collaboration with Auburn University


Samples were heat treated using the thermal cycles developed at NASA MSFC with a 2-Step Age


The 350W parameter set was observed to have slightly higher strengths due to the finer grain size and a slightly lower average fracture elongation


All other parameter sets were consistent with one another in tensile strengths


Similar trends have been observed for 625 and JBK-75

LP-DED NASA HR-1 LCF Results


LCF tests at 1% and 2% total strain and R=-1 were performed at temperatures ranging from -195°C (LN2) to 760°C


The 2-step aging cycle tended to have improved fatigue life over the 1-step age at most temperatures tested for both strain ranges, however the fatigue life converged more above 648°C


No significant impact of 1x or 2x powder reuse on LCF properties was observed for any temperatures tested


At room temp, both aging cycles had similar LCF behavior to wrought NASA HR-1 in GHe (historical data)








14

















GH2 Tensile Results


A case study in high pressure hydrogen tensile testing examined effects of surface finish and strain rates


Strain rates varied from 0.005-0.0001 in/in/min and surface finish between machined and LSG per ASTM G142


GH2 testing performed at 5000 psi at room temp and compared to GN2, GHe, and Air tests


A minor drop in ductility was consistently observed for 2-step age while a more notable drop ~7% was observed for 1-step age


1-Step age drop was attributed to test time due to slow strain rate at 0.0005 in/in/min


No significant drops in strength noted in either aging conditions


Fractography showed GH2 affected zones with quasi-cleavage near surface that increase in area with slower strain rates

Low Cycle Fatigue in High Pressure GH2


LCF testing in 34 MPa GH2 was performed on LP-DED NASA HR-1 at 2% strain range and R=-1
 


For both aging cycles, the LCF life was comparable in GH2 at 2% strain and was much higher than the wrought material in GH2
 


The LP-DED material has similar LCF life to wrought in 34 MPa GHe 
 


Powder reuse was also shown to not have an impact at 1x and 2x reuses
 


Both aging cycles achieved LCF life generally within scatter of the data in air at room temperature


Average cycles to failure around 800-1000 cycles for both aging cycles in GH2

Summary and Future Work


Desirable, consistent microstructures have been shown to be achievable and repeatable in LP-DED NASA HR-1 and the issues of Ti segregation and η-phase precipitation mitigated





LP-DED NASA HR-1 has been shown to have similar trends in strength and ductility to wrought, but strengths were lower and ductility was higher for LP-DED material


LCF behavior is comparable to the wrought material in air and LP-DED NASA HR-1 has a much smaller debit in LCF life in GH2 compared to wrought


Further testing to round out GH2 LCF testing possible for future





LP-DED NASA HR-1 has been proven to have excellent resistance to HEE in high pressure GH2 up to 34 MPa and a good candidate alloy for several liquid rocket engine applications





L-PBF NASA HR-1 still in development at NASA MSFC


Region of good parameters establish


Evaluating process sensitivities and mechanical properties
