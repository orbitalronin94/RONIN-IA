During United States Extravehicular Activity 80 (US EVA 80), water was observed in the 



helmet of an Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU) during cabin repressurization. Through a 



comparative analysis and Test, Teardown, and Evaluation (TT&E) of this EMU, the most 



likely cause of the US EVA 80 water failure was determined to be sublimator carryover caused 



by a comparatively high latent load (water vapor production by the crewmember). High latent 



load can be reduced by the crewmember adjusting the thermal control valve (TCV) setting on 



the EMU to prevent the onset of significant sweating. To reduce the risk of high latent load 



leading to water in the helmet, a potential warning system was developed using the Systems 



Improved Numerical Differencing Analyzer EMU model (SINDA EMU). The goal of this 



warning system is to alert the crewmember when they are producing a high latent load and 



recommend adjustment of the TCV to increase cooling. This warning system was designed to 



ensure high risk conditions are avoided while simultaneously preventing a system that warns 



the crewmember too frequently. Theoretical tests of the warning system through modeling 



calculated that if the warning system were used during US EVA 80, the total latent load could 



have been reduced by up to 33% which would have significantly reduced the risk of water in 



the helmet. This analysis also investigated the effectiveness of the warning system on EVAs 



that occurred after US EVA 80.

I. Introduction & Background 



 



URING the repressurization phase at the end of United States Extravehicular Activity 80 (US EVA 80) on 23 



March 2022, liquid water entered the helmet of the Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU) of Extravehicular Crew 



2 (EV2). The volume of liquid water was estimated to be between 30-50 cm3. This volume of water covered the front 



of the helmet visor, potentially disrupting the crewmember’s ability to see clearly. Had this volume of water entered 



the helmet while outside of the crewlock, it would cause significant risk to the mission and crewmember. For this 



reason, an investigation into the cause of water in the helmet during US EVA 80 was conducted. This investigation 



also investigated potential risk mitigation methods that could be implemented. 



The investigation determined that the most likely cause of water in the helmet during US EVA 80 was integrated 



performance induced sublimator carryover caused by a comparatively high latent load. Figure I-1 shows a simplified 



schematic of the ventilation loop to help explain sublimator carryover. During an EVA, the crewmember adds water 



vapor to the ventilation loop through respiration, diffusion through the skin, and sweat evaporation. The latent heat 



added to the ventilation loop through the evaporation of water is also called the latent load. The humidity added to 



the gas by the crewmember leaves the suit and enters the Portable Life Support System (PLSS). In the PLSS, the gas 



passes through a sublimator which cools the gas, causing liquid water to condense out. The sublimator contains small 



holes called slurper holes, which were designed to remove the liquid water from the sublimator. However, if the slurper 



holes aren’t able to remove all of the liquid water, some water may move downstream of the sublimator towards the 



helmet. This liquid water is called sublimator carryover. 



The amount of sublimator carryover depends on the latent load and the efficiency of the sublimator’s ability to 



remove liquid water. Through a comparative assessment of historical EVAs, it was determined that US EVA 80 had 



a high latent load compared to previous EVAs which did not present water in the helmet1. The comparative assessment 



also found that other historical EVAs with high latent loads often presented some form of sublimator carryover. For 



this reason, it was determined that the water in the helmet during US EVA 80 was most likely due to sublimator 



carryover caused by an elevated latent load. Further evidence from the Test, Teardown, & Evaluation (TT&E) of the 



EMU confirmed that there were no individual components performing below specification, which implies that the 



water was caused by the integrated performance of the EMU rather than an individual component failure. Latent load 



is an important factor when it comes to the risk of water in the helmet since a high latent load results in an increased 



likelihood of sublimator carryover that may eventually enter the helmet. 



 



 
Figure I-1: Simplified Diagram of Interactions Between Crew and Ventilation Loop 



The simplest way to reduce the latent load experienced during an EVA is to reduce the sweat produced by the 



crewmember. When the temperature of the body increases, it produces sweat to remove the excess heat that is stored 



by the crewmember. Increased sweating results in increased evaporation into the gas and a higher latent load. Figure 



I-2 shows the results of a parametric analytical study on the impact of crewmember heat storage (measurement of 



deviations in body temperature from thermal neutrality) on total latent load generated during a 7-hour EVA1. At 



negative and neutral heat storage levels, the latent load does not depend significantly on heat storage. This is because 



at negative heat storage levels, the body is not producing significant amounts of sweat. Once the crewmember heats 



up into the positive heat storage range, the latent load begins to increase rapidly due to the initiation of sweating. 



The thermal comfort of the crewmember is maintained by the transport loop of the PLSS which flows cooling 



water through the Liquid Cooling and Ventilation Garment (LCVG) of the crewmember. The crewmember adjusts

the risk of water in the helmet and determine if mitigations taken by the crewmember in response to a high latent load 



are effective. This analysis will also assess the performance of the warning system on EVAs conducted since US EVA

II. Model Description 



The Systems Improved Numerical Differencing Analyzer 



EMU (SINDA EMU) model was used to calculate the latent 



load for numerous EVAs. SINDA EMU models the 



crewmember and PLSS, connecting the two subsystems 



through flow of gas and cooling water in and out of the suit. 



SINDA EMU models all flow paths through the PLSS, which 



allows it to accurately calculate the temperature of the water, 



temperature of the gas, and dew point of the gas. The PLSS 



model accurately captures the impact of the crewmember 



adjusting the TCV by modeling the various flow paths through 



the PLSS. The crewmember side of the model accounts for the 



metabolism of the crew, sweat generation, and heat transfer to 



the LCVG and through the suit walls. This model allows for 



the conditions of US EVA 80 (and any other EVA) to be 



modeled. Additionally, it allows for theoretical changes to 



EVAs, such as adjusting the TCV, to be modeled. SINDA EMU was initially developed in the 1970s2,3, and has since 



been improved and re-correlated through improvement of the crewmember modeling4, re-correlation to EMU

A. Model Inputs and Assumptions 



SINDA EMU uses inputs from flight data, console logs, and thermal modeling to accurately model an EVA. The 



flight data is used to determine the metabolic rate, suit and environment pressure, and temperature of water exiting 



the sublimator. The metabolic rate (metrate) is determined by calculating the rate of decrease in the pressure of the 



oxygen supply tank and then calculating the metrate based on the rate of oxygen consumption4. The console logs are 



used to determine the TCV setting for the duration of the EVA. TCV setting affects the flowrate of water to the 



sublimator which impacts the amount of cooling received by the crewmember. The flow split caused by TCV setting 



varies by specific TCV, so the fleetwide average flowrate was used. Thermal modeling is used to determine the 



environmental sink temperature which impacts the heat leak through the EMU. Environment thermal modeling 



identifies the sink temperature for eight different orbit positions, with the model choosing the orbit position that best 



aligns with the actual timeline of the EVA. Finally, the model assumes the skin area and sweat rates based on average 



body size and sweat generation rates4. The mass of the crewmember is input into the model, and the average skin area 



for a crewmember of that mass is used. 



III. Proposed Warning System 



For the causal investigation into the US EVA 80 water in the helmet event, SINDA EMU was used to calculate 



the latent load for numerous historical EVAs and tests in Chamber B at Johnson Space Center (JSC)1. This analysis 



indicated that EVAs with high latent loads are more likely to present some amount of sublimator carryover (either 



water in the helmet or a CO2 sensor latching, which indicates water downstream of the sublimator). Examining the 



latent load for all modeled EVAs showed a clear split between EVAs with and without sublimator carryover. Every 



EVA that had a calculated total latent load over 1800 Btu (1899 kJ) had some amount of detectable water downstream 



of the helmet, while most EVAs below this limit did not. Using these results, a two-part warning system was developed 



to flag EVAs that are at a high risk of presenting significant water in the helmet. The warning would be triggered if 



either of the following criteria are met: 



1. Latent load of greater than 300 Btu/hr (87.9 W) maintained for over one hour. 



2. Total latent load of 1800 Btu (1899 kJ) sustained during an EVA. Total latent load is calculated by 



integrating the latent load with respect to time. For all investigated EVAs, the first criteria was also met 



in all EVAs where the second criteria was met. 



This warning system would be used to alert the crewmember during an EVA if they were producing a latent load 



that significantly increased the risk of water in the helmet. The warning system would not mandate the crewmember 



adjusts the TCV, but rather allow operations personnel to make suggestions to the crewmember to consider their 



thermal comfort and potentially adjust their TCV. The goal of this warning system is to flag EVAs where there is a 



significant risk of water in the helmet, while also avoiding false warnings. If the warning system calls out warnings 



too frequently, it may lead to reduced trust in the warning system by crewmembers and flight directors. Additionally, 



adjusting the TCV requires a manual adjustment, so issuing too many warnings could result in a reduction in 



productivity during an EVA as crewmembers stop their tasks to adjust their TCV setting. 



This warning system was tested on the same set of 45 historical EVAs that were used to develop the warning 



system. Testing the warning system using the same data that was used to develop the warning system may lead to 



overestimation of the effectiveness, which will be addressed in Section V. These EVAs were modeled using SINDA 



EMU to calculate the latent load through the duration of the EVA. These consisted of EVAs conducted using the 



sublimators that were on ISS during the time of US EVA 80 as well as any EVAs that presented some form of water 



in the helmet.

On Space Transportation System 117 (STS-117) EVA 1, EV 2 had a latched CO2 sensor which indicated potential

been implemented, a warning would have been called. This warning would have been justified since actual events

no EVAs that would have experienced a warning but did not require a warning. STS-118 EVA 2 is categorized as a 



missed warning, since there would have been no warning called out, but water was present in the helmet. This is 



because STS-118 EVA 2 was an outlier when compared to other historical EVAs. STS-118 EVA 2 presented water 



in the helmet despite its comparable latent loads to historical EVAs that did not present any water in the helmet. This 



EVA may have presented water in the helmet due to sublimator degradation. The sublimator used during STS-118 



EVA 2 was an older sublimator which may have resulted in significant carryover, even at low latent loads. The 



assumed thermal management of the crewmember during this EVA may also have not been properly determined (TCV 



and heat storage data is sparser on older EVAs) which could also explain this EVA as an outlier due to less accurate 



model inputs. Finally, there were 41 EVAs that are classified as proper no warning, since no warning would have been 



issued and no warning was required. 



This warning system’s ability to flag EVAs that are more likely to present water in the helmet was shown to 



effectively identify EVAs that will likely have significant sublimator carryover. This warning system was also shown 



to be effective at preventing warnings from occurring too frequently, particularly when warnings are not required. 



While this investigation shows that SINDA EMU is capable of identifying EVAs that are likely to present water in 



the helmet, additional work is needed to ensure that issuing a warning can provide a significant reduction in risk.

C. US EVA 35 



The TCV parametric investigation was also conducted for US EVA 35, which was another EVA that had 



significant water in the helmet, likely due to sublimator carryover. Figure IV-2 shows the calculated latent load and

During the first 1.5 hours of the EVA, the crewmember is at such a high latent load that significant amounts of the 



sweat production cannot be evaporated. This leftover sweat is absorbed by the LCVG and called sweat runoff. Sweat

the warning system could only be tested on the same set of data that was used to develop it. This may result in 



overconfidence in the capabilities of the warning system. Fortunately, EVAs that occur after US EVA 80 allow for

and reporting of the TCV setting. For this reason, all EVAs after US EVA 80 have much more accurate TCV setting 



information than was available for prior EVAs. These TCV settings allow for SINDA EMU to be run with significantly 



greater accuracy than previous EVAs. SINDA EMU was run for both crewmembers on US EVAs 81-84 and the 



performance of the proposed warning system was tested. To account for uncertainty in mass of the crewmember (since 



crewmember mass impacts latent load calculation), the model was run three times for each EVA (+/- 20 lbm [9.07

The latent load calculations for US EVAs 81-84 are plotted in Figure V-1 and compared to the proposed warning 



limit of 300 Btu/hr (87.9 W). This plot only shows one of the three parametric cases. This analysis showed that the 



impact of varying mass only affects the latent load by about 5%. For this discussion, the additional +/- masses will be 



neglected. Of the eight total model runs (2 crewmembers for each EVA), the latent load limit of 300 Btu/hr (87.9 W) 



was exceeded for a significant amount of time on two of the EVAs (US EVA 81 EV 2 and US EVA 84 EV 1). 



On US EVA 81, EV 2 exceeded the limit for approximately 1.5 hours at the start of the EVA. Since the limit was 



exceeded for more than an hour, if the warning system were implemented, a warning would have been sent to the 



crewmember. Ultimately, there was no water in the helmet of EV 2 on US EVA 81 which would classify this EVA as 



a false warning. The reason this EVA likely did not produce water in the helmet is because the high latent loads were 



not sustained for the duration of the EVA. Shortly after a warning would have been issued, the latent load for EV 2 



dropped below the limit, which is a similar response to what would be expected if a warning had been implemented. 



During US EVA 84, EV 1 experienced a latent load over the limit for slightly less than one hour. Based on criteria 



for issuing a warning, this would not have been called up as a warning since the latent load was not maintained for 



more than one hour. Towards the end of EVA 84, communication with EV2 was lost in one ear, likely due to water in 



the ear cup of the Crew Communication Electronics Module (CCEM). This could have been due to either sublimator 



carryover or sweat from the ear. While communication lost occurred near the end of the EVA, it is possible that the 



high latent load at the start of the EVA contributed to the presence of water. Losing communications during an EVA 



is a common occurrence and for this reason, this will not be considered a water anomaly and this EVA was classified 



as an accurate no-warning. 



The other six EVAs all remained below the limit for the duration of the EVA and did not report any water in the 



helmet so were flagged as accurate no-warnings. 



D. Additional Findings from Post-EVA Modeling 



In addition to testing the warning system, the results from modeling these EVAs with SINDA EMU has led to a 



few additional findings. First, the highest latent load generally occurs at the beginning of the EVA. This could be due 



to the higher sink temperature in the crewlock, high metrates, or difficulties in predicting necessary cooling at the 



beginning of an EVA. When the crew begins an EVA, they are in a warm crewlock which reduces the heat leak from 



the suit and may increase the heat storage, causing sweating near the start of an EVA. Additionally, the high latent 



load could be caused by higher metrate at the start of the EVA. Finally, at the start of the EVA it may be difficult to

anticipate the workload which may result in incorrect TCV settings at the start of the EVA resulting in warmer 



crewmembers. 



Another trend is that the highest latent load cases were on EVAs where the crewmember was performing the first 



EVA of their career. Both US EVA 81 EV2 and US EVA 84 EV1 were the first EVAs for the respective crewmembers. 



The following EVAs for the crewmember on US EVA 81 had lower latent loads which may indicate that the crew is 



learning from experience and improving their ability at maintaining thermal neutrality. At the time of writing, the 



crewmember from US EVA 84 has not conducted another EVA to see if they are also able to reduce the latent load, 



but this will be investigated going forward. 



VI. Discussion and Future Work 



Testing the warning system on EVAs 81-84 allowed for the warning system to be assessed. Overall, had the 



warning system been implemented, there would have been seven EVAs where no warning was issued or necessary, 



and one EVA where a warning was issued but not necessary. US EVA 81, EV 2 most likely did not present any form 



of water in the helmet because the crewmember was able to reduce the latent load relatively quickly after a warning 



would have been issued. This EVA shows the impact of reducing the latent load and preventing sustained high latent 



loads. Had they sustained the maximum latent load for the duration of the EVA, it is likely that significant sublimator 



carryover would have built up enough to enter the helmet. 



One significant limitation of testing the warning system on additional EVAs is the lack of positive (i.e. water in 



the helmet) events that will occur after US EVA 80. Due to the US EVA 80 investigation, corrective actions were put 



in place to prevent crewmembers from sustaining a high latent load for a significant length of time. For this reason, 



the likelihood of a future EVA having significant water in the helmet is reduced. It will be difficult to test the warning 



system’s ability to identify high risk EVAs since high risk EVAs will theoretically be less likely. For this reason it is 



necessary to rely on the historical dataset or expand knowledge on the integrated performance of the EMU to further 



quantify the divide between high risk and low risk latent loads. 



 The warning system outlined an effective method of screening for high risk EVAs, however there are still 



numerous steps that need to be taken to ensure that implementing this warning system is feasible and effective. 



 



1. Capabilities for live modeling of SINDA EMU are recommended. Currently, SINDA EMU cannot be run in 



real time during an EVA. Properly implementing this warning system would rely on a version of the model 



which is able to receive live data and calculate the latent load in real time. The model is currently fast enough 



to run live, but the workflow is not simple enough to be reliably used. In the future, this model would most 



likely be run on the ground by an analyst and potential warnings would be reported to flight directors. 



 



2. Further understanding of the EMU system is required to quantify the amount of sublimator carryover that 



will occur for a given latent loading of the sublimator. Through SINDA EMU modeling, it was shown that 



high latent loads are associated with more sublimator carryover. However, there is still a gap in understanding 



of how much carryover is expected for a given latent load and how much carryover is required to result in 



water reaching the helmet. With separately planned efforts that include testing and additional analysis and 



modeling, knowledge is expected to be gained concerning this gap. Once this is better understood, defining 



the high risk band of latent loads will become easier, improving the accuracy of the warning system. 



 



3. Corrective actions and procedures in response to a warning are also recommended. This analysis has shown 



that adjusting the TCV to increase cooling after receiving a warning has the potential to drastically reduce 



the latent load in high risk EVAs. However, more work is necessary to determine how much the TCV should 



be should be adjusted and if this adjustment may cause other unintended consequences. 



VII. Conclusions 



After the US EVA 80 water in the helmet failure, an investigation determined that the most likely cause was 



sublimator carryover caused by a comparatively high latent load. One of the most effective ways of reducing the risk 



of water in the helmet from this failure is to limit the latent load that is produced by the crewmember and sent to the 



sublimator. High latent load occurs when the crewmember is warmer than usual (high heat storage). This analysis 



outlined a potential warning system that could be used to alert the crewmember when they are experiencing a high 



latent load and at increased risk of water in the helmet. This would allow for the crewmember to adjust their TCV 



setting to increase cooling and reduce latent load. This warning system was based on thermal/fluid modeling of

numerous historical EVAs that included both EVAs with and without significant carryover. Using this data, a latent 



load limit was established. If this rate was exceeded for more than an hour, a warning would be issued and the 



crewmember would be alerted of the elevated latent load. 



This warning system was tested on the historical EVAs prior to US EVA 80. This showed that if the warning 



system had been implemented, a warning would have been issued on three of the four EVAs that presented significant 



sublimator carryover. There would have been no false warnings issued on the 41 EVAs that did not present significant 



sublimator carryover. The analysis also showed that, had the warning system been implemented and resulted in a TCV 



adjustment on US EVA 80 and US EVA 35 (two EVAs with significant amounts of water in helmet), the total latent 



load would have reduced by up to 33.1% and 26.6%, respectively. The warning system was also tested on US EVAs 



81-84, which occurred after US EVA 80. None of these EVAs presented water in the helmet, and the warning system 



would have issued one warning on US EVA 81, EV2. US EVA 81 most likely did not present water in the helmet 



because the latent load quickly lowered below the limit after a warning would have been issued. This warning system 



and operational controls will be further improved through future investigation into sublimator performance and 



modeling of future EVAs. 



Ultimately, the warning system proposed in this analysis provides an effective way to monitor the latent load 



during EVAs and provides details on how to reduce the latent load using the TCV setting in high risk conditions. This 



system would provide an effective way to operationally reduce the risk of water entering the helmet again on future 



EVAs.
