<p>SUMMARY AND CONCLUDING REMARKS 
</p>
<p>This paper presents the false positive (FP) and false 
</p>
<p>negative (FN) risk assessment process currently being 
</p>
<p>conducted for the Space Launch System (SLS) Artemis II 
</p>
<p>Fault Management (FM) detection functions. Because initial 
</p>
<p>analyses indicated a dominance in the total risk by software 
</p>
<p>and firmware failures, efforts were made to refine those risks 
</p>
<p>which involved: 
</p>
<p>• Establishing software function traces for each 
</p>
<p>detection algorithm, 
</p>
<p>• Utilizing the Logical Source Lines of Code 
</p>
<p>(LSLOC) count, 
</p>
<p>• Refinement of the software failure rate, and 
</p>
<p>• Establishing fractional multipliers for common 
</p>
<p>hardware and software failure modes across the 
</p>
<p>applicable individual fault trees. 
</p>
<p>These efforts and their impact on the overall analyses are 
</p>
<p>also discussed. The analysis scope, general assumptions and 
</p>
<p>guide rules, and key modeling concepts are discussed to 
</p>
<p>establish the basis of the risk assessments conducted. 
</p>
<p>Even with the implementation of the analysis 
</p>
<p>refinements, software and firmware are still key risk 
</p>
<p>contributors, but hardware failures, primarily in the form of 
</p>
<p>Common Cause Failures (CCFs), are also indicated as risk 
</p>
<p>drivers. The refinements enable risk estimations of 
</p>
<p>individual detection functions as well as the entire FM suite. 
</p>
<p>There still remains issues of how to account for time and 
</p>
<p>redundancy in the software risk estimations that will 
</p>
<p>continue to be the focus of future work. 
</p>
<p>1 INTRODUCTION 
</p>
<p>The purpose of this effort is to provide a risk assessment 
</p>
<p>process for a detection system that includes hardware and 
</p>
<p>software elements. The detection system is composed of 
</p>
<p>individual functions, each monitoring unique off-nominal 
</p>
<p>conditions. The process must provide a risk assessment for 
</p>
<p>each function, as well as for the entire detection system. The 
</p>
<p>process is developed specifically for the Space Launch 
</p>
<p>System (SLS) on-board Fault Management (FM) system. 
</p>
<p>The SLS FM system is a function set that enables the 
</p>
<p>launch vehicle to assess and respond to states that deviate 
</p>
<p>from required or expected performance objectives. The FM 
</p>
<p>function on a crewed SLS vehicle will first and foremost 
</p>
<p>ensure crew safety and maximize mission success. 
</p>
<p>Additionally, it will annunciate performance deviations or 
</p>
<p>detected failures to the flight crew and mission support 
</p>
<p>personnel, and in some scenarios, perform predetermined 
</p>
<p>operational responses based on the nature of the failure. 
</p>
<p>These responses include enacting redundancy management 
</p>
<p>(RM) steps to maintain flight critical functions through fault 
</p>
<p>tolerance, enacting safing actions to reconfigure the system 
</p>
<p>to prevent failures from propagating and recommending 
</p>
<p>abort when Loss of Vehicle (LOV) is imminent or has 
</p>
<p>occurred. 
</p>
<p>For any given FM response, whether abort, safing or 
</p>
<p>redundancy management, there are inherent risks that it will 
</p>
<p>not perform as intended, either by initiating a response that 
</p>
<p>is unwarranted or by not responding when needed. The 
</p>
<p>former response is defined as a FP event – an incorrect 
</p>
<p>decision that a condition exists when it does not. The latter 
</p>
<p>response is a FN event – an incorrect decision that a 
</p>
<p>condition does not exist when it does. Both have an adverse 
</p>
<p>impact on the vehicle and crew. The response to a FP 
</p>
<p>detection places the vehicle and crew in an unnecessary 
</p>
<p>high-risk situation. For example, an abort response, whether 
</p>
<p>it is a true detection or not, has its own contribution to the 
</p>
<p>total probability of a Loss of Crew (LOC) and Loss of 
</p>
<p>Mission (LOM). On the other hand, no abort response 
</p>
<p>because of a FN misdetection means the vehicle and crew 
</p>
<p>lack the awareness of, or protection from a dangerous 
</p>
<p>situation. Other FM responses (e.g., RM or safing) can also 
</p>
<p>have FP and FN detections that impact mission and crew, 
</p>
<p>albeit at less severity than aborts. 
</p>
<p>The FM functions are divided into two distinct sub-
</p>
<p>functions: detection and response. The detection portion of 
</p>
<p>the FM function includes avionics components from the </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>sensing elements and avionics boxes that generate the 
</p>
<p>monitored data to the detection decision logic within the 
</p>
<p>Flight Computers (FCs) that determine whether the 
</p>
<p>monitored condition has occurred. While not part of the on-
</p>
<p>board functions, detection can also be initiated by external 
</p>
<p>entities, such as launch control, mission control and flight 
</p>
<p>crew. The response sub-functions would include the 
</p>
<p>software and hardware elements required to generate and 
</p>
<p>transmit the commands to the actuators and/or annunciations 
</p>
<p>of the detected condition to the crew and system operators, 
</p>
<p>as well as the components that transmit and perform the 
</p>
<p>response actions. For the analysis presented here, only the 
</p>
<p>on-board FM detection sub-function is considered. 
</p>
<p>The SLS FM detection sub-function FP and FN risk 
</p>
<p>estimations have two components: threshold-based (TB) and 
</p>
<p>non-threshold-based (NTB). Both are required to provide a 
</p>
<p>complete picture of the inherent risk from employing each 
</p>
<p>FM function. The TB estimation is applicable only to FM 
</p>
<p>functions that monitor continuous state variables. These 
</p>
<p>estimations can involve the detection threshold, timing 
</p>
<p>requirements, and/or the persistence applied in the detection 
</p>
<p>logic. They require a detailed understanding of the off-
</p>
<p>nominal condition being monitored, and often require 
</p>
<p>physics-based modeling and simulations to establish the 
</p>
<p>FP/FN risk estimate. The TB risk estimation is generally 
</p>
<p>performed by the hardware system designer when needed 
</p>
<p>and is beyond the scope of this paper. 
</p>
<p>NTB FP and FN risk assessments for the SLS FM 
</p>
<p>detection sub-function address the reliability of the detection 
</p>
<p>system and include hardware components along the signal 
</p>
<p>path and SLS flight software (FSW). These assessments 
</p>
<p>attempt to determine the failures that would affect the 
</p>
<p>monitored data resulting in an erroneous detection (FP case) 
</p>
<p>or misdetection (FN case). For the assessment of each FM 
</p>
<p>detection function, the function must be defined by the 
</p>
<p>monitored signals, detection criteria, and monitoring period. 
</p>
<p>Information about the hardware design, FSW processing 
</p>
<p>flow, and hardware failure modes and reliability data for 
</p>
<p>components of the detection system are also required. This 
</p>
<p>information is then used to perform a fault tree based 
</p>
<p>Probabilistic Risk Assessment (PRA). 
</p>
<p>The combination of SLS FM detection and response 
</p>
<p>sub-function FP and FN risk assessments provide the 
</p>
<p>relative risk increase due to the FM system which can be 
</p>
<p>compared to the benefit provided by correctly detecting and 
</p>
<p>responding to the off-nominal condition. The FP/FN 
</p>
<p>assessments of the FM functions provides valuable data to 
</p>
<p>support the selection, assessment, and verification of the 
</p>
<p>abort triggers [1]. These results are fed into the SLS Mission 
</p>
<p>and Fault Management (M&amp;FM) program’s abort analyses 
</p>
<p>and are inputs to the SLS Program PRA LOM and LOC 
</p>
<p>models. In turn, the SLS PRA models feed into Cross-
</p>
<p>Program and Artemis Program PRA models.  
</p>
<p>This paper focuses on describing the NTB SLS FM 
</p>
<p>detection sub-function FP and FN risk assessments for the 
</p>
<p>SLS FM abort and safing conditions. These assessments 
</p>
<p>include only the failure risk contributions of the hardware 
</p>
<p>and software elements of the detection system, potentially 
</p>
<p>identifying areas where reliability issues should be 
</p>
<p>addressed. The analyses incorporate CCFs which are 
</p>
<p>inherent in the software and firmware failures modeled, and 
</p>
<p>explicitly modeled for the redundant hardware failure 
</p>
<p>events. They do not include contributions due to human 
</p>
<p>factors from Launch Support, Mission Control or Flight 
</p>
<p>Crew. The risk assessments do not explicitly include 
</p>
<p>phenomenological and external events (e.g., leaks, 
</p>
<p>surrounding environment, wind, lightning, debris, etc.) that 
</p>
<p>would affect the reliability of the detection hardware, 
</p>
<p>although, in some cases that information may be implicitly 
</p>
<p>included in the underlying component failure data. 
</p>
<p>Furthermore, for both FP and FN, the assessment does not 
</p>
<p>account for the effects of the underlying vehicle failure on 
</p>
<p>the detection mechanism. The assessments were conducted 
</p>
<p>following the NASA PRA Procedures Guide, NASA/SP-
</p>
<p>2011-3421 [2], and the NASA Procedural Requirements 
</p>
<p>NPR 8705.5A [3],  
</p>
<p>2 ANALYSIS SCOPE 
</p>
<p>The NTB FP/FN analyses conducted assess the risk that 
</p>
<p>a detection function will, while monitoring the system, 
</p>
<p>erroneously detect an off-nominal condition (FP outcome) 
</p>
<p>or misdetect an off-nominal condition (FN outcome). The 
</p>
<p>analysis encompasses the failure assessment of the signal 
</p>
<p>propagation path, but excludes complex external events 
</p>
<p>(e.g., debris or lightning strikes) and human factor events 
</p>
<p>(e.g., erroneous inputs from Crew or ground support 
</p>
<p>personnel actions). The detection function risk assessment is 
</p>
<p>based on the measured parameter and not on the overall 
</p>
<p>vehicle state.  
</p>
<p>This distinction is important to consider when defining 
</p>
<p>the boundaries of the PRA. For example, the SLS design 
</p>
<p>contains a pair of Autonomous Destruction Units (ADUs) 
</p>
<p>that reside on each booster. The ADUs monitor break-wire 
</p>
<p>circuits to determine if premature booster separation has 
</p>
<p>occurred. If detected, the ADUs will activate pyrotechnic 
</p>
<p>charges on the separated booster in-order to destroy it. For 
</p>
<p>an actual premature booster separation scenario, the 
</p>
<p>communication lines between the booster and the flight 
</p>
<p>computers would be severed. Therefore detection of the 
</p>
<p>ADU activation by the FCs would be an indication of 
</p>
<p>failures in the ADU or break-wire circuits that lead to an 
</p>
<p>erroneous destruction of the vehicle. An FM detection 
</p>
<p>function monitors for the unintended destruction of a booster 
</p>
<p>by the booster’s safety system. So a false detection by the 
</p>
<p>booster safety system would lead to a false abort, but for the 
</p>
<p>FM detection function this would be a true positive 
</p>
<p>detection. For the FM detection function, a FP outcome 
</p>
<p>would result only if the function detected the ADU 
</p>
<p>activation, but no ADU activation actually occurred.  
</p>
<p>Each FM detection function is defined initially by the </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>M&amp;FM group in collaboration with domain experts, the 
</p>
<p>element project office which includes element prime 
</p>
<p>contractors, ground and mission support personnel, and the 
</p>
<p>SLS Integrated Avionics and Software (IAS) discipline. The 
</p>
<p>off-nominal or fault monitoring functions are initially 
</p>
<p>defined in the SLS Program FM specification document and 
</p>
<p>implemented in SysML logic diagrams [4] by the subsystem 
</p>
<p>integrated design team; a team made up of subject matter 
</p>
<p>experts and FSW developers. The SLS FSW requirements 
</p>
<p>group then creates the associated software requirements 
</p>
<p>from which the FSW code developer builds the final 
</p>
<p>executable code. The software then goes through extensive 
</p>
<p>integrated simulation and hardware testing, as well as code 
</p>
<p>and requirements verification processes.  
</p>
<p>Key elements of the detection function used to establish 
</p>
<p>the scope of the FP/FN analysis include: 
</p>
<p>• The measurements and component health status 
</p>
<p>data monitored. 
</p>
<p>• The criteria for detection (e.g., threshold(s), 
</p>
<p>persistence, and number of parameter violations 
</p>
<p>required), including any special requirements such 
</p>
<p>as number of qualified signals needed to perform 
</p>
<p>the detection. 
</p>
<p>• The start and stop events that define when the 
</p>
<p>function is active or processed. 
</p>
<p>With the FM function defined, the detection processing 
</p>
<p>architecture can be determined and the potential contributing 
</p>
<p>components to the FP/FN risk assessment established. In 
</p>
<p>general, the detection processing architecture can be broken 
</p>
<p>down into the following areas: 
</p>
<p>• Sensors – The component measuring the system 
</p>
<p>property (e.g., pressure, etc.). 
</p>
<p>• Instrumentation/Electronics – Avionics 
</p>
<p>components that gather, process and/or transfer the 
</p>
<p>subsystem data to the FCs.  
</p>
<p>• Data Buses – The wires, harnesses and connectors 
</p>
<p>transferring the analog or digital measurement from 
</p>
<p>the instrumentation to the FCs. 
</p>
<p>• Flight Computers – The final component along the 
</p>
<p>signal propagation path where the FM detection 
</p>
<p>function resides. The SLS uses three FCs that 
</p>
<p>import data streams, and perform signal validation 
</p>
<p>and data processing. The three FCs exchange data 
</p>
<p>between each other to ensure that all FC FM 
</p>
<p>functions are using identical data sets.  
</p>
<p>The following are a high level list of Ground Rules and 
</p>
<p>Assumptions that support the analysis. 
</p>
<p>• Are performed on the M&amp;FM FM detection 
</p>
<p>functions that impact LOM/LOC. 
</p>
<p>• Are based on the SLS Block 1 crewed-vehicle 
</p>
<p>design which includes the solid rocket boosters, 
</p>
<p>Core Stage (CS) element, and the Core Stage 
</p>
<p>Engines (CSEs). 
</p>
<p>• Are based on the integrated vehicle prelaunch and 
</p>
<p>ascent timelines for the Artemis II mission. 
</p>
<p>• Do not include external failures from the Orion, 
</p>
<p>Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (ICPS) 
</p>
<p>element, ground support, flight control, or the crew. 
</p>
<p>• Do not include SLS Vehicle external monitoring 
</p>
<p>functions (e.g., Launch Commit Criteria). 
</p>
<p>• Do not include the  effects of the underlying vehicle 
</p>
<p>failure on the detection mechanism. 
</p>
<p>3 ANALYSIS PROCESS 
</p>
<p>The NTB FP and FN analyses were performed on each 
</p>
<p>of the monitored conditions independently. This was done to 
</p>
<p>make the development focused and manageable, and to 
</p>
<p>provide an individual risk assessment of each detection 
</p>
<p>function to perform risk-to-benefit assessments, comparing 
</p>
<p>the risk of including the detection function against the 
</p>
<p>benefit of providing detection of the off-nominal condition. 
</p>
<p>The risk estimations were generated using fault trees, which 
</p>
<p>graphically model the detection signal pathways that can 
</p>
<p>lead to a known and undesirable event.  
</p>
<p>The FP/FN fault tree development and uncertainty 
</p>
<p>analysis is performed in a software program called Systems 
</p>
<p>Analysis Programs for Hands-on Integrated Reliability 
</p>
<p>Evaluations (SAPHIRE) [5]. This program was developed 
</p>
<p>by the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission and is used by 
</p>
<p>the various NASA PRA groups such as SLS PRA for which 
</p>
<p>the FP/FN results feed. The program provides for a 
</p>
<p>development environment where the fault trees can be 
</p>
<p>constructed visually. It can solve the fault tree, generating 
</p>
<p>the minimal cut sets, and perform an uncertainty analysis by 
</p>
<p>performing a Monte Carlo simulation of the fault tree 
</p>
<p>solution with the assigned uncertainty data as the variant. 
</p>
<p>Model verification was performed by internal M&amp;FM 
</p>
<p>reviews of the fault trees, and a review of the fault trees and 
</p>
<p>analysis outputs by the SLS PRA group. 
</p>
<p>3.1 Hardware Failure Event Modeling 
</p>
<p>The hardware failure models assume that the failure rate 
</p>
<p>is constant for the operational phase being analyzed. The 
</p>
<p>failure probability for most hardware components can be 
</p>
<p>modeled as an exponential function of operating or exposure 
</p>
<p>time, t, and the constant failure rate, 0, as shown in equation 
</p>
<p>1. For small time periods, less than 20% of the mean time 
</p>
<p>between failures, the hardware probability can be 
</p>
<p>approximated to be the product of 0 and t. 
</p>
<p>𝑃𝑓ℎ𝑎𝑟𝑑𝑤𝑎𝑟𝑒
= 1 −  𝑒−𝜆0𝑡 ≅  𝜆0𝑡 (1) 
</p>
<p>The hardware failure rates used in the analyses were 
</p>
<p>extracted from multiple sources and represent the best 
</p>
<p>source of data available. When possible, the SLS PRA data 
</p>
<p>was used. Where PRA based failure rate data did not exist, 
</p>
<p>the SLS element’s Reliability and Maintainability (R&amp;M) 
</p>
<p>analysis report, Military Handbook: Reliability Prediction of 
</p>
<p>Electronic Equipment (MIL-HDBK-217F) [6], and/or 
</p>
<p>domain experts judgment were used. 
</p>
<p>Early SLS FP/FN analyses [7] were based on hardware 
</p>
<p>data with failure modes and reliability data assigned at the </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>functional level. The reliability analyses for the SLS Block 
</p>
<p>1 Core Stage avionics components have since become more 
</p>
<p>detailed, enabling a more refined assignment for the 
</p>
<p>hardware failure rate. The Failure Modes and Effects 
</p>
<p>(FMEA) analyses have been conducted down to the piece-
</p>
<p>part level for the avionics boxes and sensing components, 
</p>
<p>and each failure mode was traced to failure rate data. The 
</p>
<p>current analysis involves identifying a component’s failure 
</p>
<p>modes that result in effects that could contribute to FP or FN 
</p>
<p>outcomes. Assuming the individual failure modes are 
</p>
<p>independent, the failure rates aligned to those failure modes 
</p>
<p>are summed to create a single component-level failure rate. 
</p>
<p>3.2 Common Cause Failure (CCF) 
</p>
<p>CCF modeling is an attempt to include in the fault tree 
</p>
<p>logic, the intrinsic dependencies of the system that are not 
</p>
<p>explicitly represented in the independent redundant failure 
</p>
<p>models. Failures arising from these dependencies are often 
</p>
<p>difficult to identify and, if neglected in PRA modeling and 
</p>
<p>quantifications, may result in an underestimation of the risk. 
</p>
<p>CCFs can be a large risk contributor for redundant systems.  
</p>
<p>The FP/FN analysis uses the Alpha (non-staggered) 
</p>
<p>CCF method to be consistent with the SLS PRA 
</p>
<p>methodology. In a staggered system, individual units can be 
</p>
<p>tested and replaced as needed. In a non-staggered system, 
</p>
<p>the items are installed and operated as a group; individual 
</p>
<p>units cannot be isolated from the system and tested. The SLS 
</p>
<p>integrated launch vehicle is a non-staggered system. The 
</p>
<p>Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) issued NUREG-
</p>
<p>5485 [8] provides information and guidelines for modeling 
</p>
<p>CCFs that are applied in this analysis. 
</p>
<p> The CCF risk is calculated by multiplying the 
</p>
<p>component’s independent failure rate, 0, by the Global 
</p>
<p>Alpha Factor (GAF), YGAF, and the failure exposure time, t, 
</p>
<p>as shown in equation 2.  
</p>
<p> 𝑃𝑓𝐶𝐶𝐹
=  𝜆0 ∗  𝑌𝐺𝐴𝐹 ∗  𝑡 (2) 
</p>
<p>The GAF values are obtained using an Excel workbook 
</p>
<p>tool called Global Alpha Model Uncertainty Tool (GAMUT) 
</p>
<p>2012. This tool was created by the NASA Johnson Space 
</p>
<p>Center (JSC) Safety and Mission Assurance (S&amp;MA) in 
</p>
<p>support of the Space Shuttle and International Space Station 
</p>
<p>(ISS) PRAs. GAMUT uses the 2012 version of NUREG/CR-
</p>
<p>5496 as its source for the mean and uncertainty of the global 
</p>
<p>alpha parameters. Specifically, it uses generic alpha values 
</p>
<p>as opposed to specific values (e.g., pump values, valve 
</p>
<p>values, etc.). These generic values are provided by 
</p>
<p>NUREG/CR-5496 for groups of redundant components up 
</p>
<p>to eight. GAMUT uses regression to determine values for 
</p>
<p>groups of more than eight [9]. 
</p>
<p>The output from GAMUT is the GAF mean value and a 
</p>
<p>Beta parameter which defines the GAF uncertainty. Within 
</p>
<p>SAPHIRE, these values will define the model of the specific 
</p>
<p>CCF event. The standard operation of GAMUT is for all 
</p>
<p>combinations of components for the specified redundancy 
</p>
<p>level and number of component failures required to result in 
</p>
<p>system failure, but the tool can be adjusted to output the GAF 
</p>
<p>data for a specific set of combinations [10]. 
</p>
<p>3.3 Software Failure Event Modeling 
</p>
<p>SLS FSW is flight critical code. Identical copies of the 
</p>
<p>code are loaded into all three FCs that make up the Flight 
</p>
<p>Computer Operational Group (FCOG). The current SLS 
</p>
<p>PRA approach for assessing FSW failure is described in 
</p>
<p>Novack et al [11].  
</p>
<p>The quantification of the FSW risk follows the SLS 
</p>
<p>PRA modeling practice, which was based on Space Shuttle 
</p>
<p>Orbiter historical data, as described in the Primary Avionics 
</p>
<p>Software System (PASS) PRA [12]. The PASS PRA 
</p>
<p>estimated the probability for a Shuttle PASS failure during a 
</p>
<p>nominal mission resulting in a LOC event. The PASS PRA 
</p>
<p>defined a Source Line Of Code (SLOC) as the smallest 
</p>
<p>increment of source code that can be compiled into 
</p>
<p>executable code by a compiler, and an error in a SLOC as a 
</p>
<p>binary condition that either will or will not execute in a 
</p>
<p>manner compliant with requirements. Each SLOC was 
</p>
<p>treated as independent with a constant identical failure rate.  
</p>
<p>Firmware is coding written to a hardware device’s non-
</p>
<p>volatile memory. It is installed directly onto a piece of 
</p>
<p>hardware and is essentially software that enables the 
</p>
<p>hardware to run. Firmware elements, when identified in the 
</p>
<p>FP/FN analysis, are treated similarly to FSW with the SLOC 
</p>
<p>data and modeling approach, except the SLOC data is 
</p>
<p>extracted from the design algorithm that programs the 
</p>
<p>hardware. A coding language, Hardware Description 
</p>
<p>Language (HDL), is compiled into a configuration file which 
</p>
<p>can be loaded into the hardware. 
</p>
<p>Within NASA PRA, the firmware risk may be modeled 
</p>
<p>differently depending on data availability. In some cases the 
</p>
<p>firmware failures are modeled as described above, while in 
</p>
<p>other cases firmware failures are included within the 
</p>
<p>hardware reliability assessments, or estimated as a 
</p>
<p>percentage of the avionics box failure rate. The impact of 
</p>
<p>modeling the firmware failures as hardware is the the the 
</p>
<p>failure events inherit the properties of operational time and 
</p>
<p>redundancy that the software models do not. 
</p>
<p>For this FP/FN analysis, FSW and firmware failure 
</p>
<p>events are modeled as the software failure rate, FRSW, 
</p>
<p>multiplied by the SLOC of the software code or firmware 
</p>
<p>design code, NSLOC, in equation 3. 
</p>
<p> 𝑃𝑓𝑠𝑜𝑓𝑡𝑤𝑎𝑟𝑒
=  𝐹𝑅𝑆𝑊  ×  𝑁𝑆𝐿𝑂𝐶 (3) 
</p>
<p>3.4 Data Uncertainty 
</p>
<p>To properly characterize and account for the state of 
</p>
<p>knowledge in the underlying data used to model the fault tree 
</p>
<p>basic events, an uncertainty distribution is applied to each of 
</p>
<p>the failure rate point estimates. When possible, basic events 
</p>
<p>that model the same failure rate and uncertainty are 
</p>
<p>correlated. Partial correlation, events modeled unique from 
</p>
<p>each other, is not possible with SAPHIRE and therefore 
</p>
<p>uncertainty distributions may be lower than expected.  </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Typically, the failure rate data are best characterized by 
</p>
<p>a lognormal uncertainty distribution that is either based on 
</p>
<p>existing SLS PRA data or uncertainty estimation guidelines. 
</p>
<p>The lognormal uncertainty distribution can be characterized 
</p>
<p>by an Error Factor (EF), which defines the dispersion about 
</p>
<p>the median. NASA PRAs have used and currently use EF as 
</p>
<p>defined by the ratio of the 95th percentile over the 50th 
</p>
<p>percentile.  
</p>
<p>When the failure rate data is derived or provided by the 
</p>
<p>PRA group, its inherent failure rate uncertainty distribution 
</p>
<p>is maintained. If the failure rate data is derived from other 
</p>
<p>reliability reports (e.g., MIL-HDBK-217F) or domain 
</p>
<p>expert’s inputs where no uncertainty distribution is 
</p>
<p>available, then a NASA PRA based uncertainty assignment 
</p>
<p>is based on engineering judgement.  
</p>
<p>4 ANALYSES CONDUCTED 
</p>
<p>Preliminary FP analyses conducted on several abort 
</p>
<p>detection functions indicated that software failure risk 
</p>
<p>contributions would dominate, and in some cases, be on the 
</p>
<p>same level as the probability of the abort condition. In 
</p>
<p>general, the software risk contributions were one to two 
</p>
<p>orders of magnitude greater than the hardware failures. 
</p>
<p>Because the FP/FN detection analyses were at a lower level 
</p>
<p>than the SLS PRA studies, the assumption was that 
</p>
<p>additional refinement of the failure data was required to 
</p>
<p>provide a better assessment of software and hardware 
</p>
<p>elements for the detection function. The following sections 
</p>
<p>detail the refinements that were developed and applied. 
</p>
<p>4.1 Detailed FSW Code Traces 
</p>
<p>The SLS IAS discipline provided code traces through 
</p>
<p>the actual flight software that handled or processed the 
</p>
<p>measurements or status data monitored by the detection 
</p>
<p>function. For each detection function case, the tracing 
</p>
<p>process was conducted from input data processed by the 
</p>
<p>Avionics Data Handler Input (ADHIN), to data qualification 
</p>
<p>and consolidation, [13] to the data exchange process 
</p>
<p>between the FCs by the Flight Computer Redundancy 
</p>
<p>Management (FCRM) software, and finally to the detection 
</p>
<p>and notification logic. For each area, a set of software 
</p>
<p>functions and modules were identified that could result in 
</p>
<p>the FP or FN outcome. Summing the SLOC counts for each 
</p>
<p>trace provided the overall FSW SLOC specific to the 
</p>
<p>detection function. This provided more refined code paths 
</p>
<p>than was developed for the SLS PRA and resulted in lower 
</p>
<p>SLOC counts. 
</p>
<p>4.2 Estimation of Logical SLOC Data 
</p>
<p>For the FP/FN analysis, Logical SLOC (LSLOC) counts 
</p>
<p>were used instead of SLOC counts as they focused the 
</p>
<p>failure model on the executable portions of the software, 
</p>
<p>reducing the sensitivity to the coding style convention and 
</p>
<p>code formatting. The FSW compiling package reported an 
</p>
<p>estimated SLOC and LSLOC count for each software 
</p>
<p>function, and the software trace SLOC counts were simply 
</p>
<p>converted into LSLOC counts. 
</p>
<p>4.3 Update of Software Failure Rate 
</p>
<p>The initial software failure rate estimated for the SLS 
</p>
<p>PRA modeling was based on the PASS PRA estimation. The 
</p>
<p>SLS PRA has implemented a new process for approximating 
</p>
<p>the software failure rate using the commercial Softrel 
</p>
<p>FREstimate Predictor software reliability tool. The total time 
</p>
<p>under test as well as total number of defects found in each 
</p>
<p>software version release, along with general software 
</p>
<p>development information are input into Frestimate. 
</p>
<p>Frestimate then compares this to common statistical models 
</p>
<p>of software development to quantify expected defect density 
</p>
<p>and rate of failure. This update to the FP/FN analyses alone 
</p>
<p>reduced the failure rate and hence the software risk 
</p>
<p>contributions by an order of magnitude. 
</p>
<p>4.4 Common Failure Split Fractions CFSF) 
</p>
<p>As was stated earlier, each monitored condition was 
</p>
<p>modeled and assessed independently and separately, with 
</p>
<p>the intention of combining the individual fault trees into a 
</p>
<p>total integrated fault tree that would represent the total 
</p>
<p>FP/FN impact of the SLS FM. This was done to evaluate the 
</p>
<p>risk of each detection function individually to allow for a 
</p>
<p>direct risk-to-benefit informed assessment. 
</p>
<p>One issue with this approach is that common hardware 
</p>
<p>and software failures are inherent across the fault trees. In an 
</p>
<p>integrated fault tree, the proper use of a fault tree basic event 
</p>
<p>naming convention will facilitate the Boolean reduction to 
</p>
<p>remove these common events. Common Failure Split 
</p>
<p>Fractions (CFSF) are employed within the fault trees to 
</p>
<p>divide common failure events included in multiple FP and 
</p>
<p>FN fault trees. The CFSF prevents the over-estimation of 
</p>
<p>risk across the suite of detection functions, allowing the 
</p>
<p>independent risk assessment of each detection function and 
</p>
<p>the eventual merger of the fault trees to provide a risk 
</p>
<p>assessment of the entire detection function suite.  
</p>
<p>These CFSFs address two common failure modelling 
</p>
<p>scenarios, failures that impact multiple detection functions 
</p>
<p>directly, and failures that impact multiple detection 
</p>
<p>functions exclusively. For each failure modeled in the FP/FN 
</p>
<p>analyses, across all the detection functions analyzed, a CFSF 
</p>
<p>is created. The CFSF is the inverse of the number of 
</p>
<p>detection function fault trees (including the FP and FN fault 
</p>
<p>trees) that the failure is included for the same time period. 
</p>
<p>Because detection function time periods may differ in start 
</p>
<p>and end times, and to reduce the analytical complexity, 
</p>
<p>mission phases were used to consider the detection 
</p>
<p>monitoring overlap.  
</p>
<p>5 CURRENT FP/FN ASSESSMENT RESULTS AND 
</p>
<p>COMMENTS 
</p>
<p>Current analyses conducted for the SLS Artemis II 
</p>
<p>mission have provided risk contributions for the </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>implemented FM detection function, as well as the relative 
</p>
<p>contributions of software and hardware elements of each 
</p>
<p>abort detection function and most safing response detection 
</p>
<p>functions. With the updates described in previous sections, 
</p>
<p>the primary risk drivers for these functions are flight 
</p>
<p>software failures, firmware design failures, and hardware 
</p>
<p>CCFs. The results provide a more reasonable estimate of the 
</p>
<p>software and firmware risk, and do not eclipse the hardware 
</p>
<p>risk estimations.  
</p>
<p>Still, there are concerns that the software risks are 
</p>
<p>overestimated. Software is represented as a demand failure, 
</p>
<p>meaning that it only fails when used, like a valve, hence no 
</p>
<p>time is included in the failure risk calculation. For a 
</p>
<p>detection function, the ‘demand’ is every processing cycle, 
</p>
<p>and yet the risk estimate for a software failure is the same 
</p>
<p>whether the condition is monitored for one second or one 
</p>
<p>hour. Future FM FP/FN analyses will consider applying 
</p>
<p>fractions based on the active processing time of the function 
</p>
<p>over the entire mission to the software failure models. 
</p>
<p>Additionally, software and firmware failures are 
</p>
<p>modeled as a common cause failure across all the redundant 
</p>
<p>devices, e.g., flight computers. In order to assess the risk 
</p>
<p>impacts of the redundancy strategies for these devices, 
</p>
<p>additional detail needs to be developed to estimate software 
</p>
<p>failures that would result in independent failures. 
</p>
<p>The issue of how to properly model software and 
</p>
<p>firmware failure events and to integrate those models with 
</p>
<p>hardware failure events is a continued focus going forward. 
</p>
<p>More accurate failure modeling of these events would 
</p>
<p>provide improved verification of design selections, and risk-
</p>
<p>informed decisions in applying future development 
</p>
<p>resources.
