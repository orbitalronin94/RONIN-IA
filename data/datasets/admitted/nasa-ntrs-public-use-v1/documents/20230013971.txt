SUMMARY AND CONCLUDING REMARKS 



This paper presents the false positive (FP) and false 



negative (FN) risk assessment process currently being 



conducted for the Space Launch System (SLS) Artemis II 



Fault Management (FM) detection functions. Because initial 



analyses indicated a dominance in the total risk by software 



and firmware failures, efforts were made to refine those risks 



which involved: 



• Establishing software function traces for each 



detection algorithm, 



• Utilizing the Logical Source Lines of Code 



(LSLOC) count, 



• Refinement of the software failure rate, and 



• Establishing fractional multipliers for common 



hardware and software failure modes across the 



applicable individual fault trees. 



These efforts and their impact on the overall analyses are 



also discussed. The analysis scope, general assumptions and 



guide rules, and key modeling concepts are discussed to 



establish the basis of the risk assessments conducted. 



Even with the implementation of the analysis 



refinements, software and firmware are still key risk 



contributors, but hardware failures, primarily in the form of 



Common Cause Failures (CCFs), are also indicated as risk 



drivers. The refinements enable risk estimations of 



individual detection functions as well as the entire FM suite. 



There still remains issues of how to account for time and 



redundancy in the software risk estimations that will 



continue to be the focus of future work. 



1 INTRODUCTION 



The purpose of this effort is to provide a risk assessment 



process for a detection system that includes hardware and 



software elements. The detection system is composed of 



individual functions, each monitoring unique off-nominal 



conditions. The process must provide a risk assessment for 



each function, as well as for the entire detection system. The 



process is developed specifically for the Space Launch 



System (SLS) on-board Fault Management (FM) system. 



The SLS FM system is a function set that enables the 



launch vehicle to assess and respond to states that deviate 



from required or expected performance objectives. The FM 



function on a crewed SLS vehicle will first and foremost 



ensure crew safety and maximize mission success. 



Additionally, it will annunciate performance deviations or 



detected failures to the flight crew and mission support 



personnel, and in some scenarios, perform predetermined 



operational responses based on the nature of the failure. 



These responses include enacting redundancy management 



(RM) steps to maintain flight critical functions through fault 



tolerance, enacting safing actions to reconfigure the system 



to prevent failures from propagating and recommending 



abort when Loss of Vehicle (LOV) is imminent or has 



occurred. 



For any given FM response, whether abort, safing or 



redundancy management, there are inherent risks that it will 



not perform as intended, either by initiating a response that 



is unwarranted or by not responding when needed. The 



former response is defined as a FP event – an incorrect 



decision that a condition exists when it does not. The latter 



response is a FN event – an incorrect decision that a 



condition does not exist when it does. Both have an adverse 



impact on the vehicle and crew. The response to a FP 



detection places the vehicle and crew in an unnecessary 



high-risk situation. For example, an abort response, whether 



it is a true detection or not, has its own contribution to the 



total probability of a Loss of Crew (LOC) and Loss of 



Mission (LOM). On the other hand, no abort response 



because of a FN misdetection means the vehicle and crew 



lack the awareness of, or protection from a dangerous 



situation. Other FM responses (e.g., RM or safing) can also 



have FP and FN detections that impact mission and crew, 



albeit at less severity than aborts. 



The FM functions are divided into two distinct sub-



functions: detection and response. The detection portion of 



the FM function includes avionics components from the 











 



 



sensing elements and avionics boxes that generate the 



monitored data to the detection decision logic within the 



Flight Computers (FCs) that determine whether the 



monitored condition has occurred. While not part of the on-



board functions, detection can also be initiated by external 



entities, such as launch control, mission control and flight 



crew. The response sub-functions would include the 



software and hardware elements required to generate and 



transmit the commands to the actuators and/or annunciations 



of the detected condition to the crew and system operators, 



as well as the components that transmit and perform the 



response actions. For the analysis presented here, only the 



on-board FM detection sub-function is considered. 



The SLS FM detection sub-function FP and FN risk 



estimations have two components: threshold-based (TB) and 



non-threshold-based (NTB). Both are required to provide a 



complete picture of the inherent risk from employing each 



FM function. The TB estimation is applicable only to FM 



functions that monitor continuous state variables. These 



estimations can involve the detection threshold, timing 



requirements, and/or the persistence applied in the detection 



logic. They require a detailed understanding of the off-



nominal condition being monitored, and often require 



physics-based modeling and simulations to establish the 



FP/FN risk estimate. The TB risk estimation is generally 



performed by the hardware system designer when needed 



and is beyond the scope of this paper. 



NTB FP and FN risk assessments for the SLS FM 



detection sub-function address the reliability of the detection 



system and include hardware components along the signal 



path and SLS flight software (FSW). These assessments 



attempt to determine the failures that would affect the 



monitored data resulting in an erroneous detection (FP case) 



or misdetection (FN case). For the assessment of each FM 



detection function, the function must be defined by the 



monitored signals, detection criteria, and monitoring period. 



Information about the hardware design, FSW processing 



flow, and hardware failure modes and reliability data for 



components of the detection system are also required. This 



information is then used to perform a fault tree based 



Probabilistic Risk Assessment (PRA). 



The combination of SLS FM detection and response 



sub-function FP and FN risk assessments provide the 



relative risk increase due to the FM system which can be 



compared to the benefit provided by correctly detecting and 



responding to the off-nominal condition. The FP/FN 



assessments of the FM functions provides valuable data to 



support the selection, assessment, and verification of the 



abort triggers [1]. These results are fed into the SLS Mission 



and Fault Management (M&FM) program’s abort analyses 



and are inputs to the SLS Program PRA LOM and LOC 



models. In turn, the SLS PRA models feed into Cross-



Program and Artemis Program PRA models. 



This paper focuses on describing the NTB SLS FM 



detection sub-function FP and FN risk assessments for the 



SLS FM abort and safing conditions. These assessments 



include only the failure risk contributions of the hardware 



and software elements of the detection system, potentially 



identifying areas where reliability issues should be 



addressed. The analyses incorporate CCFs which are 



inherent in the software and firmware failures modeled, and 



explicitly modeled for the redundant hardware failure 



events. They do not include contributions due to human 



factors from Launch Support, Mission Control or Flight 



Crew. The risk assessments do not explicitly include 



phenomenological and external events (e.g., leaks, 



surrounding environment, wind, lightning, debris, etc.) that 



would affect the reliability of the detection hardware, 



although, in some cases that information may be implicitly 



included in the underlying component failure data. 



Furthermore, for both FP and FN, the assessment does not 



account for the effects of the underlying vehicle failure on 



the detection mechanism. The assessments were conducted 



following the NASA PRA Procedures Guide, NASA/SP-



2011-3421 [2], and the NASA Procedural Requirements 



NPR 8705.5A [3], 



2 ANALYSIS SCOPE 



The NTB FP/FN analyses conducted assess the risk that 



a detection function will, while monitoring the system, 



erroneously detect an off-nominal condition (FP outcome) 



or misdetect an off-nominal condition (FN outcome). The 



analysis encompasses the failure assessment of the signal 



propagation path, but excludes complex external events 



(e.g., debris or lightning strikes) and human factor events 



(e.g., erroneous inputs from Crew or ground support 



personnel actions). The detection function risk assessment is 



based on the measured parameter and not on the overall 



vehicle state. 



This distinction is important to consider when defining 



the boundaries of the PRA. For example, the SLS design 



contains a pair of Autonomous Destruction Units (ADUs) 



that reside on each booster. The ADUs monitor break-wire 



circuits to determine if premature booster separation has 



occurred. If detected, the ADUs will activate pyrotechnic 



charges on the separated booster in-order to destroy it. For 



an actual premature booster separation scenario, the 



communication lines between the booster and the flight 



computers would be severed. Therefore detection of the 



ADU activation by the FCs would be an indication of 



failures in the ADU or break-wire circuits that lead to an 



erroneous destruction of the vehicle. An FM detection 



function monitors for the unintended destruction of a booster 



by the booster’s safety system. So a false detection by the 



booster safety system would lead to a false abort, but for the 



FM detection function this would be a true positive 



detection. For the FM detection function, a FP outcome 



would result only if the function detected the ADU 



activation, but no ADU activation actually occurred. 



Each FM detection function is defined initially by the 











 



 



M&FM group in collaboration with domain experts, the 



element project office which includes element prime 



contractors, ground and mission support personnel, and the 



SLS Integrated Avionics and Software (IAS) discipline. The 



off-nominal or fault monitoring functions are initially 



defined in the SLS Program FM specification document and 



implemented in SysML logic diagrams [4] by the subsystem 



integrated design team; a team made up of subject matter 



experts and FSW developers. The SLS FSW requirements 



group then creates the associated software requirements 



from which the FSW code developer builds the final 



executable code. The software then goes through extensive 



integrated simulation and hardware testing, as well as code 



and requirements verification processes. 



Key elements of the detection function used to establish 



the scope of the FP/FN analysis include: 



• The measurements and component health status 



data monitored. 



• The criteria for detection (e.g., threshold(s), 



persistence, and number of parameter violations 



required), including any special requirements such 



as number of qualified signals needed to perform 



the detection. 



• The start and stop events that define when the 



function is active or processed. 



With the FM function defined, the detection processing 



architecture can be determined and the potential contributing 



components to the FP/FN risk assessment established. In 



general, the detection processing architecture can be broken 



down into the following areas: 



• Sensors – The component measuring the system 



property (e.g., pressure, etc.). 



• Instrumentation/Electronics – Avionics 



components that gather, process and/or transfer the 



subsystem data to the FCs. 



• Data Buses – The wires, harnesses and connectors 



transferring the analog or digital measurement from 



the instrumentation to the FCs. 



• Flight Computers – The final component along the 



signal propagation path where the FM detection 



function resides. The SLS uses three FCs that 



import data streams, and perform signal validation 



and data processing. The three FCs exchange data 



between each other to ensure that all FC FM 



functions are using identical data sets. 



The following are a high level list of Ground Rules and 



Assumptions that support the analysis. 



• Are performed on the M&FM FM detection 



functions that impact LOM/LOC. 



• Are based on the SLS Block 1 crewed-vehicle 



design which includes the solid rocket boosters, 



Core Stage (CS) element, and the Core Stage 



Engines (CSEs). 



• Are based on the integrated vehicle prelaunch and 



ascent timelines for the Artemis II mission. 



• Do not include external failures from the Orion, 



Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (ICPS) 



element, ground support, flight control, or the crew. 



• Do not include SLS Vehicle external monitoring 



functions (e.g., Launch Commit Criteria). 



• Do not include the effects of the underlying vehicle 



failure on the detection mechanism. 



3 ANALYSIS PROCESS 



The NTB FP and FN analyses were performed on each 



of the monitored conditions independently. This was done to 



make the development focused and manageable, and to 



provide an individual risk assessment of each detection 



function to perform risk-to-benefit assessments, comparing 



the risk of including the detection function against the 



benefit of providing detection of the off-nominal condition. 



The risk estimations were generated using fault trees, which 



graphically model the detection signal pathways that can 



lead to a known and undesirable event. 



The FP/FN fault tree development and uncertainty 



analysis is performed in a software program called Systems 



Analysis Programs for Hands-on Integrated Reliability 



Evaluations (SAPHIRE) [5]. This program was developed 



by the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission and is used by 



the various NASA PRA groups such as SLS PRA for which 



the FP/FN results feed. The program provides for a 



development environment where the fault trees can be 



constructed visually. It can solve the fault tree, generating 



the minimal cut sets, and perform an uncertainty analysis by 



performing a Monte Carlo simulation of the fault tree 



solution with the assigned uncertainty data as the variant. 



Model verification was performed by internal M&FM 



reviews of the fault trees, and a review of the fault trees and 



analysis outputs by the SLS PRA group. 



3.1 Hardware Failure Event Modeling 



The hardware failure models assume that the failure rate 



is constant for the operational phase being analyzed. The 



failure probability for most hardware components can be 



modeled as an exponential function of operating or exposure 



time, t, and the constant failure rate, 0, as shown in equation 



1. For small time periods, less than 20% of the mean time 



between failures, the hardware probability can be 



approximated to be the product of 0 and t.

The hardware failure rates used in the analyses were 



extracted from multiple sources and represent the best 



source of data available. When possible, the SLS PRA data 



was used. Where PRA based failure rate data did not exist, 



the SLS element’s Reliability and Maintainability (R&M) 



analysis report, Military Handbook: Reliability Prediction of 



Electronic Equipment (MIL-HDBK-217F) [6], and/or 



domain experts judgment were used. 



Early SLS FP/FN analyses [7] were based on hardware 



data with failure modes and reliability data assigned at the 











 



 



functional level. The reliability analyses for the SLS Block 



1 Core Stage avionics components have since become more 



detailed, enabling a more refined assignment for the 



hardware failure rate. The Failure Modes and Effects 



(FMEA) analyses have been conducted down to the piece-



part level for the avionics boxes and sensing components, 



and each failure mode was traced to failure rate data. The 



current analysis involves identifying a component’s failure 



modes that result in effects that could contribute to FP or FN 



outcomes. Assuming the individual failure modes are 



independent, the failure rates aligned to those failure modes 



are summed to create a single component-level failure rate. 



3.2 Common Cause Failure (CCF) 



CCF modeling is an attempt to include in the fault tree 



logic, the intrinsic dependencies of the system that are not 



explicitly represented in the independent redundant failure 



models. Failures arising from these dependencies are often 



difficult to identify and, if neglected in PRA modeling and 



quantifications, may result in an underestimation of the risk. 



CCFs can be a large risk contributor for redundant systems. 



The FP/FN analysis uses the Alpha (non-staggered) 



CCF method to be consistent with the SLS PRA 



methodology. In a staggered system, individual units can be 



tested and replaced as needed. In a non-staggered system, 



the items are installed and operated as a group; individual 



units cannot be isolated from the system and tested. The SLS 



integrated launch vehicle is a non-staggered system. The 



Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) issued NUREG-



5485 [8] provides information and guidelines for modeling 



CCFs that are applied in this analysis. 



 The CCF risk is calculated by multiplying the 



component’s independent failure rate, 0, by the Global 



Alpha Factor (GAF), YGAF, and the failure exposure time, t, 



as shown in equation 2. 



 𝑃𝑓𝐶𝐶𝐹
= 𝜆0 ∗ 𝑌𝐺𝐴𝐹 ∗ 𝑡 (2) 



The GAF values are obtained using an Excel workbook 



tool called Global Alpha Model Uncertainty Tool (GAMUT) 



2012. This tool was created by the NASA Johnson Space 



Center (JSC) Safety and Mission Assurance (S&MA) in 



support of the Space Shuttle and International Space Station 



(ISS) PRAs. GAMUT uses the 2012 version of NUREG/CR-



5496 as its source for the mean and uncertainty of the global 



alpha parameters. Specifically, it uses generic alpha values 



as opposed to specific values (e.g., pump values, valve 



values, etc.). These generic values are provided by 



NUREG/CR-5496 for groups of redundant components up 



to eight. GAMUT uses regression to determine values for 



groups of more than eight [9]. 



The output from GAMUT is the GAF mean value and a 



Beta parameter which defines the GAF uncertainty. Within 



SAPHIRE, these values will define the model of the specific 



CCF event. The standard operation of GAMUT is for all 



combinations of components for the specified redundancy 



level and number of component failures required to result in 



system failure, but the tool can be adjusted to output the GAF 



data for a specific set of combinations [10]. 



3.3 Software Failure Event Modeling 



SLS FSW is flight critical code. Identical copies of the 



code are loaded into all three FCs that make up the Flight 



Computer Operational Group (FCOG). The current SLS 



PRA approach for assessing FSW failure is described in 



Novack et al [11]. 



The quantification of the FSW risk follows the SLS 



PRA modeling practice, which was based on Space Shuttle 



Orbiter historical data, as described in the Primary Avionics 



Software System (PASS) PRA [12]. The PASS PRA 



estimated the probability for a Shuttle PASS failure during a 



nominal mission resulting in a LOC event. The PASS PRA 



defined a Source Line Of Code (SLOC) as the smallest 



increment of source code that can be compiled into 



executable code by a compiler, and an error in a SLOC as a 



binary condition that either will or will not execute in a 



manner compliant with requirements. Each SLOC was 



treated as independent with a constant identical failure rate. 



Firmware is coding written to a hardware device’s non-



volatile memory. It is installed directly onto a piece of 



hardware and is essentially software that enables the 



hardware to run. Firmware elements, when identified in the 



FP/FN analysis, are treated similarly to FSW with the SLOC 



data and modeling approach, except the SLOC data is 



extracted from the design algorithm that programs the 



hardware. A coding language, Hardware Description 



Language (HDL), is compiled into a configuration file which 



can be loaded into the hardware. 



Within NASA PRA, the firmware risk may be modeled 



differently depending on data availability. In some cases the 



firmware failures are modeled as described above, while in 



other cases firmware failures are included within the 



hardware reliability assessments, or estimated as a 



percentage of the avionics box failure rate. The impact of 



modeling the firmware failures as hardware is the the the 



failure events inherit the properties of operational time and 



redundancy that the software models do not. 



For this FP/FN analysis, FSW and firmware failure 



events are modeled as the software failure rate, FRSW, 



multiplied by the SLOC of the software code or firmware 



design code, NSLOC, in equation 3.

3.4 Data Uncertainty 



To properly characterize and account for the state of 



knowledge in the underlying data used to model the fault tree 



basic events, an uncertainty distribution is applied to each of 



the failure rate point estimates. When possible, basic events 



that model the same failure rate and uncertainty are 



correlated. Partial correlation, events modeled unique from 



each other, is not possible with SAPHIRE and therefore 



uncertainty distributions may be lower than expected. 











 



 



Typically, the failure rate data are best characterized by 



a lognormal uncertainty distribution that is either based on 



existing SLS PRA data or uncertainty estimation guidelines. 



The lognormal uncertainty distribution can be characterized 



by an Error Factor (EF), which defines the dispersion about 



the median. NASA PRAs have used and currently use EF as 



defined by the ratio of the 95th percentile over the 50th 



percentile. 



When the failure rate data is derived or provided by the 



PRA group, its inherent failure rate uncertainty distribution 



is maintained. If the failure rate data is derived from other 



reliability reports (e.g., MIL-HDBK-217F) or domain 



expert’s inputs where no uncertainty distribution is 



available, then a NASA PRA based uncertainty assignment 



is based on engineering judgement. 



4 ANALYSES CONDUCTED 



Preliminary FP analyses conducted on several abort 



detection functions indicated that software failure risk 



contributions would dominate, and in some cases, be on the 



same level as the probability of the abort condition. In 



general, the software risk contributions were one to two 



orders of magnitude greater than the hardware failures. 



Because the FP/FN detection analyses were at a lower level 



than the SLS PRA studies, the assumption was that 



additional refinement of the failure data was required to 



provide a better assessment of software and hardware 



elements for the detection function. The following sections 



detail the refinements that were developed and applied. 



4.1 Detailed FSW Code Traces 



The SLS IAS discipline provided code traces through 



the actual flight software that handled or processed the 



measurements or status data monitored by the detection 



function. For each detection function case, the tracing 



process was conducted from input data processed by the 



Avionics Data Handler Input (ADHIN), to data qualification 



and consolidation, [13] to the data exchange process 



between the FCs by the Flight Computer Redundancy 



Management (FCRM) software, and finally to the detection 



and notification logic. For each area, a set of software 



functions and modules were identified that could result in 



the FP or FN outcome. Summing the SLOC counts for each 



trace provided the overall FSW SLOC specific to the 



detection function. This provided more refined code paths 



than was developed for the SLS PRA and resulted in lower 



SLOC counts. 



4.2 Estimation of Logical SLOC Data 



For the FP/FN analysis, Logical SLOC (LSLOC) counts 



were used instead of SLOC counts as they focused the 



failure model on the executable portions of the software, 



reducing the sensitivity to the coding style convention and 



code formatting. The FSW compiling package reported an 



estimated SLOC and LSLOC count for each software 



function, and the software trace SLOC counts were simply 



converted into LSLOC counts. 



4.3 Update of Software Failure Rate 



The initial software failure rate estimated for the SLS 



PRA modeling was based on the PASS PRA estimation. The 



SLS PRA has implemented a new process for approximating 



the software failure rate using the commercial Softrel 



FREstimate Predictor software reliability tool. The total time 



under test as well as total number of defects found in each 



software version release, along with general software 



development information are input into Frestimate. 



Frestimate then compares this to common statistical models 



of software development to quantify expected defect density 



and rate of failure. This update to the FP/FN analyses alone 



reduced the failure rate and hence the software risk 



contributions by an order of magnitude. 



4.4 Common Failure Split Fractions CFSF) 



As was stated earlier, each monitored condition was 



modeled and assessed independently and separately, with 



the intention of combining the individual fault trees into a 



total integrated fault tree that would represent the total 



FP/FN impact of the SLS FM. This was done to evaluate the 



risk of each detection function individually to allow for a 



direct risk-to-benefit informed assessment. 



One issue with this approach is that common hardware 



and software failures are inherent across the fault trees. In an 



integrated fault tree, the proper use of a fault tree basic event 



naming convention will facilitate the Boolean reduction to 



remove these common events. Common Failure Split 



Fractions (CFSF) are employed within the fault trees to 



divide common failure events included in multiple FP and 



FN fault trees. The CFSF prevents the over-estimation of 



risk across the suite of detection functions, allowing the 



independent risk assessment of each detection function and 



the eventual merger of the fault trees to provide a risk 



assessment of the entire detection function suite. 



These CFSFs address two common failure modelling 



scenarios, failures that impact multiple detection functions 



directly, and failures that impact multiple detection 



functions exclusively. For each failure modeled in the FP/FN 



analyses, across all the detection functions analyzed, a CFSF 



is created. The CFSF is the inverse of the number of 



detection function fault trees (including the FP and FN fault 



trees) that the failure is included for the same time period. 



Because detection function time periods may differ in start 



and end times, and to reduce the analytical complexity, 



mission phases were used to consider the detection 



monitoring overlap. 



5 CURRENT FP/FN ASSESSMENT RESULTS AND 



COMMENTS 



Current analyses conducted for the SLS Artemis II 



mission have provided risk contributions for the 











 



 



implemented FM detection function, as well as the relative 



contributions of software and hardware elements of each 



abort detection function and most safing response detection 



functions. With the updates described in previous sections, 



the primary risk drivers for these functions are flight 



software failures, firmware design failures, and hardware 



CCFs. The results provide a more reasonable estimate of the 



software and firmware risk, and do not eclipse the hardware 



risk estimations. 



Still, there are concerns that the software risks are 



overestimated. Software is represented as a demand failure, 



meaning that it only fails when used, like a valve, hence no 



time is included in the failure risk calculation. For a 



detection function, the ‘demand’ is every processing cycle, 



and yet the risk estimate for a software failure is the same 



whether the condition is monitored for one second or one 



hour. Future FM FP/FN analyses will consider applying 



fractions based on the active processing time of the function 



over the entire mission to the software failure models. 



Additionally, software and firmware failures are 



modeled as a common cause failure across all the redundant 



devices, e.g., flight computers. In order to assess the risk 



impacts of the redundancy strategies for these devices, 



additional detail needs to be developed to estimate software 



failures that would result in independent failures. 



The issue of how to properly model software and 



firmware failure events and to integrate those models with 



hardware failure events is a continued focus going forward. 



More accurate failure modeling of these events would 



provide improved verification of design selections, and risk-



informed decisions in applying future development 



resources.
