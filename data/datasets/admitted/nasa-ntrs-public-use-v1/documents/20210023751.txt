The Light Microscopy Module (LMM) is a modified commercial, highly flexible,

state-of-the-art light imaging microscope facility that provides researchers with

powerful diagnostic hardware and software onboard the International Space Station

(ISS). The LMM enables novel research of microscopic phenomena in microgravity,

with the capability of remotely acquiring and downloading digital images and videos

across many levels of magnification. The way that matter is organized and moves

on the microscopic level profoundly affects the macroscopic world and an

understanding of such processes helps scientists and engineers build more efficient

materials and machines for both Earth and space environments.

• CVB is a basic scientific study in interfacial phenomena,

phase-change interfaces. The information obtained from

the study optimizes the design and operation of passive

heat transfer devices for Earth and microgravity

environments and is critically important for the successful

completion of long-term lunar and Mars missions.

ACE–M2R (David Weitz and Peter Lu, Harvard University) [From final report]

The ACE–M2R samples have been equilibrating on the ISS for several years. Recently, ACE–M2R collected a large and spectacular set of 3D

confocal microscopy data on a colloid-polymer sample that has been kinetically-arrested in microgravity.

Our ACE-M-2/R observations represent a “final state” of evolution, as the sample has undergone kinetic arrest and sat in stowage for 
several years and is no longer evolving. Previous determinations of when a sample stops evolving, i.e., kinetic arrest, have been made 
on timescales of days to weeks; our ISS-based experiment thereby extends that time horizon by several orders of magnitude. Strikingly, 
the sample has developed network-like structures with angular corners and sharp edges, as shown in the figure below, which we 
resolve with micron-scale resolution in full 3D; these structures are quite different from those seen in the mixtures with similar 
composition that we observed with photography in the BCAT series of experiments (BCAT-3, BCAT-4, BCAT-5, and BCAT-6). In those 
cases, the samples separated into two phases, and the interfaces between the two remained smooth, and the structures formed 
evolved constantly until there was one cm-scale spherical droplet of one phase surrounded by the other.

• This experiment involves the design and assembly of

complex 3D structures from small particles suspended

within a fluid medium. These so-called “self-assembled

colloidal structures,” are vital to the design of advanced

optical materials.

• In the microgravity environment, where 3D structures are

formed, insight will be provided into the relation between

particle shape, crystal symmetry, and structure: a

fundamental issue in condensed-matter science. ACE–T7

demonstrates the synthesis and 3D assembly of uniform

fluorescent hollow silica microcubes in microgravity using

depletion interactions.

• On Earth, assembly is 2D as a result of rapid

sedimentation due to gravity. Thus, experiments were

necessarily conducted in microgravity aboard the ISS are

necessary to probe the nucleation and growth of crystals in

3D space. After performing confocal microscope z-stacks

in a few regions in Capillary 3, we noticed that crystals

have grown much larger, and crystals in the bulk volume

seem to exist.

• The Advanced Colloids Experiment-Temperature-2

experiment looks at the assembly of complex

structures from micron-scale colloidal particles

interacting via tunable attractive interactions.

• The samples contain suspensions of trifluoroethyl

methacrylate (FEMA) colloidal particles (10 percent

volume) of type A and B in binary solvents

composed of water (H2O, 68 percent mass) and

lutidine (32 percent mass), that upon nearing the

critical solvent temperature (Tc ~32 °C) give rise to

critical Casimir interactions between the particles.

• Regulating the temperature enables control of the

particle interactions, which for these mixtures of

particles A and B are different, leading to the growth

of complex structures, and provide a better

understanding of how complex interactions lead to

complex structures, and to understand the dynamics

of growth of these structures.

Studying Nano-particle Haloing

ACE-T12-2

The three ACE-T12-2 samples have different 

concentrations that behave very differently

´

This experiment investigates the role of smaller charged
nanoparticles (of different concentrations in the three
capillaries) in enhancing the stability of larger particles (of the
same concentration in the three capillaries) and/or enhancing
the crystallinity of aggregates. This interaction is also known as
nanoparticle haloing (NPH). We have mixed and imaged three
capillaries. The present observations have been at ambient
temperature and are being used to develop the analysis
processes to automatically identify colloidal aggregates
through a computer program specifically designed for these
experiments. The results can then be used to not only
determine the size of and spacing between aggregates, but to
also determine their movement over both short and long-time
scales (see figure below). Advances in the understanding of
Nano-particle haloing could have a significant role in improving
the efficiency of solar arrays. This work has the potential to
increase the collection efficiency of solar collectors for adding
charge carriers, moving the present 13-15% collection
efficiency closer to the theoretical value of 30-40%. Additional
observations are being made to record any changes in the
physics guiding NPH after a sample temperature change from
ambient to 50 °C has been introduced. The Science Team,
Professors Stuart Williams and Gerold Willing and Master’s
candidate Adam Cecil are from the University of Louisville
(Kentucky) and are sponsored by NASA Established Program to
Stimulate Competitive Research (EPSCoR).

The second module for the Advanced Colloids

Experiment with Temperature Control - 4 (ACE-T4-2)

completed the first of three samples on August 5,

2020. This is the fourth of six samples. The remaining

two samples will be operated over the next 3 to 4

weeks at the Glenn ISS Payload Operations Center

(GIPOC). The test matrix has samples of different

concentrations and dopants that react at different

temperatures. As the temperature increases, the

particles get smaller creating a stress throughout the

gel structure and ultimately cracks the gel. Certain

additives (dopants) can influence gel microstructure

and hold potential to affect failure mechanisms. The

samples are observed in real time using the GRC-

managed Light Microscopy Module (LMM) and three-

dimensional (3-D) confocal scanning camera. The

understanding of the performance (and ultimate

failure) of gel structures affects everyday consumer

products and new technologies such as 3-D printing.

In space, stresses on these gel structures can be

observed on a timescale thousands of times longer

than on Earth.

Summary of ACE-T4-2 Activity August 19, 2020.
Tonight we will finish the second week of measurements on Sample Module 2 for ACE-T4-
2. Our primary accomplishment this week was to complete a set of dynamics experiments
on Capillary 1 of Sample Module 2. We also obtained low (2.5x objective) and high (100x
oil objective) magnification images of Capillary 2 and Capillary 3, which will be useful for
designing experiments for week three.

Per Capillary 1, the work this week built on week one work, which obtained
3D static images of the steady-state average microstructure of the colloidal gel in Capillary
1 as a function of internal stress (which is controlled by sample temperature).
The dynamics experiments of week 2 investigated several issues associated with the
temporal and spatial fluctuations of these “average” microstructures. The first set of
studies in the second week generated “movies” of the vibrational motions of the individual
particles in the gels at a fixed temperature (fixed packing and internal stress). The
correlations of the particle motions in these movies enable us to determine the so-called
phonon modes of the gel. The softest modes, i.e., modes with lowest energies, are often
indicative of the optimal reconfiguration route when the sample changes state. The second
set of dynamics experiments directly measured the microstructure reconfigurations
following a step-change in internal stress (a sample temperature jump). Again, this is
another way to learn about how gels respond to mechanical stress. A third set of
experiments explored dynamics and microstructure in samples with gradients of internal
strain/stress, i.e., brought about by spatial temperature gradients along the capillary.
These data will require extensive image analysis, but our preliminary observations clearly
show that the more porous gels that we see at higher temperature exhibit collective
dynamical fluctuations involving large groups (branches) of bonded particles. In
the Figure right, we show two images of the gel in Capillary 1 at 32 degrees: a typical
unprocessed black-and-white image, which is what the camera sees, along with a
preliminary 3D rendering of the gel.

In total, this research will teach us about the structure and dynamics of gels. Gels are
important materials that not only find applications in everyday products such as
toothpaste and pharmaceutical gel capsules, but also in high-tech processing schemes such
as those that arise in 3D-printers. Our work will provide information about the
microstructure of gels, and about how gels evolve and break in response to internal
stresses that are different from stresses due to gravity that arise on earth. These insights,
enabled by microgravity, could be useful for the design of tougher and more durable gels.

Arjun Yodh, Xiaoguang Ma, Chandan Mishra, Analisa Hill University 
of Pennsylvania (PENN)

Front-Cover of Langmuir:

The use of microbots created from micron-size particles to create lassos that can be used to transport micron-sized cargo and release it is 

highlighted in the June 13 (2017) issue of Langmuir (and on its front-cover) [1].  This approach successfully loaded cargo ranging from 1.8 

to 13.2 μm using a 16-mer (colloid particle) lasso whose largest diameter is ∼13.6 μm under a weak field of 3.3 mT. The authors  show that 
these magnetic lassos can roll at substantial velocities, with precise spatial control by manipulating both field strength and phase lag. 

Moreover, the lasso can curl around and capture cargo tightly and transport it based on a wheel-type mechanism. At the targeted destination, 

cargo is easily released upon field removal and the lasso can be readily reused. Since the entire process is physically controlled with no 

chemistry for attachment or disengagement involved, this system can potentially be used for transporting diverse types of cargo under 

different solution conditions.  The upcoming Advanced Colloids Experiment (ACE) - ACE-T9 flight experiment will use magnetic fields 

and the future ACE-E3 flight experiment plans to use electric fields in microgravity (in the absence to sedimentation) to pursue the creation 

of 3D controllable microstructures.  This work by David Marr, Ning Wu (Colorado School of Mines), et al., acknowledges partial support 

through NASA Grant NNX13AQ54G.

After cycling the temperature (that moved the bubble) Nematic
ordering of elliptical colloidal particles and associated defects
are now being seen in ACE-TR-2, capillary 3.

For the ACE-TR-2 (Ellipsoids) flight experiment [NJIT

(Boris Khusid) and NYU (Paul Chaikin and Andrew

Hollingsworth)] running on the International Space Station

(ISS), a large bubble developed in sample module 2,

capillary 3 (SM2, C3). This capillary is showing bubble

motion due to expansion or generated flow (possibly

Marangoni forces) which drives motion of the particles. In

the absence of the bubble, we do not see particle

movement with temperature change or gradient because

there is no thermophoresis (no Soret effect).

This work provides understanding about concentrated

colloidal suspensions of elliptical particles and models the

behavior of liquid crystals and their defects (called

Hedgehogs), which are well below the resolution of an

optical microscope. These results may also be important

because of potential uses in photonics.
