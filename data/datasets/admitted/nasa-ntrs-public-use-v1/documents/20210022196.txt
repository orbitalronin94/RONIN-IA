NASA’s Space Nuclear Propulsion Project

• The MSFC Space Nuclear Propulsion (SNP) project has the responsibility for managing 
nuclear fission-related propulsion systems (nuclear electric propulsion (NEP) and nuclear 
thermal propulsion (NTP)) for NASA Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD)

• The SNP project spent the last year identifying specific, high-confidence, viable NEP 
system design options to develop an understanding of the tasks required to mature critical 
technologies

• Technology maturation solutions should
‒ Take advantage of recent and ongoing investments
‒ Where feasible, advance a strategy that aligns with industry participation and identify key 

areas for such alignment and collaboration

• Simpler options may win out over higher performing options
• Other factors to consider: manufacturability, operability, testability, cost, industry alignment, 

other investments

Recent Findings Related to Nuclear Propulsion

Mission Studies
• Past and recent mission studies for NEP-powered human Mars missions put the required thruster 

power throughput on the order of MWe for a duration of 2-3 years
‒ Possibly lower power and longer duration for deep space science missions

• For the given readiness levels of the technologies, this order of magnitude power number is sufficiently 
refined to start technology development and maturation

1 “Space Nuclear Propulsion for Human Mars Exploration,” National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2021.

STMD Nuclear Propulsion Plan must be responsive to the recommendations of recent 
high-level independent reviews for future NASA mission needs

Recent non-advocate studies indicate significant technology investment is 
needed to overcome low technology readiness for critical subsystems 
• National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Medicine1: Low and intermittent 

investment in NEP makes it unclear if critical subsystems can be developed to 
meet a NASA mission with timeline ~ 20 years 

• NASA’s Engineering & Safety Center: Most of the critical technologies are relatively 
immature, especially at the system level and the degree of difficulty to make 
advancements is considered high

CTE 1: Nuclear Reactor

Options
• Heat pipe, direct gas, or pumped liquid metal thermal transport
• HALEU (< 20% enrichment) UO2, UN, particle-based/micro-

engineered fuels
• Be-based, Hydride moderators

Preliminary results
• UO2 w/Be-based moderator may be sufficient for NEP 

‒ more complex options like UN or TRISO may not be necessary

• A heat pipe reactor may have sufficient heat transport at low 
relative reactor mass  to support multi-MW NEP (passive, low P)

Analytical hierarchy results for a lithium heat pipe reactor – from “Application of the Analytical 
Hierarchy Process for Narrowing Down NEP Candidate Reactor Designs,” Rao et al., in 
JANNAF Propulsion Meeting, June 2021.

Challenges
• Lifetime
• Fuel and moderator materials temperatures (coolant at 1200 K or above – higher T, 

generally smaller radiator)
• Thermal transport
• Internal Pressure* (* mainly issue for direct gas) Missouri S&T nuclear reactor

CTE 2: Dynamic Power Conversion

Options and Issues
• Several thermodynamic cycle-based conversion systems, but closed-cycle Brayton scales best for MWe power levels
• He-Xe mixture working fluid

‒ Noble gas mixture, systems implications well-understood for the space application, operated in ground test at up 
to 1200 K, has not operated at MWe power levels, larger/heavier turbomachinery

• Supercritical CO2 working fluid
‒ Higher pressures (~7-20 MPa), systems implications not well-understood for the space application, lighter 

turbomachinery, operated in ground test at MWe power levels, has operated only up to 925 K, molecular 
dissociation and corrosion properties not well understood at high temperatures, best performance requires 
compressor inlet at or near critical point (~304 K) → potentially large/cold radiator

Challenges
• Lifetime
• Efficient power extraction
• Inlet and heat exchanger materials temperatures
• Heat Exchanger Creep
• Compatibility with Reactor and Radiator needs
• Capability to handle transients

CTE 4: Electric Propulsion System

Options and Issues
• Lithium-fed MPD thruster

‒ 1 MW thrusters (est. T ≈ 30 N, Isp ≈ 4400 s)

‒ electrode lifetime, propellant feeding of a liquid metal, low pressure Li-liquid storage, relatively easy to 
condense/pump in a modest vacuum facility, PPU topology potentially leverages past high current thruster 
developments, very high current – isolation required between multiple thrusters

• Xenon-fed Hall thruster
‒ 100 kW thrusters (T ≤ 5.4 N, Isp ≤ 3000 s)
‒ cathode lifetime, high pressure (supercritical) Xe-gas storage in large tanks, relatively difficult to 

condense/cryopump in even a very large vacuum facility, Direct-Drive Unit (DDU) PPU topology never before 
fabricated/tested – should be easier than present DC-DC Hall thruster PPUs, regulated discharge voltage –
isolation required between multiple thrusters

Challenges
• Lifetime
• Power throughput in a reasonably-sized package
• Stable operation 
• Ground testability – correlation with flight data
• Power Processing Unit (PPU) design and components availability 200 kW MAI Li-MPD

50 kW NASA-457M v2 Xe-Hall
