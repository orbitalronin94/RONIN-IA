<p>Introduction
Mars Sample Return (MSR) is a NASA and ESA campaign planning to bring samples of Mars 
rocks and atmosphere back to Earth. 
</p>
<p>Overall concept used for these analyses was to return samples to Earth using three missions: 
◦ Sample collection: NASA’s Perseverance rover, launched in 2020, currently operational on Mars 
</p>
<p>collecting and caching samples
◦ Sample retrieval: a rocket delivered by a lander would launch the samples into Mars orbit
◦ Sample return: Earth Return Orbiter (ERO) would secure the Mars samples in Mars orbit using the 
</p>
<p>Capture, Containment, and Return System (CCRS), and bring them back to Earth.
◦ MSR campaign architecture currently undergoing reassessment 
</p>
<p>CCRS would capture the Orbiting Sample container, orient it, sterilize its exterior, and transfer 
it to the Earth Entry System (EES) for return to Earth. 
</p>
<p>When near Earth, the EES would separate from the CCRS, enter the Earth’s atmosphere, and 
land. </p>

<p>The Risk of Potential Biological Contamination
Backward planetary protection (BPP) requirements are in place to prevent introduction of 
uncontained or unsterilized Martian material to Earth’s biosphere
◦ Prevent exposure to possible extraterrestrial biology
</p>
<p>Exposure a possibility due to impacts from meteoroids
◦ EES structural integrity could be compromised during its return
◦ Damage to the heat shield could cause break-up during entry with potential dispersal of samples in 
</p>
<p>the atmosphere
</p>
<p>To assess the risks associated with potential meteoroid damage, NASA develops equations 
that predict what level of damage the various EES elements may sustain as a result of such 
an impact. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>7
</p>
<p>Why Are We Here?
Ballistic Limit Equations (BLEs): Equations used to predict if a particular system or structural 
element will sustain a critical failure following a hypervelocity impact
◦ Essential for of spacecraft system design
◦ Used in quantitative spacecraft meteoroid and orbital debris risk assessments 
</p>
<p>BLEs are specific for the structural element or system used in the test programs that 
generate the data upon which they are based 
</p>
<p>Also a function of the failure criteria 
◦ Failure criteria—used to define failure thresholds for the particular system or structural element 
</p>
<p>under consideration
</p>
<p>In this presentation
◦ Review recent progress in the development of the BLEs for the EES forebody and aftbody
◦ Present an overview of the processes used to develop such equations and offer some insights into 
</p>
<p>the next stages of development for these BLEs. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>8
</p>
<p>Ballistic Limit Equations 101
There are two basic types of BLEs
◦ Type 1: statistically derived from damage predictor equations 
◦ Type 2: use fail/no fail (or P/NP) data points and fitting an equation between them
</p>
<p>P/NP data produced using hypervelocity impact testing and hydrocode simulations
◦ Smooth Particle Hydrodynamics Code (SPHC) hydrocode primarily used for these BLEs 
</p>
<p>An effective BLE has all P (failure) data points above the BLE curve and all NP(no failure) 
data points below the curve.
◦ Plot of a BLE is a Ballistic Limit Curve (BLC)</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>9
</p>
<p>Type 1 BLE
Based on a damage predictor equation
◦ Typically a statistically based curve-fit to damage measurements 
◦ Example: equation that predicts penetration depth into a structural element in terms of impact 
</p>
<p>parameters, material properties, and geometries involved.
</p>
<p>Once a critical acceptable damage level is identified, this equation is manipulated into a form 
to yield the particle diameter whose impact would result in the specified maximum 
acceptable damage level. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>10
</p>
<p>Type 1 BLEs
For example, suppose we can write the following (empirically-derived) equation for 
penetration depth:
</p>
<p>penetration depth = f(proj diam, other impact parameters, material properties, etc)
</p>
<p>Next, impose a failure criterion, such as a maximum allowable penetration depth
</p>
<p>Finally, solve for proj diam in terms of max allowable pen depth, etc to get an equation that 
looks like
</p>
<p>crit proj diam = g(max allowable pen depth, other impact parameters, material properties, etc)</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>11
</p>
<p>Type 2 BLEs
Based on a “hand-drawn” discriminant line
◦ An example of this type of BLE is one that separates regions of projectile diameter and impact 
</p>
<p>velocity combinations that result in perforation of a dual-wall Whipple Shield from those that do not. 
◦ These are the kinds of BLEs used for some of the shields on the International Space Station (ISS). 
</p>
<p>Unlike BLEs based on damage predictor equations, discriminant line BLEs are not 
statistically derived curve-fits.
◦ Lines of demarcation between regions where something either does or does not happen based on a 
</p>
<p>specified failure criterion. 
</p>
<p>BLEs discussed today are all Type 2</p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>Forebody Multi-Shock Shield (MSS) BLE
The meteoroid shield protecting the EES forebody TPS consists of alternating layers of a 
shocking material and a foam filler—“multi-shock shield” 
</p>
<p>The forebody MSS BLE was initially developed by: 
◦ Combining two previous multi-shock BLEs into a single BLE, and 
◦ Adjusting its coefficients so that it fit P/NP test results for other shield systems.
</p>
<p>Further adjustments to this BLE using the results of SPHC and ALE3D hydrocode simulations
◦ At impact velocities beyond the testable regime 
◦ To include others projectile materials including ice, aluminum, dunite, and iron. </p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>Comments 
Overall, this new, more general, MSS BLE compares favorably with hydrocode and impact test 
results. 
</p>
<p>This expanded BLE is now applicable to: 
◦ MSS designs with materials other than those used to develop it,
◦ Impact velocities beyond the testable regime, and 
◦ Wider range of projectile materials. 
</p>
<p>This more generalized form of the MSS BLE can be used in trade studies that are performed 
as part of efforts to select multi-shock shield designs for future spacecraft.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>17
</p>
<p>Forebody MSS+TPS System BLE
Forebody shield+TPS system consists of an MSS with TPS standing off at a distance beneath 
it
◦ Does not include the supporting structure behind the TPS
</p>
<p>Applicable for impact velocities of 1-60 km/s, and impact obliquities of 0°-45°
</p>
<p>BLE is sufficiently flexible so that it can be used for configuration design trade studies.
</p>
<p>Two versions of the BLE were developed for two failure criteria—depending on location on 
EES
◦ 50% of TPS thickness penetration depth
◦ 100%  of TPS thickness penetration depth</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>18
</p>
<p>Forebody MSS+TPS BLE Development
Step 0 – Determine if the impacting projectile has perforated the forebody shield. 
◦ Use the forebody MSS BLE described previously. 
◦ If it has not perforated, there can be no damage to the TPS. If it has, then assume some remnant of 
</p>
<p>the original projectile will impact the TPS
</p>
<p>Step 1 – If there is a forebody shield perforation (Step Zero), calculate size and speed of the 
largest fragment exiting the forebody shield. 
◦ Accomplished by equivalencing the multilayer forebody shield with a single aluminum shield having a 
</p>
<p>thickness that yields the same areal density as the forebody shield
</p>
<p>Step 2 – Using the size and speed characteristics of the largest perforating fragment from 
Step 1, calculate the depth of penetration of that largest fragment into the TPS. 
◦ Use an existing empirical TPS penetration depth predictor equation.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>19
</p>
<p>Forebody MSS+TPS BLE Development
Step 3 – Calibrate the penetration depth predictor model developed in Step 2 against 
penetration depth values from hydrocode and testing. 
◦ Modify the existing TPS penetration depth predictor equation from Step 2 so that it will yield TPS 
</p>
<p>depth predictions that are closer to the values predicted by hydrocode and observed in testing. 
</p>
<p>Step 4 – Compare the calibrated TPS penetration depths against an allowable maximum TPS 
penetration depth value (50% or 100% of TPS thickness)
◦ Assign a penetration (P)/no penetration (NP) value, depending on whether the allowed penetration 
</p>
<p>depth has been exceeded, to the characteristics of the original impacting projectile. 
</p>
<p>Step 5 – Repeat Steps 1 through 4 for a range of projectile diameters, impact velocities, and 
trajectory obliquities to obtain the full BLE.
</p>
<p>19</p>
<p></p>

<p>Forebody MSS+TPS BLE Modifications
MSS+TPS BLE was originally developed using only aluminum projectiles and based on test 
results at impact velocities near 7 km/s and hydrocode predictions
</p>
<p>Additional term added to account for projectile materials other than aluminum
◦ Based on results obtained using SPHC and ALE3D hydrocodes. 
</p>
<p>Recent modifications include terms that account for TPS thickness and stand-off distance 
between the rear of the shield and the forebody TPS.</p>

<p>Aftbody Shield BLE
Minimal data were available regarding the P/NP performance of a shield having a 
construction similar to the EES baseplate, which functioned as the aftbody shield. 
</p>
<p>The baseplate comprises two thin composite plates standing off at a small distance from 
each other, with the space between them filled with a low-density non-metallic foam.
</p>
<p>Similar types of MMOD shield constructions have been examined (1) in testing performed in 
the 1960s and 1970s, and (2) using the SPHC hydrocode with a different low-density foam 
filler material.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>23
</p>
<p>Aftbody Shield BLE Development
Start with the New Nonoptimum (NNO) BLE—an existing dual-wall shield BLE
</p>
<p>Modify NNO coefficients to predict P/NP test results and SPHC predictions for MMOD shields 
having a construction similar to that of the aftbody shield 
</p>
<p>Several new terms were also added to take into account the trends observed in hydrocode 
simulations and in test results when non-aluminum projectiles and wall materials were used. </p>

<p>Aftbody Shield + TPS System BLE
The development of this BLE would have proceeded using the process used in the 
development of the forebody MSS+TPS BLE. 
</p>
<p>Work on this BLE would have begun once a preliminary design had been finalized and initial

<p>Conclusions
NASA and ESA are currently designing the Mars Sample Return campaign, which is intended 
to return samples to Earth 
</p>
<p>There is a concern regarding the risk of potential biological contamination by returned 
Martian samples in the event of a perforation resulting from a meteoroid impact. 
</p>
<p>To assess the risks associated with such an event, NASA is developing equations to predict 
◦ The damage that spacecraft elements would sustain following such an impact
◦ Whether or not a particular system will sustain a critical failure following such an impact. 
</p>
<p>The BLEs developed have flexibility to easily modify parameters to account for some changes 
in shield configurations.
◦ Trade studies of the forebody multi-shock configuration varied materials, spacing, and foam filler
◦ Baseplate materials, including aluminum and multi-layer insulation were also considered
</p>
<p>Any changes made to the configurations would require BLE modification with new hydrocode 
and testing data.</p>
