<p>Stennis Space Center
Definitions of Autonomous Systems
</p>
<p>Understanding Automated and Autonomous Systems and Thinking Autonomous Systems
</p>
<p>Fernando Figueroa and Lauren Underwood
</p>
<p>Autonomous Systems Laboratory
</p>
<p>NASA Stennis Space Center
</p>
<p>• Definition: An Automated System follows a script, potentially a very sophisticated script (e.g. operational sequences), in which all 
</p>
<p>allowable courses of action have explicitly been defined and programmed ahead of time. If the system encounters unanticipated situations, 
</p>
<p>the outcomes may be unpredictable, or the system may be programmed to stop and wait for human intervention (e.g. it “phones home”). 
</p>
<p>For automated systems, choices have either already been made, and encoded, or must be externally made. 
</p>
<p>• Definition: Autonomy is the ability of a system to achieve goals while operating independently of external control (NASA
</p>
<p>Technology Roadmaps, TA 4: Robotics and Autonomous Systems, 2015). Autonomy requires self-awareness (to understand its health
</p>
<p>condition and resources to operate), self-directedness (to achieve goals) and self-sufficiency (to operate independently). An autonomous
</p>
<p>system applies strategies to mitigate any unforeseen events that may interfere with its current plans to achieve its goals. An autonomous
</p>
<p>system must encompass knowledge and understanding of the system (itself) that enables analysis, reasoning, and decision-making.
</p>
<p>• The classic approaches and technologies used to achieve autonomy have been denominated Brute-Force Autonomy (BFA). (BFA)
</p>
<p>consists of considering all possible cases for autonomous decisions, and then generating solutions offline. In BFA, these cases and
</p>
<p>solutions are then incorporated in the processor used for implementing autonomy; the processor simply chooses the decisions
</p>
<p>which correspond to each prescribed case. An interpretation of this methodology suggests that the “thinking” is done offline by
</p>
<p>experts.
</p>
<p>• BFA has significant shortcomings: (1) cases and solutions are not comprehensive, which results in limited autonomy, which
</p>
<p>consequently exposes risks for potentials failures; (2) software complexity becomes literally unmanageable for the levels of
</p>
<p>autonomy required for Gateway and planetary outposts which do not have timely ground control support; and (3) evolve-
</p>
<p>ability is prohibitive and/or not achievable, due to complexity and cost.
</p>

<p>Stennis Space Center
Definitions of Autonomous Systems (cont.)
</p>
<p>• The future of “true” autonomous systems requires independent (on-board) thinking in order to eliminate the need for 1- persistent
</p>
<p>updates and 2- accounting for unforeseen oversight made by humans. A “Thinking System” encompasses the following
</p>
<p>capabilities: Reason, Intellect, Understanding, Decision, Presentation, Sensory, and Will (actions); and a language founded on a
</p>
<p>sound ontology. The approach and technologies associated with “Thinking Autonomy” (TA) enable the “thinking” to be done on-
</p>
<p>board. The code itself transfers to the system the knowledge and “thinking” capabilities of human experts. That is, self-awareness,
</p>
<p>self-directedness, and self-sufficiency, are not hard-coded off-line; they are exercised on-board, using generic and systemic
</p>
<p>knowledge of a “true” autonomous system.
</p>
<p>• Definition: Hierarchical Distributed Autonomy occurs when an overall system is defined as a collection of autonomous systems,
</p>
<p>where there is a hierarchy that reflects default command authority relationships. For example, the Gateway may be an overall autonomous
</p>
<p>system that includes modules that are themselves autonomous systems. By default, the Gateway has command authority over its modules,
</p>
<p>as its function is to execute Gateway activities related to its mission.
</p>
<p>• Definition: Crewed Autonomy occurs when an autonomous system operates in collaboration with crew. This is considered a case of
</p>
<p>distributed autonomy where crew members are autonomous systems themselves. In this case, the expectation is that crew will have
</p>
<p>command authority over the autonomous system it occupies, or it will decide which member of the autonomous system has command
</p>
<p>authority at any given time.
</p>
<p>• Definition: Un-Crewed Autonomy occurs when an autonomous system functions without crew, in accordance with the definition of
</p>
<p>Autonomy, as provided above; and also operates according to the definition of Hierarchical Distributed Autonomy, as un-crewed.
</p>
<p>3National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>An autonomous system includes on-board knowledge (about systems topology, functions, behaviors; 
about strategies to detect and mitigate anomalies; about concepts of operations and policies; about 
</p>
<p>mission planning and  execution) and knows how to use that knowledge to achieve its missions.</p>

<p>Stennis Space Center

<p>Digital Twins (live): NASA Platform for Autonomous Systems 
</p>
<p>(NPAS)
</p>
<p>• NASA Stennis Space Center has developed and continues to evolve the 
</p>
<p>NASA Platform for Autonomous Systems (NPAS) 
</p>
<p>(https://techport.nasa.gov/view/94884)
</p>
<p>• NPAS-based autonomous operations utilize a software domain reference 
</p>
<p>model—a ”digital twin” (DT) for mission management as well as for 
</p>
<p>detection, diagnosis/prognosis, and resolution of anomalies
</p>
<p>• NPAS supports the creation of such domain specific DTs using built-in and 
</p>
<p>extensible component model libraries. These model libraries are typically 
</p>
<p>designed to match the level of representation included in provided 
</p>
<p>schematics and Interface Control Documents (ICDs)
</p>
<p>• One benefit of the digital twin model is the provision of a dynamic system 
</p>
<p>state model that the autonomous software can reference in real-time. This 
</p>
<p>enables the software to make use of both topological and operational context 
</p>
<p>when employing its reasoning logic
</p>
<p>• NPAS Digital Twin domain representations are 
</p>
<p>– ontological (e.g., they are composed of model constructs that map 
</p>
<p>well to the ontologies present within the systems, processes, and 
</p>
<p>equipment)
</p>
<p>– topological (e.g., they include the components, the interconnections, 
</p>
<p>and the associations between the components in an operational 
</p>
<p>context), and 
</p>
<p>– dynamic (e.g., they are populated with live telemetry data and real-
</p>
<p>time state assessment regarding health and operational availability). 
</p>
<p>– The representations are incorporated into the autonomous system 
</p>
<p>manager software architecture seamlessly so that regardless of 
</p>
<p>domain, all applicable measurement data can be properly integrated 
</p>
<p>into a state model.
</p>
