<p>TECHNICAL MEMORANDUM
</p>
<p>DAMAGE TOLERANCE STUDY OF CARBON FIBER/EPOXY SANDWICH STRUCTURE 
IN SUPPORT OF THE SLS PAYLOAD ADAPTER FITTING (PAF)
</p>
<p>1.  INTRODUCTION
</p>
<p> A payload adaptor fitting (PAF) has been manufactured at NASA’s Marshall Space Flight 
Center (MSFC) in support of the Space Launch System (SLS) program. This structure is essen-
tially a large cone made up of eight curved “lobes” that are joined together. The lobes were manu-
factured via automated fiber placement (AFP). The PAF will carry predominantly compressive 
loads, thus compression strength will be the focus of this study. The lobes are a sandwich structure 
made with aluminum honeycomb and carbon/epoxy face sheets. The lobes will be inserted into 
metallic rings at the top and bottom to form the full cone. Both front and back exterior surfaces of 
each lobe have a plain weave fabric placed at ±45° to the vertical direction. The face sheet of each 
lobe is thickest at top and plies are dropped as the cone reaches the main acreage in the bottom of 
the cone.
</p>
<p> Compression after impact (CAI) strength of sandwich structure is one of the leading met-
rics being used to evaluate suitability of the PAF for the SLS program. This study was undertaken 
to evaluate the compression strength of sections of acreage of the PAF that have been damaged by 
impact. 
</p>
<p> One aspect that needed to be addressed was where to evaluate impact damage on any of the 
lobes. In general, a part made from the sandwich structure is most vulnerable where the face sheets 
are thinnest. This is the bottom half  of the structure, which also represents the area most likely to 
experience foreign object impact damage since it consumes the most area of the conical structure. 
Thus, the core and lay-up that makes up the bottom half  of the structure were chosen.
</p>
<p> Another aspect that needed to be considered was where along the bottom section of the 
lobe to take the specimens from. The lobes were manufactured with the load bearing 0° fibers 
aligned in the same direction (not radially) with the center fibers being parallel to the loading direc-
tion, as sketched in figure 1. Thus, most of the lobe has fibers that will be loaded off-axis to the 
0° direction. Since the load introduction into the PAF will be displacement driven by two metallic 
end rings being compressed toward one another, the off-axis regions will experience less load than 
the centerline of the lobe since the lay-up of the face sheets contain more 0° plies than other angles 
(i.e., it is highly directional in the 0° direction). Thus, the center section where the fibers are parallel 
to the loading direction was chosen since this area will be the most highly loaded and would rep-
resent a worst-case scenario for location of damage. In addition, “off-axis” loading was shown to 
have similar CAI strength to “on-axis” loading in a previous study.1</p>

<p>2.  MATERIALS
</p>
<p>The sandwich structure used in this study was manufactured by co-curing the face sheets to 
aluminum honeycomb core. The core was 0.625-in thick and had a density of 4.5 lb/ft3. All the face 
sheets were manufactured by AFP at NASA’s MSFC. The T1100/3690 carbon/epoxy prepreg tape 
that made up the face sheets had an areal weight of 190 g/m2. The honeycomb sandwich structure 
was manufactured with the core ribbon (“L”) direction aligning with the 0° fiber direction. The 8 
ply face sheets had a lay-up of [±45f /+45/ 0 /–45/ 0 /–45/ 0 /+45]T. The outer ply of fabric was a plain 
weave T1100/3690 that was hand laid up on the tool before the fiber placement of the tape began 
for the outer (tool side) surface of the cone. Another ply of the plain weave was placed on the inner 
surface of the cone (bag side) before bagging and curing. The sandwich structure had a layer of 
FM300-2M epoxy film adhesive placed between the core and face sheet during the automated tape 
laying process used to manufacture the face sheets.
</p>
<p>The sandwich structure was cured in an autoclave with a pressure of 40 psi and a tempera-
ture of 350 °F. The flat sandwich panels made for use in this study were 24-in by 48-in in size. The 
sandwich structure showed good consolidation with very little porosity as shown in figure 2. Typi-
cal fiber waviness of the face sheets on the honeycomb core panels was noted. The thickness values 
of the face sheets on the honeycomb panels varied from a minimum at the cell walls (tmin) to a 
maximum between the cell walls (tmax) as shown in the top cross-sectional photomicrograph in fig-
ure 2. A nominal value for the face sheet thickness can be found based on the average of numerous 
random thickness measurements.
</p>
<p> 
Using photomicroscopy and measuring tools contained within the software attached to the 
</p>
<p>microscope, the nominal face sheet thicknesses of the specimens tested was measured and found to 
be 0.05 in.</p>

<p>Once the epoxy cured, the top and bottom edges of these specimens were machined to 
±0.001-in tolerance of parallelism using a vertical end mill with a solid carbide cutting tool 
(Onsrud 67-526 designed for carbon fiber machining). The side edges of the specimens were 
machined to be perpendicular to the top and bottom edges.
</p>
<p>Undamaged strength testing of the honeycomb sandwich structure was not pursued in this 
study since the undamaged specimens exhibited end-brooming (even with potting the ends with 
epoxy) which is not a valid failure mode, and this study concerns damage tolerance testing; undam-
aged strength values are not relevant since the structure must be designed to damage tolerance 
strength values, not pristine values.
</p>
<p>2.1  Impact Damage Testing
</p>
<p>Each sandwich specimen was impacted at its geometric center on the tool side face sheet 
since the tool side is to be the outer side of the PAF structure and thus the most prone to impact 
damage. The impactor had a diameter of 0.5 in and each specimen was placed on a steel plate with 
a 2.5-in circular opening and clamped on top with a steel plate with a small circular opening to 
allow the impactor to pass through and hit the specimen. A blunt tipped impactor was chosen over 
a sharp tipped impactor since an impact by a sharp object is more readily visible and thus a blunt 
impactor represents a worst-case scenario when barely visible impact damage (BVID) is governing 
the initial inspection process.2 While the sandwich specimen has a high stiffness rigidity, it is not 
infinite and some flexing of the PAF honeycomb structure will occur during a foreign object impact 
event (unless the impact is at the bottom of the structure near where the steel ring is located). The 
boundary condition of the impact event was chosen to represent the actual structure as close as 
possible. Some flexure that may develop during the impact was allowed by the 2.5-in opening in the 
bottom plate. This may be important when considering BVID and the amount of damage associ-
ated with it.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>6
</p>
<p>An instrumented drop weight impact apparatus was used to inflict damage to the specimens. 
A picture of the impact tester used is shown in figure 4. The selected impact energies were based 
on what was determined to be BVID (with the peel ply still on the surface) to easily visible dam-
age without the peel ply present. Impact energy levels between these two extremes were chosen for 
the remainder of impacts on the sandwich specimens so that residual strength curves could be con-
structed. Results of the impact tests showing the impact energy used, number of specimens tested, 
width of damage as detected by thermography, the dent depth formed, and the maximum load of 
impact on the specimens are summarized in table 1. The dent measurements were taken at least 
24 hours after impact to allow for any “relaxation” of the dent depth. The maximum load of impact 
is the largest load recorded during the impact event.

<p>2.2  Visual Damage
</p>
<p>While other measured parameters in this study were not affected by the presence of peel 
ply, the amount of visible damage is highly dependent upon the presence of peel ply. Thus, there 
are two damage severity levels at which to define BVID: one with peel ply on the surface and one 
without peel ply. As will be seen, the peel ply greatly enhances the visibility of an impact event 
while removal of the peel ply leaves a matte black surface that tends to “hide” the damage.
</p>
<p>Examples of the visual damage caused by each of the five impact energies are shown in the 
photographs in figure 6. Visual damage both with and without the outer layer of peel ply present 
are shown. It should be noted that in practice the amount of visual damage in the field will vary 
depending upon such factors as the available lighting, the angle of the lighting, and the surface 
finish of the specimen.

<p>2.6  Compression After Impact Testing

<p>The impacted sandwich specimens were assessed for residual compression strength using

placed under the edge that was reading low until the gages were even. During compression testing, 
the gages were monitored and if  any deviation greater than 10% occurred, the test was stopped, 
and shims would be rearranged until the gages read within 10% of each other all the way until fail-

<p>2.7  Effect of Peel Ply
</p>
<p>One of the challenging aspects of this structure is that the outer layer of peel ply used dur-
ing manufacture is to remain on the PAF until integration at the launch site, at which time the peel 
ply will be removed. While the impact results on the sandwich specimens were expected to not be 
affected by the presence of the peel ply since one layer of a much tougher material than peel ply 
had no effect on damage resistance or CAI strength in a study on carbon/epoxy face sheet sand-
wich structure done decades ago,4 ten specimens were impact tested at one of the mid-level impact 
energies (10 ft-lb) both with and without peel ply to assess any differences in damage resistance and 
damage tolerance due to the presence of the peel ply. Details of the method of impacting the speci-
men, measuring the damage size and CAI strength, have been supplied in more detail earlier in this 
paper. Results from these tests are presented in table 3.</p>

<p>3.  CONCLUSIONS
</p>
<p> CAI strength versus impact energy and CAI strength versus damage size plots have been 
constructed to aid the PAF program office on decision making should the piece of hardware expe-
rience a foreign object impact. The data shows that the peel ply on the outer surface has a great 
effect on the amount of visible damage seen. The peel ply also influences the dent depth and CAI 
strength values. The peel ply causes a smaller dent depth but is easier to see than an equivalent 
impact on specimens without peel ply on the surface. The peel ply also tends to give the impacted 
sandwich structure higher CAI strength values, which is likely a result of the shallower dent depth 
and thus less damage.
</p>
<p> It is suggested that the B-Basis curve of figure 14 be used as a conservative measure of CAI 
strength based on the size of the damage as measured by thermography until further testing is con-
ducted.
</p>
<p> It is also suggested that more testing be done to vet the effect of the peel ply on the damage 
characteristics of this sandwich structure.</p>
