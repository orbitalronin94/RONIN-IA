<p>Insulation
</p>
<p>• Environment is everything
</p>
<p>• Conductive insulation works as a function of DT &amp; Tmean
</p>
<p>• Material properties change with both the gradient across the insulation 
specimen and the temperatures encompassed.
</p>
<p>• Lead to multiple different methodologies of testing.
</p>
<p>• Radiative insulations work as a function mainly of TH
</p>
<p>• Wavelength and temperature dependent properties can make solutions 
become complicated quickly.
</p>
<p>• Vacuum also plays a large role in system level performance.
</p>
<p>• A working tool box
• Different materials work in different situations
• No global solution
</p>

<p>Multilayer Insulation (MLI)
</p>
<p>• Fundamentally, MLI is an attempt to minimize all three forms of 
heat transfer:
</p>
<p>• Radiation: highly reflective layers stacked on top of each other
</p>
<p>• Conduction: reflective layers spaced by low conductivity spacer + low 
contact pressure between layers
</p>
<p>• Convection: always installed in a vacuum (&lt; 10-4 Torr)
• Performance of MLI at ambient pressure better than foam!
</p>
<p>• Key notes:
• Can’t use thermal conductivity (k-value) to define MLI performance
</p>
<p>• Performance varies with temperature as approximately T3
</p>
<p>• Good looking MLI =/= Good performance MLI
</p>
<p>• Cannot determine IR emissivity by looking at a material
</p>

<p>The Folly of 2nd Layer Reflectors
</p>
<p>• It is very hard to spot 2nd surface 
reflectors with the naked eye
when the substrate is transparent.
</p>
<p>• The easiest way to tell is that 
generally, 1st surface mirrors 
have a backing on the tape, 2nd
</p>
<p>surface mirrors don’t.
</p>
<p>• Substrate is either 
FEP or Polyimide
</p>
<p>• The radiative heat load onto a 
surface is proportional to the 
emissivity of the surface.

<p>General Conclusions on Controlling Temp / Press
</p>
<p>• If you want to control liquid temperature, but not ullage 
pressure, keep a refrigerated heat exchanger submerged.
</p>
<p>• If you want to control liquid temperature and vapor pressure, 
need the refrigerated heat exchanger exposed to both phases.
</p>
<p>• In hydrogen, need to determine appropriate surface area to effectively 
remove heat. It will probably be larger than you think.
</p>
<p>• Liquid hydrogen will stay saturated unless you intentionally drive it via 
ullage pressure.
</p>
<p>• For effectively heating liquid within a tank – heaters need to be 
as low on tank as possible.
</p>
<p>• Otherwise will have a section of the liquid below heater that is not 
easily heated with rest of liquid.
</p>

<p>Heat Load Reduction Via “Heat Intercept”
</p>
<p>• One method that is often used for heat load reduction is heat 
interception at an intermediate temperature. 
</p>
<p>• Cryocoolers get better performance at higher temperatures.
• Allows for any boil-off vapor to be used as forced convection coolant
• Either reduces boil-off or reduces heat load removal requirements at lower 
</p>
<p>temperatures (ultimately saving energy)
</p>
<p>• A key mistake often made in design and evaluation of such systems 
is to assume that the passive heat load and the load being reduced 
from are the same.
</p>
<p>• This is exacerbated by the general Key Parameter of heat load reduction from 
passive to heat intercept case.  
</p>
<p>• Severely complicates the testing approach in certain configurations.
• Has caused multiple PIs to overstate the predicted performance.
• You must reanalyze both sides of the cooling location to solve the energy 
</p>
<p>conservation in the heat intercept case.
</p>
<p>• That being said, “Heat Intercept” does work!</p>

<p>Fitting Testing
</p>
<p>• Recent testing of Swagelok VCR fittings down to 20 K showed very 
little leakage.
</p>
<p>1. Testing of three (3) different VCR fitting sizes: 1/4, 1/2, and 1 inch. 
</p>
<p>2. Five (5) samples of each fitting size 
</p>
<p>3. Two (2) different seal material types: SST and Ni. These materials were 
selected because they are the most compatible with the cryogenic space 
flight fluids. 
</p>
<p>4. Four (4) complete ambient (300K) to cryogenic (20-30K) thermal cycles in 
TVAC for each fitting size and seal material combination. Two (2) thermal 
cycles were performed before and two (2) cycles after vibration testing. 
</p>
<p>5. Vibration testing to relevant launch dynamic profile, see Section 9.1 Test 
Method. 
</p>
<p>6. Leak checking of fittings throughout the TVAC test sequence by 
pressurizing to 400-420 psi (27.5-29 bar) with GHe and monitoring the 
chamber background with a GHe mass spectrometer leak detector. 
</p>

<p>Fitting Testing Results
</p>
<p>• The results of this testing programs showed that the fittings remain leak tight at 
cryogenic temperature (20K- 30K). 
</p>
<p>• All fittings passed the requirement that a measured leak rate not exceed 10-6 sccs. 
</p>
<p>• Measured leak rates for the 30 fitting/seal pairs were typically in the range of 10-9 -10-10 sccs during 
both Pre-Vibe and Post-Vibe thermal vacuum cycles.
