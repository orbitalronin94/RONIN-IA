<p>2.1 High Temperature Materials 
</p>
<p>High temperature materials are those which are capable of withstanding constant or 
</p>
<p>quickly cycling temperatures and varying levels of operating stress. High temperature 
</p>
<p>materials must also exhibit oxidation resistance at those temperatures.20,21 These and 
</p>
<p>other high temperature materials have been greatly important to the aerospace indus-
</p>
<p>try due to the effciency of power conversion’s direct relation to operating temperature.20 
</p>
<p>High temperature metals in aerospace are primarily used in hot sections of turbine en-
</p>
<p>gines, heat exchangers, and other power conversion components.18–21 
</p>
<p>Research into high-temperature materials at NASA Glenn Research Center has fo-
</p>
<p>cused on not only turbine engine hot sections, but also rocket combustion chamber 
</p>
<p>liners, the surface of atmospheric reentry vehicles, and space power systems. The re-
</p>
<p>search has specifcally focused on understanding the processing - structure - property 
</p>
<p>relationships of the high-temperature materials and the interactions with the harsh, 
</p>
<p>application-specifc environments.21 High-temperature materials research continues to 
</p>
<p>ft into the current technology areas NASA will advance in order to achieve their future 
</p>
<p>aerospace missions and activities.22 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  11 Literature Review 
</p>
<p>Not only are NASA and other research agencies interested in higher-temperature 
</p>
<p>materials, but there is signifcant interest in manufacturing these materials into complex-
</p>
<p>shaped components. Highly complicated metal components, such as combustor domes 
</p>
<p>and compact heat exchangers are at the forefront of current high temperature materials 
</p>
<p>research, and cannot easily be made with traditional, subtractive manufacturing tech-
</p>
<p>niques. These complicated shapes necessitate advanced manufacturing techniques like 
</p>
<p>additive manufacturing (AM), which has been noted by numerous agencies including 
</p>
<p>NASA.22,23,57,58 
</p>
<p>2.1.1 Nickel-base Superalloys 
</p>
<p>Ni-based superalloys have been studied for decades as a high temperature material im-
</p>
<p>portant to the aerospace industry. Over these decades, advances have come in the form 
</p>
<p>of improved alloy compositions and novel processing alterations of Ni-based superal-
</p>
<p>loys in order to increase their operating temperature.18,33,59 Nickel-based superalloys 
</p>
<p>have been used for applications with temperatures up to tens of degrees above 1000 ◦C. 
</p>
<p>Cr and Co have been alloying elements within nickel-base superalloys, for example, to 
</p>
<p>act as oxidation resistance and solid solution strengthening elements.18,20,60 
</p>
<p>In order to achieve the high temperature strengths that make Ni-based superalloys 
</p>
<p>such a desired material, they need to contain a large variety of elements. These alloys 
</p>
<p>contain elements to enable γ’ precipitation strengthening and oxidation and corrosion 
</p>
<p>resistance, which all combine to give these alloys notably stable properties up to 1000 ◦C, 
</p>
<p>or around 60% of their melting temperature.18,33,51 These complex chemistries require 
</p>
<p>very precise heating and processing in order to form the correct volume fraction and size 
</p>
<p>of the γ’ phases necessary for precipitation strengthening.33 Nickel-base superalloys, </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  12 Literature Review 
</p>
<p>especially those with high γ’ volume fractions, are, however, often diffcult to thermo-
</p>
<p>mechanically process (i.e., working or forging) as they require specifc microstructural 
</p>
<p>processing to avoid cracking.43,44,51,61 
</p>
<p>In addition to the complex processing requirements of Ni-based superalloys, the 
</p>
<p>progress in increasing the temperature capabilities of Nickel-base superalloys has long 
</p>
<p>been nearing an asymptote.18 The dissolution of the γ’ precipitates, which is the main 
</p>
<p>strengthening mechanism in Ni-based superalloys, presents a hard limit on the operat-
</p>
<p>ing temperature of these materials.18,31,33,62,63 There is a need to transition from solid-
</p>
<p>solution strengthening to precipitate or dispersion strengthening, which are methods of 
</p>
<p>strengthening alloys at temperatures above those at which precipitation strengthening 
</p>
<p>is effective.31,63 
</p>
<p>Issues surrounding the complex chemistry required by Ni-based superalloys con-
</p>
<p>tributed to the need for powder metallurgy (PM). Powder metallurgy started to become 
</p>
<p>popular due to the ability to achieve high material purity and homogeneity, not to men-
</p>
<p>tion that powdered metal billets were easier to work and shape than their forged prede-
</p>
<p>cessors.33,59,64 Powder metallurgy also allows for the low-cost manufacturing of unique 
</p>
<p>components not achievable with traditional manufacturing methods, due in part to 
</p>
<p>PM’s high material and energy effciency.34,59 
</p>
<p>Alloy powder is traditionally made by a prealloying process which can include me-
</p>
<p>chanical milling, chemical methods, or atomization.34,65,66 Mechanical powder man-
</p>
<p>ufacturing methods include mechanical alloying and milling, both of which can alter 
</p>
<p>the morphology of the original powders and potentially introduce contamination in the </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  13 Literature Review

<p>form of either the a mixing additive or the grinding media.65,66 Chemical methods re-
</p>
<p>quire a reaction medium in addition to the elements intended to be mixed.65 Atomiza-
</p>
<p>tion methods can be used to create powder with a specifc composition and size distri-
</p>
<p>bution, but is not a method to combine multiple dissimilar powders.66,67 
</p>
<p>Once the powder is made with the correct alloy chemistry, PM parts have commonly 
</p>
<p>been consolidated in a die with the aid of heat and pressure. It is diffcult, however, to 
</p>
<p>impart the consistent material density throughout a complex part with rigid die com-
</p>
<p>paction.34 PM parts also inherently contain porosity, for which the level is dependent 
</p>
<p>on the consolidation methods and parameters, as well as heat treatments.33,34 Internal 
</p>
<p>porosity is commonly reduced with hot isostatic pressing (HIP), but this can only allevi-
</p>
<p>ate porosity to an extent.33,34,40,43,60,68 
</p>
<p>In addition to the porosity and consolidation issues inherent to PM, powder met-
</p>
<p>allurgy superalloys, specifcally, are considered diffcult to extrude due in part to the 
</p>
<p>high strain rates and intense controls required to produce the desired microstructure 
</p>
<p>and mechanical properties.61 These diffculties arise from the complex chemistries of 
</p>
<p>Ni-based superalloys, and despite the high chemical purity and customizability advan-
</p>
<p>tages of powder metallurgy, Ni-based superalloys are still considered diffcult to manu-
</p>
<p>facture.33,51,61 Multi-principle element alloys (MPEAs), on the other hand, are a class of 
</p>
<p>material with less complex chemistries that have been studied as an alternative material 
</p>
<p>for high temperature applications.69,70 
</p>
<p>2.1.2 Multi-Principle Element Alloys (MPEA) 
</p>
<p>MPEAs, formerly referred to as high and medium entropy alloys (HEA and MEA respec-
</p>
<p>tively), have been developed and explored in since the early 2000s.70 HEAs are com-
</p>
<p>posed of fve or more elements (3 or 4 for MEAs) in near-equiatomic proportions to </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  14 Literature Review 
</p>
<p>originally promote solid-solution strengthening.9,24,71–73 They are described as having 
</p>
<p>high confgurational entropy among their elements, which favor a single solid solution 
</p>
<p>phase (as opposed to the formation of intermetallic compounds). The simplifed mech-
</p>
<p>anism behind HEAs is a balance between entropy and enthalpy achieved through a min-
</p>
<p>imization of Gibbs Free Energy.69,70 Diffusion is also said to be slow in HEAs, supported 
</p>
<p>by several studies referring to high activation energies and diffcult substitutional diffu-
</p>
<p>sion.70 
</p>
<p>The CoCrFeMnNi family (known as the Cantor alloy) is one of the most widely stud-
</p>
<p>ied HEA, from which NiCoCr has been shown to have a great strength, ductility, and 
</p>
<p>toughness combination, with a melting temperature of 1690 K.8,25,71,72 NiCoCr is hard-
</p>
<p>ened through multi-phase regions (face-centered-cubic or primitive -FCC or FCP- nor-
</p>
<p>mally with regions of hexagonal-close-packed -HCP), low stacking fault energy, and de-
</p>
<p>formation twins, all which help to hinder deformation movement.71–76 This HCP (sigma) 
</p>
<p>phase is Cr-rich, and has been shown form at temperatures at 700 C and lower, and to 
</p>
<p>increase strength and decrease ductility.70,74,77 
</p>
<p>While MPEAs alleviate many of the chemical complexities from Ni-based superal-
</p>
<p>loys, they face a similar intrinsic strengthening asymptote derived from solid solution 
</p>
<p>strengthening. In recent years, research has shifted to exploring elemental doping or 
</p>
<p>other phase additions to induce precipitation strengthening in MPEAs. A study by He et 
</p>
<p>al.24, for example, combined thermomechanical processing with molybdenum-doping 
</p>
<p>of an equiatomic NiCoCr MEA in order to increase its strength and ductility, which was 
</p>
<p>achieved through precipitation hardening and hierarchically related recrystallized and 
</p>
<p>non-recrystallized zones. The fully recrystallized Mo-doped NiCoCr MEA, aided by solid </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  15 Literature Review 
</p>
<p>solution strengthening from the Mo, exhibited a yield strength 12% higher than the non-
</p>
<p>doped NiCoCr MEA.24 It has also been shown that micro-additions of Ti and C to NiCoCr 
</p>
<p>promote equiaxed grains in drop-cast material. The Ti additions improved the tensile 
</p>
<p>properties of the NiCoCr, primarily due to TiC forming as temperature stable particles 
</p>
<p>that acted as heterogeneous nucleation sites for grain growth.25 Elemental additions (of 
</p>
<p>Al and Ti) to the FeCoNiCr matrix were shown to increase strength through formation of 
</p>
<p>nanoscale precipitates, but that these precipitates dissolved at high temperatures (&gt;800 
</p>
<p>◦C).26 While these studies have shown that precipitates can be used to impart strength 
</p>
<p>to MPEAs, precipitates will always have an inherent stability limit lower than the melting 
</p>
<p>temperature of the base alloy. This will result in a similar asymptotic performance issues 
</p>
<p>that currently hinder the advancement of nickel-base superalloys. 
</p>
<p>2.1.3 ODS 
</p>
<p>Dispersion-strengthened alloys lack the same performance asymptote of solid-solution 
</p>
<p>strengthened alloys because they rely on strength from an ex-situ added dispersed phase. 
</p>
<p>It has been noted from previous studies that precipitation and dispersion strengthening 
</p>
<p>generally have a stronger effect on mechanical properties than solid-solution harden-
</p>
<p>ing, which supports the need for dispersion-strengthened materials in a variety of tra-
</p>
<p>ditionally solid solution-strengthened alloys.70 The dispersed phase is fnely distributed 
</p>
<p>within the crystalline matrix, which yields more strength than the matrix phase alone.78 
</p>
<p>Regarding the size of the dispersed phase, it has been shown that nano-sized particles 
</p>
<p>integrated into a metal matrix are have a benefcial effect on the mechanical properties 
</p>
<p>of the metal matrix material.27 
</p>
<p>In ODS materials, the dispersed particulate phase is an oxide, traditionally thoria or 
</p>
<p>yttria. These ODS materials were studied throughout the 1960s, 70s, and 80s, and were </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  16 Literature Review 
</p>
<p>thought of as a potential go-to solution for extending the high temperature capabilities 
</p>
<p>of nearly any alloy system as they could remain stability at high temperatures, above 
</p>
<p>55%-70% (up to 90%) of the melting temperature of the pure metal alloy.26,30,79 Many 
</p>
<p>commercial ODS alloys contain 0.5-1.3 (1.14 avg) wt% yttria.79 The main interest in us-
</p>
<p>ing oxides as the dispersed phase for circumventing the asymptotic temperature per-
</p>
<p>formance of nickel-based superalloys revolves around their stability and strengthening 
</p>
<p>capabilities in the 800 ◦C to 1200 ◦C temperature range.30,34 
</p>
<p>Not only do the oxide dispersoids add strength by providing stable particles of ap-
</p>
<p>propriate size within the matrix to pin defects at temperatures higher than that which 
</p>
<p>other precipitates would dissolve, but these particles enable the retention of thermo-
</p>
<p>mechanically introduced strengthening mechanisms, too.30 The yield strength of ODS 
</p>
<p>materials is associated with the stress required to move dislocations along slip planes 
</p>
<p>greater distances than the dispersoids are spaced apart.78 The oxide dispersoids add 
</p>
<p>strength to the material because they successfully pin grain boundaries and disloca-
</p>
<p>tions during processing and performance.80 Dislocation motion in ODS materials is 
</p>
<p>believed to be hindered by either direct interference from the dispersed particles or 
</p>
<p>from the lattice strain induced from the matrix accommodating the dispersoids.78 Re-
</p>
<p>gardless, it is well understood that Orowan type strengthening (particle strengthening 
</p>
<p>through a dislocation bypass) is the dominant type of strengthening seen in the ODS 
</p>
<p>samples.26,52–54,81,82 
</p>
<p>ODS powders, like other PM materials, are typically made by mechanically alloying 
</p>
<p>the oxide particles with the metal, then compacting and/or extruding at high temper-
</p>
<p>atures in order to consolidate the material.26,32,60,79 The resulting consolidated billets 
</p>
<p>exhibited some advantageous characteristics over comparable cast material, but tensile </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  17 Literature Review 
</p>
<p>and rupture ductility, for example, were lower, and parts required additional processing 
</p>
<p>in order to achieve similar mechanical properties of traditional alloys.19,28,29,31 Another 
</p>
<p>issue identifed when creating complex parts from ODS materials was that the uneven 
</p>
<p>distribution of cold work required to create the complex part shapes would result in 
</p>
<p>larger variations of strength, as compared to wrought materials which would be forged 
</p>
<p>into similarly complex shapes.30 It was eventually believed that ODS materials would 
</p>
<p>not be a cost-effective panacea over traditional cast or wrought materials, mainly due to 
</p>
<p>manufacturing diffculties and expenses.30,31 
</p>
<p>Precipitation strengthening, while similar to dispersion strengthening, does not in-
</p>
<p>volve an extrinsic phase addition. Precipitates can strengthen materials also through 
</p>
<p>Orowan strengthening or Zenner pinning (pinning of grain boundaries)27, however the 
</p>
<p>extrinsic addition of the dispersion phase reduces diffusion-mediated evolution. Diffu-
</p>
<p>sion strengthening also motivates the requirement for conventional powder manufac-
</p>
<p>turing. 
</p>
<p>Oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) Ni-based alloys and superalloys have been 
</p>
<p>studied as a viable material improvement over solid-solution strengthened Ni-based su-
</p>
<p>peralloys.19,28,29 ODS and other precipitation strengthening mechanisms are most effec-
</p>
<p>tive at high temperatures, while solid-solution strengthening remain effective at lower 
</p>
<p>temperatures.52,53 In numerous studies, for example, it has been shown that ODS Ni-
</p>
<p>based superalloys did beneft from γ’ precipitate strengthening at intermediate temper-
</p>
<p>atures and dislocation pinning from the oxide dispersoids at high temperatures. Since 
</p>
<p>the oxides are not precipitated from the melt, they are stable at higher temperatures 
</p>
<p>where other precipitates (such as γ’) would dissolve.19,29,30,78,79 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  18 Literature Review 
</p>
<p>As with Ni-based superalloys, ODS has shown promise in MPEAs. Many studies 
</p>
<p>have explored the benefts of ODS to increase the temperature capabilities of MPEAs. 
</p>
<p>For example, a study of FeCoNiCr strengthened by yttria found that not only were ODS 
</p>
<p>MPEAs feasible, but they can exhibit superior mechanical properties compared to non-
</p>
<p>ODS HEAs.26 Another study investigated ODS CoCrFeMnNi and found that the oxides 
</p>
<p>enabled ultrafne microstructures and increased tensile strength in the material.83 One 
</p>
<p>other study found that the addition of Mn to CoCrFeNi during the LPBF process formed 
</p>
<p>oxides, and the resulting ODS material had high strength and intermediate ductility.84 
</p>
<p>These and similar studies attribute strength and smaller grain sizes to the oxide particles 
</p>
<p>ability to pin grain boundaries17,85 
</p>
<p>2.2 Powder Manufacturing: Mixing 
</p>
<p>Alloy powders are traditionally prepared by both mechanical (ball milling and regular 
</p>
<p>mixing) and non-mechanical (gas atomization, electrodeposition, agent-assisted depo-
</p>
<p>sition) mixing method.49,86 An additional powder preparation method, useful to create 
</p>
<p>homogeneous composite powders, is acoustic mixing. 
</p>
<p>Acoustic mixing has been used to make powder blends in the pharmaceutical com-
</p>
<p>munity, often in combination with a liquid to create a slurry.38,87 Acoustic mixing has 
</p>
<p>been used in the materials science community, as well, both with and without the aid of 
</p>
<p>a lubricant.17,86,88–90 
</p>
<p>Acoustic mixing’s use in powder processing of composite powders relies on electro-
</p>
<p>static forces and cohesion between the particles and has been used in numerous stud-
</p>
<p>ies.86,91 Han et al.92, used a high speed mixing technique, similar to acoustic mixing, to 
</p>
<p>make a composite powder of 1 wt% TiC and Hastelloy X superalloy. They found that the </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  19 Literature Review 
</p>
<p>mixing technique resulted in an even distribution of the TiC particles on the surface of 
</p>
<p>the metal particles. Mizes et al.93, specifcally explain how the Derjaguin approximation 
</p>
<p>is used to estimate the force of Van der Waals and electrostatic attraction between par-
</p>
<p>ticles. A basic description of this is that as the shape of the contact between particles 
</p>
<p>gets sharper, the adhesion decreases. Soulier et al.86, used various mixing techniques, 
</p>
<p>including acoustic, to coat (roughly 35 µm) aluminum powder with (roughly 35 nm) 
</p>
<p>satellite particles of SiC. They found that acoustic mixing lead to complete electrostatic 
</p>
<p>adhesion of the nanosized guest particles and the Al host particles and that the forces 
</p>
<p>in the acoustic mixing process helped reduce agglomeration of guest particles on the 
</p>
<p>surface, as compared to the other mixing methods. 
</p>
<p>Regardless of how the powder is produced, powder consolidation issues still hinder 
</p>
<p>the process.

<p>3.1 Introduction 
</p>
<p>Oxide dispersion strengthening (ODS) was a revolutionary idea that, during the 1960s-
</p>
<p>80s, was thought to be applicable to any metal alloy system in order to increase it’s tem-
</p>
<p>perature capabilities. The prevailing thought was that adding approximately half to one 
</p>
<p>weight percent of an oxide (like thoria or yttria) could increase microstructural stabil-
</p>
<p>ity at temperatures above 55%-70% of the melting temperature of the pure metal alloy. 
</p>
<p>Not only do the oxide dispersoids add strength by providing stable particles of appro-
</p>
<p>priate size within the matrix to pin defects at temperatures higher than which other 
</p>
<p>precipitates would dissolve, but these particles also enable the retention of thermo-
</p>
<p>mechanically introduced strengthening mechanisms.30 At the time, this seemed like 
</p>
<p>a panacea for solving many of the high-temperature material needs of the aerospace

<p>Conventionally, ODS alloys were manufactured by frst mechanically alloying pow-
</p>
<p>der then subsequently solid-state consolidating the powder into bulk products. Me-
</p>
<p>chanical alloys relies on high-energy interaction between powders to be mixed and mix-
</p>
<p>ing media (often stainless steel balls) to plastically deform the powders, essentially cold 
</p>
<p>welding them together. involves. The repeated welding and fracturing of the powders 
</p>
<p>typically results in coarse, non-spherical powder.117 Apart from altering the morphology 
</p>
<p>of the original powders, mechanical alloying can introduce contamination in the form 
</p>
<p>of either the a mixing additive or the grinding media.65,66 
</p>
<p>After producing the ODS powder, it needed to be consolidated, but consolidation of 
</p>
<p>powders into complex shapes resulted in challenges in homogeneity. Like other powder 
</p>
<p>metallurgy materials, ODS materials were usually consolidated by compacting and/or 
</p>
<p>extruding at high temperatures.26,32,60,79 This resulted in a need for complex post pro-
</p>
<p>cessing and working in order to achieve the material properties of interest. These com-
</p>
<p>plicated processes and uneven distribution of work made it diffcult to produce complex-
</p>
<p>shaped parts. Parts produced in this manner would yield too much variation in strength 
</p>
<p>as compared to wrought materials, which could be simply forged into complex shapes.30 
</p>
<p>Because of these pitfalls, ODS technology was largely set aside. 
</p>
<p>A way to overcome these manufacturing challenges is with additive manufacturing 
</p>
<p>(AM). AM has been of interest to the industrial and research communities for the past 
</p>
<p>few decades. AM combines powder metallurgy with the ability to produce complex-
</p>
<p>shaped metal components.36,118 More on AM and its benefts can be found in Chapter 
</p>
<p>2, but needless to say, AM is an enabling technology of much interest to the scientifc 
</p>
<p>community. The combination of ODS and AM technologies means that metal compo-
</p>
<p>nents with superior high-temperature capabilities can be manufactured with complex </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>        28 Acoustic Mixing of Oxide Dispersion Strengthened LPBF Feedstock 
</p>
<p>part geometries and at a fraction of diffculty compared to when they were initially stud-
</p>
<p>ied.36 
</p>
<p>AM feedstock powder is generally spherical and has a size distribution and rheology 
</p>
<p>that promote even packing behavior.35,49,119,120 In Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) sys-
</p>
<p>tems, the powder needs to be able to exit the hopper and be spread evenly across the 
</p>
<p>build plate. As fowability is one of the key metrics to the success of powder AM, a num-
</p>
<p>ber of NIST119 and ASTM standards,121–123 and other research studies35,49,120 focus on 
</p>
<p>the evaluation and qualifcation of powder for AM. 
</p>
<p>AM feedstock powders are traditionally prepared by a process of melting, casting, 
</p>
<p>and gas atomization.49,85,102 This is because the alternative process of mechanical alloy-
</p>
<p>ing can result in non-spherical, rough powder morphology, as well as material contam-
</p>
<p>ination from the mechanical alloying media.85,86 Gas atomization, however, normally 
</p>
<p>results in spherical powders which are suitable for AM.67 
</p>
<p>Composite powders for additive manufacturing, such as ODS powders, are tradition-
</p>
<p>ally produced by both mechanical (ball milling, regular mixing) and non-mechanical 
</p>
<p>(conventional mixing and agent-assisted deposition) mixing methods.38,49,86 Studies in-
</p>
<p>vestigating the characteristics of composite powders often showed a difference in pow-
</p>
<p>der fowability owing to powder morphology and presence or lack of satellite particles. 
</p>
<p>One study which looked at the fowability and other characteristics of two powders, 
</p>
<p>one with and one without nano-sized satellite particles, found that the satellite parti-
</p>
<p>cles contributed to increased particle interlocking and thus decreased fowability com-
</p>
<p>pared to the powder without satellites.124 An investigation of a composite powder (5 
</p>
<p>wt.% B4C with Ti-6Al-4V) produced by traditional mixing and ball milling by Fereiduni 
</p>
<p>et al., showed that the composite powders all had lower fowability than the original </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>        29 Acoustic Mixing of Oxide Dispersion Strengthened LPBF Feedstock 
</p>
<p>metal powder; this was linked to increased mechanical interlocking due to the surface-
</p>
<p>attached guest powders and reduced contact surface area from increased roughness of 
</p>
<p>the composite powder particles. The reduced sphericity of the ball-milled powder also 
</p>
<p>contributed to decreased fowability of the composite powder.49 It has been concluded 
</p>
<p>by several studies that feedstock powder with a high density of satellite particles would 
</p>
<p>compromise fowability and make non-ideal composite powder feedstock.49,102 
</p>
<p>The size ratios of the individual powders comprising the composite powder are im-
</p>
<p>portant to consider. The size difference between the host and guest/satellite powders in 
</p>
<p>the Fereiduni et al. study was roughly 15:1 (15-45 µm sized host particles with 1-3 µm 
</p>
<p>guest particles), and the study found decreased fowability for all of the composite pow-
</p>
<p>ders compared to the un-mixed host powder.49 A much higher size difference between 
</p>
<p>guest and host particles has been shown to actually increase fowability of the compos-
</p>
<p>ite powders. In a study involving the fowability characteristics of cornstarch mixed with 
</p>
<p>various sizes of silica powders showed that the larger cornstarch powder when coated 
</p>
<p>with the nano-sized silica powder (size ratio ∼750:1), had improved fowability com-
</p>
<p>pared to both the cornstarch alone and the cornstarch mixed with larger silica powder 
</p>
<p>(size ratio ∼30:1). The authors attributed this to the decrease in cohesive forces between 
</p>
<p>the cornstarch particles in the presence of nano-sized satellite particles due to the re-
</p>
<p>duced surface contact.91 
</p>

</p>
<p>advantageous fowability. One study looked specifcally at different methods of mix-
</p>
<p>ing nano-sized particles with micron-scale host particles as a method of increasing the 
</p>
<p>fowability of the host material. Soulier and colleagues investigated a composite powder 
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>compared to the unmixed powder, and that acoustic and high-shear mixing were both 
</p>
<p>effective in breaking up silica agglomerates and resulted in a mixture that was stable 
</p>
<p>during spreading.86 The powder used in the current study is a composite of NiCoCr host 
</p>
<p>powder with nano-sized yttria oxide powder (size ratio ∼166:1), which is much closer to 
</p>
<p>the size ratio of the powders that had improved fowability from the guest particles, and

<p>Acoustic mixing, which has primarily been used in the pharmaceutical and poly-
</p>
<p>mer industries to combine powders, utilizes ultrasonic resonance vibrations to induce 
</p>
<p>complex mixing motions and quick homogenization of powder, and doesn’t require the 
</p>
<p>assistance of a lubricant or liquid medium.38,39,87 Acoustic mixing has also been utilized 
</p>
<p>in making metal composites. In the study looking at acoustic mixing of silica guest and 
</p>

<p>the oxide nanoparticles at these stages.

<p>There are a number of ways to quantify the dispersed secondary phase/particles 
</p>
<p>within a metal matrix, from electron to X-ray based methods. The ability to characterize 
</p>

<p>identify nanoscale particles. Likewise, the ability to resolve the characteristic chemi-
</p>
<p>cal signal overlaps dictate the chemical resolution of these techniques.125 Transmission 
</p>
<p>Electron Microscopy (TEM) is well known to provide the highest resolution analysis and 
</p>
<p>can measure a suffciently small area to capture nano-scale features, but lengthy sample </p>

<p>3.2 Experimental Methods 
</p>
<p>The analysis of the mixing parameters’ effect on the oxide distribution on the the powder 
</p>
<p>was done in two phases. The initial phases of experiments were designed to qualitatively 
</p>
<p>evaluate the impact of two powder processing inputs (mixing condition A-E and mixing

</p>

<p>4.1 Introduction 
</p>
<p>Additive manufacturing has been used for the past few decades as a way to manufac-
</p>
<p>ture complex-shaped metal parts for a number of industries including the aerospace 
</p>
<p>industry. One of the common aerospace materials that has been the focus of years of 
</p>
<p>research as an AM material are nickel-base superalloys. Ni-based superalloys have been 
</p>
<p>engineered to maintain note worthy mechanical properties at temperatures around 900-
</p>
<p>1100 ◦C.18,33 They contain a variety of elements to enable γ’ precipitation strengthening 
</p>
<p>and oxidation and corrosion resistance, which combine to give these alloys notably sta-
</p>
<p>ble properties up to 1000 ◦C , or around 60% of their melting temperature.18,33,51 
</p>
<p>However, Ni-base superalloys require very precise heating and processing to form 
</p>
<p>the phases necessary to precipitation strengthening in Ni-superalloys, in their volume 
</p>
<p>fraction and size.33 Precipitation hardened Ni-base superalloys are highly susceptible 
</p>
<p>to cracking during processes involving complex thermal histories such as welding or 
</p>
<p>additive manufacturing.43,44 Some Ni-base superalloys are sometimes categorized as </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>       50 AM Parameters’ Effect on Printed ODS NiCoCr 
</p>
<p>non-weldable due to strain-age cracking from high gamma prime fraction.51,109 Multi-
</p>
<p>principle Element Alloys (MPEAs), which have a much less complex chemistry, provide 
</p>
<p>a good solution to many of these issues. 
</p>
<p>MPEAs have been the focus of numerous research projects over the past couple of 
</p>
<p>decades. These materials have 3-5 (generally less than 10) elements that combine with 
</p>
<p>high confgurational entropy and solid solution phases.70 MPEAs have been additively 
</p>
<p>manufactured and have proven successful in many research studies. Almost all of the 
</p>
<p>issues with additively manufacturing MPEAs relate to the AM process and not to any 
</p>
<p>complex chemical interactions within the material.69,102,110 
</p>
<p>Oxide dispersion strengthening (ODS) has been applied to both MPEAs and Ni-based 
</p>
<p>superalloys to increase the operating temperature of the materials. In the 1960s, when 
</p>
<p>ODS alloys were frst becoming a major research interest, it was thought that ODS could 
</p>
<p>be applied to any alloy to increase its operating temperature. One of the major draw-
</p>
<p>backs of ODS materials, which largely contributed to them falling out of research in-
</p>
<p>terest was that they were diffcult and expensive to manufacture. Another issue identi-
</p>
<p>fed through past attempts at creating complex parts from ODS materials was that the 
</p>
<p>uneven distribution of cold work required to create the complex shapes of these parts 
</p>
<p>would result in too much variation of strength.30 Additive manufacturing (AM) provides 
</p>
<p>a manufacturing method that overcomes these challenges and allows for ODS material 
</p>
<p>to be printed into complex shapes more easily. 
</p>
<p>The AM parameters used in laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) have been known to 
</p>
<p>impact the microstructure and physical properties of AM components, and many stud-
</p>
<p>ies have investigated which parameters are most important. In one study, for example, </p>
<p></p>

<p>5.1 Powder Experiment Conclusions 
</p>
<p>The powder experiments were discussed in Chapter 3. These experiments were de-
</p>
<p>signed to understand the impact of the powder preparation parameters on the quality 
</p>
<p>of the oxide distribution on the composite material. The powders mixed were a multi-
</p>
<p>principle element alloy, NiCoCr, which was nominally 25 µm in diameter and an oxide, 
</p>
<p>yttria, which was roughly 100-200 nm in diameter. The oxide powder was added to the 
</p>
<p>metal powder at a 1:99 weight ratio and combined using an acoustic resonance mixer 
</p>
<p>(Resodyn37). The mixing parameters in question were the mixing time and a condition 
</p>
<p>fairly equivalent to energy input. Previous studies have shown that these are the main 
</p>
<p>variables that can impact the extent of the mix in an acoustic mixer.37–39 
</p>
<p>The impact of these parameters on the distribution of yttria on the surface of the 
</p>
<p>NiCoCr powder was evaluated qualitatively through analysis of secondary electron im-
</p>
<p>ages of both whole and cross-sectioned mixed powder samples. A framework for char-
</p>
<p>acterizing the powder coatings was used to determine which mixing parameter combi-
</p>
<p>nations yielded the best and worst coatings on the powders. The framework considered 
</p>
<p>percent coverage, consistency, and thickness of the oxide coating (this was tabulated in 
</p>
<p>Table 3.3). </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>

</p>
<p>In order to understand the quantitative impact that the various mixing parameter 
</p>
<p>combinations had on the oxide coating, Time of Flight Secondary Ion Mass Spectrome-
</p>
<p>try (TOF-SIMS) was used to measure the amount of yttrium on the surface of the mixed 
</p>
<p>ODS NiCoCr powders mixed with seventeen different parameter combinations. These 
</p>
<p>evaluations indicated that more mixing time was advantageous to less, to an extent, and 
</p>
<p>that higher mixing condition (energy) resulted in more yttrium on the surface of the 
</p>
<p>powders.

<p>6.1 Optimization Studies 
</p>
<p>This project included screening and modeling experiments, as described by the NIST 
</p>
<p>Engineering Handbook. Optimization is the fnal step in the design of experiments 
</p>
<p>(DOE) outline and can be done with enough data to plot a response surface. This would 
</p>
<p>allow for determining the minimum and maximum of a quadratic model, which would 
</p>
<p>represent the desired output.48 In order to optimize the mixing or AM parameters from 
</p>
<p>this study, it would be necessary to collect more data. The semi-quantitative analysis 
</p>
<p>in Chapter 3 evaluated seventeen samples, but wasn’t able to defne the entire response 
</p>
<p>surface. Enough data is needed to model the local maximum or minimum, as well as the 
</p>
<p>neighboring slope changes used to confrm that the high and low results are actually the 
</p>
<p>extremum on the response surface.In order to quantitatively determine the extremum, 
</p>
<p>quadratic modeling would be required. </p>
<p></p>

<p>6.2 Powder particle size vs Porosity 
</p>
<p>The current study did not include a large enough variety of particle size distributions 
</p>
<p>to test the relationship between powder particle size and porosity, however this could 
</p>
<p>be done through an extended mixing parameter space, such as the TOF-SIMS analy-
</p>
<p>ses from Chapter 3, combined with printing multi-feature blocks from those powder 
</p>
<p>batches. Several previous studies have shown a correlation between powder size and 
</p>
<p>porosity of the printed part.49,102 Roughly 5kg of powder is required to complete a print 
</p>
<p>of multi-feature blocks on the EOS M100 at NASA Glenn, which is attained from com-
</p>
<p>bining 3 metal containers (900g full each) worth of mixed powder. Without knowing the 
</p>
<p>standard deviation of the powder size distributions, the amount of samples can only be 
</p>
<p>estimated. According to the NIST handbook of statistical methods, a sample size of 10 
</p>
<p>is estimated to be needed for a statistical correlation with 95% confdence interval with 
</p>
<p>unknown standard deviation.48 Therefore, at least 18 metal container samples would 
</p>
<p>need to be produced and used to print 6 sets of multi-feature blocks to be able to quan-
</p>
<p>tify the powder particle size and bulk material porosity relationship. 
</p>
<p>6.3 Strength vs grain size 
</p>
<p>A number of studies have shown that grain size and dispersion extent have an effect on 
</p>
<p>yield strength.4,8,11,71,92,102 Yield stress is affected by a combination of stresses, which 
</p>
<p>include precipitate hardening and grain boundary strengthening.8 One study described 
</p>
<p>how high grain aspect ratio (more than 10:1) were desirable for strength at very high tem-
</p>
<p>peratures for ODS alloys.79 Wang et al., studied an AM Cantor alloy and related mechan-
</p>
<p>ically induced twins (a major strengthening element) to grain size. They determined a </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  106 Future Research 
</p>
<p>critical grain size (and noted that this was hard to calculate for elongated grains) needed 
</p>
<p>for the twins to form.102 
</p>
<p>According to ASTM Standard E2627148 and E1382,149 at least fve image frames en-
</p>
<p>compassing at least 100 grains each with 500 or more indexed points is required to de-
</p>
<p>termine grain size. The EBSD frames collected from Loop 2 of this study included the 
</p>
<p>correct number of grains, but only one image per sample was measured. At least four 
</p>
<p>more EBSD maps would need to be collected per sample to properly determine the grain 
</p>
<p>size. These results could couple with the mechanical test results to provide a quantita-
</p>
<p>tive correlation between grain size and mechanical strength for ODS NiCoCr. 
</p>
<p>6.4 Multivariate Linear Models and Beyond 
</p>
<p>This project included a number of multivariate linear models, but several of the corre-
</p>
<p>lations seen throughout this project would be better represented by non-linear mod-
</p>
<p>els with quantifed coeffcients (yttrium vs mixing conditions correlations explored in 
</p>
<p>Chapter 3 and shown in Figure 3.4). Non-linear models were outside the scope of the 
</p>
<p>current project, but could help quantify those relationships. 
</p>
<p>6.5 Material Alternatives

<p>Composite materials have long been studied for their favorable combinations of prop-
</p>
<p>erties. Metal Matrix Composites have been studied for aerospace applications due to 
</p>
<p>their lightweight, wear resistance, high strength, and high temperature resistance prop-
</p>
<p>erties.150,151 Metal matrix naoncomposites, which are reinforced with nano-sized par-
</p>
<p>ticles, have more recently been studied for aerospace applications.27 Refractory metal 
</p>
<p>complex alloys have also received interest in the aerospace industry for their superior </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  Future Research 107 
</p>
<p>high temperature properties.70 These felds of research may yield valuable material al-
</p>
<p>ternatives to ODS NiCoCr that offer similar or superior high temperature capabilities. 
</p>
<p>6.6 Micro-Fatigue and Full-Scale Fatigue Comparisons

</p>
<p>down samples. The potential to validate the small-scale fatigue testing against full-scale

<p>fatigue tests could mean that accurate mechanical properties could be calculated with-
</p>
<p>out the need to create full-scale parts for evaluation. This (yet unpublished) work has 
</p>
<p>been focusing on a copper alloy, GRCop-42, and as an additional validation step is set 
</p>
<p>to include ODS NiCoCr samples. Full-sized fatigue testing sample bars have been ma-
</p>

</p>
<p>scale evaluations will be performed on sections of the multi-feature blocks used through-
</p>
<p>out this dissertation project. </p>

<p>2 Particle Size Distribution 
</p>
<p>Particle size distribution was investigated using NASA Glenn’s Horiba Partica LA-960V2 
</p>
<p>Laser Scattering Particle Size Distribution Analyzer following Horiba’s standard proce-
</p>
<p>dures152 and according to ASTM-52907.122 In order to prepare for use, tap water was 
</p>
<p>added to the chamber refll bucket under the machine and wipes and ethanol were made 
</p>
<p>available. The computer program was set up to use water as the dispersion medium and 
</p>
<p>Ni powder as the background reference material. Water was fed into the chamber, then 
</p>
<p>an ’agitate and circulate’ cycle was run. Alignment of the lasers was optimized for perfor-
</p>
<p>mance, followed by a de-bubbling cycle, and then the lasers were blanked. Using a metal 
</p>
<p>spatula, a small amount of powder (1-5 grams) was added to the analysis chamber of the 
</p>
<p>Horiba until the appropriate amount of particulates were detected by the machine (as 
</p>
<p>indicated by the optimum zones marked on the red and blue laser level bars, roughly at 
</p>
<p>1000-1500 units). Once the powder was added to the water in the analysis chamber, the 
</p>
<p>ultrasonic cycle was run for three minutes, followed by another de-bubble cycle. Once 
</p>
<p>the normal measure cycle was concluded, the analysis chamber was drained, rinsed, 
</p>
<p>and wiped clean with a paper towel and ethanol. The procedure was then repeated, be-
</p>
<p>ginning with feeding water into the chamber, for each analysis. A complete analysis of </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> Appendix 109 
</p>
<p>each sample set consisted of six repetitions (measurement of six unique samples from 
</p>
<p>each powder mix batch).

<p>3 Morphologi Powder Dispersion Tool 
</p>
<p>The procedure for attaining an even powder dispersion on a carbon tape stub using 
</p>
<p>the Malvern Morphologi Automated Particle Size &amp; Shape Analyzer is as follows. After 
</p>
<p>the computer and equipment are turned on and the software package Morphology is 
</p>
<p>loaded, the air hose is frst un-clipped and the dispenser removed. After removing the 
</p>
<p>dispenser lid and blowing out the sample holder with the compressed air, the whole 
</p>
<p>dispenser inside is wiped down with anti-static spray. Once the sample holder disc is re-
</p>
<p>placed onto the dispenser, 5 mm3 of powder is placed into the center of the sample disc, 
</p>
<p>using an appropriately sized dispensing spoon. The lid of the dispenser is then replaced 
</p>
<p>and the dispenser and powder assembly is put back into its holder on the Malvern and 
</p>
<p>reattached to the air hose. A SEM stub covered in carbon tape is then placed on the glass 
</p>
<p>so that it will be moved to under the dispenser. Using the software, ’Measure’ and then 
</p>
<p>’Disperse Sample’ are then selected. On the next window, where pressure and time ad-
</p>
<p>justments are made, 2 bars of pressure, 20 microseconds of air, and 60 seconds of rest 
</p>
<p>were determined to be adequate for the powders used in this study. When done, the 
</p>
<p>software’s microscope view can be used to check the success of the powder dispersion. 
</p>
<p>Selecting ’Load Position’ in the Morphology software returns the sample glass slide to a 
</p>
<p>position where the coated stub can be removed. The glass sample slide is then blown off 
</p>
<p>and cleaned with lens cleaner and a Kim wipe. </p>
