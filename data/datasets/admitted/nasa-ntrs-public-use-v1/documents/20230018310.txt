Motivation
1. To turn real-world, full-scale, sparse, noisy wind 



measurements into accurate estimates of ground wind 
conditions on the rocket and its mobile launch tower, 
with an ultimate goal of estimating aerodynamic 
loading and comparing to careful wind tunnel test 
measurements and computational simulations.



2. Develop a tool to estimate in real-time the wind profile 
experienced by the unfueled Artemis II vehicle and 
mobile launcher any time they are on the pad for long 
durations and exposed to strong weather systems, to 
assist in potentially important operational decision-
making (e.g., rollback to shelter).

SLS Transport to Pad 39B at KSC



4



• The rocket and launch tower (or Mobile Launcher) are transported together from the Vehicle Assembly 
Building (VAB) to launch pad 39B using the crawler-transporter over a 4.2-mile pathway made of Alabama 
river rock at a speed of ~0.8 mph. This process takes about 10 hours end-to-end. The weight of the entire 
system is over 20M pounds. Wind loads are considered when prepacking (conditioning) the Crawlerway.











Criticality of Ground Winds to Pad-stay
1. The vehicle is tall. With stronger winds at altitude, vortex shedding has a higher chance of tuning with 



the vehicle natural bending modes and enter a fluid-structure resonance.
2. Higher than expected Mobile Launcher (ML) weight and liftoff blast loads have made wind loads a driving 



factor for prelaunch analysis.
3. The vehicle can stay on the launch pad for many days before launch, exposing it to potentially strong 



weather systems. 
4. The flow field interactions between the launch tower and the vehicle have shown to be significant for 



certain wind directions in the wind tunnel.
5. Weather at KSC can change rapidly and exhibit strong, fast-moving systems, in particular during 



hurricane season. Storm systems are difficult to predict, and their exact paths are highly uncertain. 
6. A few hardware systems, like the forward attach bolts between the boosters and the core stage, were not 



designed for strong lateral wind loading situations when the vehicle is unfueled on the launch pad 
(critical bolts in low compression, or even tension).



7. For such a complex launch vehicle, increasing launch availability is challenging but critical. A high-
fidelity understanding of the flow and vehicle/ML response to ground winds is required to make better-
informed decisions for rolling out to the pad, safely remaining on the pad during high-wind events, or 
rolling back to protect all systems in case of an extreme wind event.

Conclusions
• Data from twelve anemometers at Pad 39B over 121 days were analyzed and 



post-processed to result in accurate estimates of winds experienced by the 
rocket and launch tower during 4 separate unfueled pad-stay periods before 
the Artemis I launch. 18 of these days were high-wind days (>35 kts), and 4 were 
extreme wind days (>50 kts).



• Misleading, missing, and corrupted data are not always obvious in real-world 
measurements, but a rigorous methodology has been developed to post-
process LPS anemometer measurements and will be implemented for Artemis II 
pad-stay winds monitoring.



• The next phase includes estimating full-scale wind loads from sparse and noisy 
strain gauge measurements at the base of the vehicle, with the knowledge of 
post-processed incoming wind conditions.
