<p>Validation of a Computational Fluid 
</p>
<p>Dynamics Model of Axial Jet Mixing for 
</p>
<p>Cryogenic Propellant Tank Pressure Control 
</p>
<p>Jacob M. Brodnick1, Brandon R. Williams2, and Edward J. Reske1 
</p>
<p>NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, Alabama, USA 
</p>
<p>The Fluid Dynamics Branch at the NASA Marshall Space Flight Center is preparing to 
</p>
<p>support flight programs through analysis of a variety of cryogenic fluid management (CFM) 
</p>
<p>applications. Many vehicles being considered for future manned missions to the moon and 
</p>
<p>beyond use chemical or nuclear thermal propulsion systems that rely on cryogenic 
</p>
<p>propellants. Storing cryogenic propellants for later use is a challenge, though. Many areas of 
</p>
<p>active CFM research, testing, and design involve propellant conditioning to ensure propellant 
</p>
<p>remains a usable liquid for propulsion. Multiple technologies may be used to achieve adequate 
</p>
<p>conditioning including the subject of this paper, a jet-based mixer. Mixing serves to 
</p>
<p>homogenize fluid temperatures and decrease ullage pressure. Development and validation of 
</p>
<p>a modeling methodology for jet-based mixing was conducted to prepare for in-line design 
</p>
<p>work. 
</p>
<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>Cryogenic propulsion systems have long been used for launch vehicles due to their high thrust, throttle ability, and 
</p>
<p>high specific impulse, compared to other chemical systems. For these reasons, the propulsion systems are also being 
</p>
<p>implemented in landers and ascent vehicles for celestial bodies beyond Earth, among other spacecraft. However, 
</p>
<p>thermal conditioning of cryogenic propellants is still a challenge for long duration missions. The low vapor pressure 
</p>
<p>of cryogenic propellants results in evaporation or boiling even for small heat leaks to the liquid making cryogenic 
</p>
<p>fluid management (CFM) difficult. Systems may be developed that are robust to temperature and pressure changes 
</p>
<p>caused by heat leaks, e.g., propellant tanks with high maximum operating pressures, technologies yielding active 
</p>
<p>thermal conditioning, and systems with ample pressurant for conditioning. Development of a predictive capability of 
</p>
<p>an aspect of active thermal control is the topic of this work. 
</p>
<p>Active thermal control has traditionally been conceptualized as a group of technologies working in concert to 
</p>
<p>homogenize and subcool a cryogenic propellant while reducing and rejecting heat leaks. Using multiple technologies, 
</p>
<p>a maximum amount of propellant is expected to remain a usable liquid. One of the considered technologies is a mixer 
</p>
<p>that is designed to recirculate liquid in a propellant tank. Mixing homogenizes liquid temperatures precluding disparate 
</p>
<p>temperature growth along liquid interfaces with structure or the ullage gas. As a result, heat entering the system is 
</p>
<p>evenly distributed avoiding local evaporation or boiling that could subsequently pressurize the tank necessitating 
</p>
<p>venting. Any venting is a loss of commodities and may represent a direct loss of performance as well. As vehicles are 
</p>
<p>optimized for increased payload capacity and reliance upon in-situ resource utilization for long duration missions, 
</p>
<p>mixers are likely to be leveraged for both ground and space-based use. 
</p>
<p>Cryogenic mixer technologies have been tested before. The most common mixers recirculate liquid propellant by 
</p>
<p>pulling it through a pump and injecting it back into the tank. Jet-induced mixing for cryogenic tanks has primarily 
</p>
<p>consisted of jets aimed along the symmetry axis. Test data for these axial jet mixers at various flow rates, tank scales, 
</p>
<p>and fluid types exist including some subscale microgravity test data. A particular set of ground test data for a 1 m 
</p>
<p>radius liquid hydrogen propellant tank tested at the NASA Glenn Research Center K-Site facility [1] was used to 
</p>
<p>validate a modeling capability of axial jet mixing. 
</p>
<p>Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation was used to model the jet mixing process using the 
</p>
<p>Loci/STREAM code with a recently implemented sharp interface method of distinctly representing both fluid phases 
</p>
<p>to reduce computational expense versus other two-phase approaches. In particular, a boundary condition was used to </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>2 
</p>
<p>communicate between the gas and liquid phase domains along a static interface. Both heat and mass transfer occur 
</p>
<p>across the interface while momentum for each phase is coupled to prevent interface normal velocity but allow parallel 
</p>
<p>velocity, i.e., shear flow. This method of simulation is only necessarily applicable when the gas-liquid interface is 
</p>
<p>unperturbed, but results can demonstrate model features needed in other more general simulation approaches like the 
</p>
<p>volume of fluid approach. 
</p>
<p>The conducted CFD simulations demonstrate jet expansion and forced convection which reduces tank pressure 
</p>
<p>through heat exchange and condensation at the interface. Both mixing characteristics and ullage pressure drop for 
</p>
<p>different modeling methodologies were quantified through comparisons of transient temperatures and pressure within 
</p>
<p>the tank. Demonstration of a predictive capability for such a mixer design enables rapid assessment and refinement of 
</p>
<p>the technology for eventual infusion into future vehicles, supporting some of the most ambitious exploration and 
</p>
<p>science missions humans have undertaken.  
</p>
<p>II. Test Description 
</p>
<p>Testing of the jet mixing process was conducted at the NASA Glenn Research Center K-Site facility [1]. The 
</p>
<p>ground test was conducted within a vacuum chamber and thermal shroud to control the heat leak to the test article. 
</p>
<p>The heat leak of 4.2 W/m2 was quantified by measuring the steady boiloff rate using the procedure outline in references 
</p>
<p>[2] and [3]. This heat leak was achieved by covering the tank in multilayer insulation (MLI).  
</p>
<p>The test article was a nearly ellipsoidal tank with a 1.2 major-to-minor axis ratio and a 2.2 m major diameter. The 
</p>
<p>axial jet mixer from the Shuttle Centaur hydrogen tank was installed along the tank centerline in the test article. The 
</p>
<p>inner diameter of the jet nozzle outlet was 0.0221 m and was located 0.51 m from the tank bottom. An illustration of 
</p>
<p>the tank configuration and mixing process is shown in Fig. 1a. An array of temperature sensors and were installed as 
</p>
<p>shown in Fig. 1b. A pressure sensor in direct communication with the ullage, and a capacitance probe used to measure 
</p>
<p>the fill level were also installed.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>Several operations preceded mixer testing to condition the liquid and vapor phases. The test article was initially 
</p>
<p>filled with liquid hydrogen and continuously vented to a target pressure. This brought the hardware to a target liquid 
</p>
<p>saturation temperature. Then, liquid was drained to just above a target fill level, and the vent was closed. This allowed 
</p>
<p>the tank to pressurize due to heat leak into the system. During pressurization, the liquid becomes saturated with a 
</p>
<p>warmer layer of liquid settling at the gas-liquid interface due to natural convection. The mixer was turned on once a 
</p>
<p>target ullage pressure was reached, beginning the test. The cold jet expanded and mixed with the warmer layers near 
</p>
<p>the interface dropping the pressure again. Testing ended once a steady ullage pressure was reached. 
</p>

<p>III. CFD Methodology 
</p>
<p>A two-dimensional (2D) axisymmetric version of the tank test article and jet mixer were modeled. The full tank

</p>
<p>investigated but is expected to have a negligible effect on the mixing and depressurization process primarily due to

<p>A. Computational Tools 
</p>
<p>Loci/STREAM, a pressure-based solver, was used in the preparation of these results. Loci/STREAM [4][5] is an 
</p>
<p>all-speed CFD code for generalized grids in the rule-based programming framework called Loci [6]. The Loci 
</p>
<p>framework allows the solver to integrate new multidisciplinary physics using a modular manner and automatic 
</p>
<p>handling of massively parallel computing. The Loci/STREAM code has been applied and validated over a wide range 
</p>
<p>of problems, including incompressible laminar flows to compressible turbulent flows with heat transfer. 
</p>
<p>Loci/STREAM has been shown to also scale very well on significant problems [4][5]. Loci/STREAM is a pressure-
</p>
<p>based solver with SIMPLE, SIMPLEC, and PISO algorithms available for pressure-momentum coupling. Also 
</p>
<p>available are various turbulence models that can be executed in Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) or Large 
</p>
<p>Eddy Simulation (LES) mode. It has support for reacting flows and flamelet models for turbulent combustion. 
</p>
<p>Loci/STREAM supports first and second-order discretization for inviscid fluxes as well as first and second-order 
</p>
<p>temporal discretization. The code has been in use at NASA MSFC for large-scale simulations of low-speed flows, 
</p>
<p>turbulent combustion, cavitation, and other problems.  
</p>
<p>The code employs multiple methodologies to represent distinct fluid phases and their shared interface. The volume 
</p>
<p>of fluid (VoF) method distinctly represents a liquid and gaseous phase in the same simulation by tracking the volume 
</p>
<p>of a particular fluid and the geometric liquid interface in each cell of the discretized computational domain. This 
</p>
<p>simulation technique can capture gas-liquid interface deformation and breakup and has been applied extensively to 
</p>
<p>propellant tank dynamics and cryogenic fluid management applications. 
</p>
<p>Recently, a method of distinctly representing two fluid phases with a common static interface was recently 
</p>
<p>developed for Loci/STREAM that is referred to as a sharp interface model and is described below. By eliminating 
</p>
<p>interface movement with the corresponding calculations and temporal resolution requirements for stability and 
</p>
<p>accuracy, the sharp interface method is expected to reduce computational expense by more than an order of magnitude 
</p>
<p>as compared to a VoF method for practical problems.  
</p>
<p>Sharp Interface Model 
</p>
<p>The Loci/STREAM implementation of a sharp interface method to model CFM applications is similar to previous 
</p>
<p>studies [7],[8]. The model comprises a specialized interface boundary condition between the liquid and the gas phases 
</p>
<p>that does not allow momentum transfer normal to the interface. The sharp interface boundary separates two regions 
</p>
<p>of the mesh, each containing a pure gas or a pure liquid phase. The sharp interface model augments the existing VoF 
</p>
<p>module to allow the use of the same liquid equation of state and liquid model files and minimize changes to the 
</p>
<p>Loci/STREAM input file. 
</p>
<p>The momentum boundary conditions were derived by setting the tangential velocity and tangential components of 
</p>
<p>the interfacial stress on the gas and liquid sides to be the same (𝑣𝑙 �̂� = 𝑣𝑔�̂�, 𝜏𝑙 �̂� = 𝜏𝑔�̂� ). The normal velocity at the 
</p>
<p>interface is set to zero for both gas and liquid, and the interfacial phase change mass source term is applied at the gas 
</p>
<p>side cell center of the interface.  
</p>

</p>

<p>B. Computational Domain and Mesh 
</p>
<p>Multiple views of the nominal resolution computational mesh are presented in Fig. 4. A 2D surface mesh of mixed 
</p>
<p>cell type was extruded through a 1° wedge forming a single layer of volume cells. The mesh consisted of about 62,000 
</p>
<p>volume cells. The boundary fitted mesh included refined normal wall spacing and stretching to the mesh interior. The 
</p>
<p>gas and liquid domains were made separately. Each domain connected to the gas-liquid interface which was node 
</p>
<p>matched on either side. The domains were combined into a single file, but volume cells remained linked to the gas-
</p>
<p>liquid interface boundary. This allowed boundary condition specification on the interface. The interface was refined 
</p>
<p>in the same manner as the solid walls to capture velocity and temperature gradients there.  
</p>
<p>The meshes simulated to investigate spatial resolution sensitivity are shown in Fig. 5. Normal spacing from walls 
</p>
<p>and the gas-liquid interface was 0.01 mm for every mesh to yield a y+ of less than 1 throughout the domain. Peak 
</p>
<p>velocity was within the mixer, so spacing could be increased elsewhere in the domain. Stretch rates were maintained 
</p>
<p>for each mesh. The maximum edge length was 50 mm, 10 mm, and 5 mm for the coarse, nominal, and fine meshes, 
</p>
<p>respectively. Similarly, the total volume cell count was 36,500, 62,500, and 119,700. 
</p>

<p>C. Simulation Inputs 
</p>
<p>Equations were solved with a 2nd order accuracy scheme in space and a 1st order accuracy scheme in time. A time 
</p>
<p>step of 0.1 s was used for nominal simulations. Liquid hydrogen (LH2) and gaseous hydrogen (GH2) were modeled 
</p>
<p>in their respective domains. Table 2 contains the fixed fluid properties used for LH2 at the temperature and pressure 
</p>
<p>shown [12]. The Boussinesq approximation was used to model buoyancy in the liquid phase only. The compressible 
</p>
<p>gas phase was modeled as an ideal gas with a transport model calibrated to data [12]. The initial temperature field and 
</p>
<p>ullage pressure measured for each test, shown in Fig. 2 and Table 1 respectively, were imposed as initial conditions 
</p>
<p>onto a quiescent flow field. 
</p>
<p>The kinetics-based method of calculating local phase change across the gas-liquid interface described above was 
</p>
<p>used throughout. The phase change model includes a coefficient which can act as a phase change limiter if set too low. 
</p>
<p>The dimensionless coefficient that can vary from 0 to 1 was nominally set to 0.001, but a sensitivity study was also 
</p>
<p>conducted. The Antoine equation with coefficients taken from data [12] was used to calculate the saturation conditions 
</p>
<p>throughout the simulation. 
</p>
<p>The improved 2003 Shear Stress Transport model (SST) [13] was nominally used for the mixing simulations. 
</p>
<p>Laminar equations and various other turbulence models were investigated, but those results are only briefly discussed 
</p>
<p>in this paper. Initial turbulence quantities for 𝑘 and 𝜇𝑡 in the computational domain were set low but non-zero values, 
</p>
<p>at 1e-8 m2/s2 and 1e-10 Pa-s, respectively.  
</p>
<p>All solid walls were modeled with a no-slip boundary condition. A 4.2 W/m2 heat flux was applied to tank walls 
</p>
<p>while all others were assumed adiabatic. A fixed velocity was held on the inlet boundary forming the mixer jet, with 
</p>
<p>low turbulence intensity specified. The initial temperature at the outlet at the bottom of the tank was nominally used 
</p>
<p>as the inlet temperature. Inlet velocity was set for each simulation to yield the tested volumetric flow rate while the 
</p>
<p>same volumetric flow rate was explicitly specified at the outlet. The gas-liquid interface was modeled as previously 
</p>
<p>described with the interface turbulence nominally using the wall treatment in both fluid domains. All other boundaries 
</p>
<p>were modeled with a symmetry boundary condition. 
</p>

<p>IV. Axial Jet Mixing Validation 
</p>
<p>Results of jet mixing simulations are compared to corresponding test data in this section. Several sensitivity studies 
</p>
<p>were conducted to ensure model convergence and identify modeling requirements for any future related in-line work.  
</p>
<p>The analysis results shown were generated from an initially quiescent flow field. Self-pressurization prior to 
</p>
<p>mixing was also simulated but found to have negligible effect on the pressure drop rate as forced convection  
</p>
<p>overwhelmed natural convection. It is expected that properly representing natural convection during the mixing 
</p>
<p>process is needed when simulating sufficiently low mixing rates such that either a jet does not penetrate to the liquid 
</p>
<p>surface or there is comparable energy in the forced and natural convection flow fields.  
</p>
<p>A two equation 𝑘 − 𝜔 SST turbulence model was used to generate all the analysis results shown. Laminar model 
</p>
<p>equations were used separate from the results shown here that demonstrated negligible jet spreading and mixing. The 
</p>
<p>pressure transient and final pressure consequently compared poorly with the data. Similar results, but with more 
</p>
<p>mixing, were observed when running a Detached Eddy Simulation (DES). Turbulence production was predominately 
</p>
<p>at the mixer exit where jet expansion begins. The lack of turbulent production further downstream halted jet expansion 
</p>
<p>and mixing. Turbulence treatment on the gas-liquid interface was also investigated. Rather than a wall treatment where 
</p>
<p>turbulent kinetic energy was driven to 0 for the nominal simulations, turbulence was extrapolated to the gas-liquid 
</p>
<p>interface with zero gradient. The result was significant turbulent mixing at the interface that destroyed the thermally 
</p>
<p>stratified liquid layer there. Corresponding comparisons with pressure drop measurements were poor. Higher fidelity </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>8 
</p>
<p>methodologies will be explored in the future, but consistent treatment at gas-liquid interfaces must be used for a wide 
</p>
<p>parameter space if reliable predictions are to be made for flight programs. 
</p>
<p>A transient of the liquid temperature field for the nominal simulation of the high flow rate test, Case 434, is shown 
</p>
<p>in Fig. 6. The imposed initial temperature stratification is disrupted by the axial jet impinging on the liquid surface. 
</p>
<p>Mixing at the surface continues eventually leading to a nearly isothermal liquid by the end of the simulation. These 
</p>
<p>results are typical of every simulation conducted for this analysis.

<p>C. Phase Change Model Sensitivity 
</p>
<p>The phase change model contains a coefficient which acts as a limiter on mass transferred in a time step. A value 
</p>
<p>of 0 would inhibit mass transfer while a value of 1 would allow for a maximum amount of mass transfer. Numerical 
</p>
<p>stability issues can arise from using too high of a phase change coefficient, so values between 0 and 1 are commonly 
</p>
<p>used. Fig. 9 shows convergence of the phase change coefficient (alpha, , from Eq. 6) by at least the nominal phase 
</p>
<p>change coefficient value, 0.001, given the nominal mesh resolution and time step. Future simulations of jet mixing 
</p>
<p>will target relaxing spatial resolution of boundaries necessitating additional phase change coefficient sensitivity 
</p>
<p>studies. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

</p>
<p>Sensitivity of the pressure transient to nominal cell edge length is shown in Fig. 10. Only a minor difference exists 
</p>
<p>between the coarse mesh and more refined meshes which is likely due to capture of jet expansion or turning at the 
</p>
<p>gas-liquid interface, as the normal spacing at the interface is constant among these meshes. Convergence was clearly 
</p>
<p>reached for nominal cell edge lengths. Additional assessment of other mesh features like boundary layer spacing will 
</p>
<p>be studied in the future since it is driving mesh size and will have a profound impact on support of any related in-line 
</p>
<p>work.

<p>E. Temporal Resolution Sensitivity 
</p>
<p>Results of the time step sensitivity study are shown in Fig. 11. Little variation was observed up to the nominal time 
</p>
<p>step of 0.1 s. This result is unsurprising since RANS models smooth spatial and temporal fluctuations relaxing 
</p>
<p>resolution requirements compared to other methods that more explicitly resolve turbulence. Additionally, the phase 
</p>
<p>change model was already shown to be converged at the nominal time step. The time step achievable in this study is 
</p>
<p>enabling of future in-line work, where long duration simulations can be computationally prohibitive for practical 
</p>
<p>applications and offers room for additional model complexity that is sure to be added with flight-like designs of tanks 
</p>
<p>and mixers.

<p>V. Conclusion 
</p>
<p>CFD simulations of axial jet mixing were conducted to develop and validate a modeling methodology in 
</p>
<p>preparation for similar in-line design work. The nominal modeling methodology resulted in reasonable representation 
</p>
<p>of the pressure drop trend, magnitude, and timing. There was little variation in the RANS model solutions for axial jet 
</p>
<p>mixing across the multiple sensitivity parameters and spaces investigated. As such, a capability exists, at least within 
</p>
<p>the analyzed parameter space, to make predictions of mixing flow rate and duration demands that inform a concept of 
</p>
<p>operations and power budget.  
</p>
<p>Future study will include expanded sensitivity ranges to identify the limits of each modeling parameter. Turbulence 
</p>
<p>modeling features like imposed damping on the gas-liquid interface will be implemented. Finally, features of the sharp 
</p>
<p>interface method used within this study will be incorporated into the Loci/STREAM VoF method and validated. 
</p>
<p>Having a capable VoF method for prediction of jet-based mixing will enable simulation of axial jet flow rates that 
</p>
<p>significantly perturb the liquid interface, a scenario that may be preferential or unavoidable if in-space or reduced 
</p>
<p>gravity.
