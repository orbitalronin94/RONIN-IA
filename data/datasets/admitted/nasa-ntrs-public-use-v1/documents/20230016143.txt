We apply the Fully Numerical Predictor-corrector Aerocapture Guidance to capture a vehicle
into orbit for a mission at Uranus. Using the Genesis flight mechanics simulation, we analyze
both undispersed and dispersed trajectories in order to tune the guidance parameters. We
then assess the performance of the guidance using Monte Carlo analyses. Properly accounting
for the oblateness of Uranus within the guidance proves to be critical. We identify high-to-low
density gradients as the cause of large target orbit misses. Intentionally targeting a steeper
flight-path angle at entry interface mitigates the risk of exiting the atmosphere on a hyperbolic
orbit. Finally, We deliver the guidance code and tuned parameters for integration into the
Program to Optimize Simulated Trajectories II.

II. Introduction



Missions to other planetary bodies can utilize fully-propulsive braking or an aero-assist maneuver to insert into
a desired orbit. While fully-propulsive and aero-assist maneuvers such as aerobraking and skip entry have been



demonstrated [1, 2], aerocapture remains an untested technology with a great deal of potential to save future missions
time, mass, and cost [3]. Aerocapture is an orbital maneuver whereby the vehicle takes advantage of a planet’s atmosphere
to help reduce the amount of Δ+ , or fuel, required to insert into a target orbit. Figure 1 illustrates the maneuver.



First the vehicle skims the atmosphere, reducing the hyperbolic arrival energy using aerodynamic forces. Following
this atmospheric pass, the vehicle performs a periapsis raise maneuver followed by an apoapsis correction maneuver to

Recently, the National Academies Planetary Science and Astrobiology Decadal Survey of 2023-2032 identified
Uranus as a top priority science destination [9]. Visiting Uranus has become a priority due to its unique atmospheric
dynamics, axial tilt, and similarities to the most common of exoplanets discovered by the Kepler telescope [10]. However,
Uranus has not been visited in over three decades. One of the biggest challenges in visiting the Ice Giants is the long
transit times and high fuel required to perform a traditional, fully-propulsive orbit insertion. The more a vehicle allocates
mass to fuel, the less mass is devoted to the science payload. While aerocapture could benefit many deep space missions,
for distant destinations like Uranus, the effect of using aerocapture becomes even more impactful, with the potential
to reduce the transit time by years. For these reasons, NASA is assessing the feasibility of performing aerocapture at
Uranus, with the Ice Giants Aerocapture Project [11–13].



This paper details the application of aerocapture guidance to a Uranus orbiter mission, leveraging a method flown
in previous guided reentry missions at Earth and Mars known as bank angle control [2, 14]. While other guidance
methodologies and conceptual vehicle designs have been studied for missions to the Ice Giants, we chose to select the
architecture and guidance approach most commonly flown to reduce potential risk and complexity should an aerocapture
mission be selected over a fully propulsive orbit insertion [10]. In 2004, a team studied aerocapture at Neptune and
found that an L/D of 0.8 was required for bank control to be feasible [8].



Our results here will show how guidance and navigation advances over the past two decades make aerocapture at the
Ice Giants possible with much less L/D. Monte Carlo analysis will be discussed to quantify the mission’s sensitivities
to uncertainty and guidance parameter selection. This paper will also expand upon unique Uranus considerations in
guidance design such as its oblateness and atmospheric uncertainties. Finally, we will discuss how the Julia-based
guidance from Genesis was integrated into the Program to Optimize Simulated Trajectories II (POST2) for the project’s
runs for the record.



III. FNPAG Overview
This analysis uses the Fully Numerical Predictor-corrector Aerocapture Guidance (FNPAG) algorithm [15]. FNPAG



is based on the optimal solution to the aerocapture problem using bank angle control, which dictates a bang-bang
solution, where the vehicle first flies full lift up and then at some point switches to full lift down. The guidance consists
of two phases. In the first phase, the predictor uses the profile shown in Figure 2, where q0 and q3 are specified by the
user. The corrector iterates to solve for the value of the switching time, CB , such that the predicted radius of apoapsis is

angle command, and the corrector solves for the value of that command that achieves the radius of apoapsis target. For
the lateral channel, FNPAG uses bank reversals to target a desired orbital plane.



FNPAG is a model-based guidance and does not require extensive tuning. It uses basic vehicle and environment
parameters, such as the vehicle’s mass and the planet’s gravitational parameter, on-board models of the vehicle’s
aerodynamics and the planet’s atmospheric density, and a small number of guidance-specific parameters.



We selected FNPAG for this Uranus mission because of its previous performance in studies for both small and
large mass orbiters [6, 15–17]. The algorithm has also been shown to be robust to large uncertainties, an important
consideration for planetary bodies with limited atmospheric data [9].



IV. Simulation Overview
Genesis was used to simulate open-loop and guided three-degree-of-freedom trajectories in order to tune FNPAG.



Genesis is a generic, multi-vehicle, variable-degree-of-freedom flight mechanics simulation. It enables rapid mission
design and is capable of modeling ascent, aerocapture, entry, descent, and landing trajectories around a single planetary
body [18]. It is designed to be flexible and easily reconfigured for new vehicles. Genesis provides generic models for
atmospheric properties, winds, aerodynamics, and propulsion. It can easily be extended with new environment, vehicle,
or flight software models. Genesis can be used for a wide variety of analyses, ranging from optimizing a single trajectory
to running a large Monte Carlo analysis in a High-Performance Computing (HPC) environment. It is a successor to the
Flight Analysis and Simulation Tool (FAST) and consolidates and replaces a suite of legacy flight mechanics simulations
used at JSC. It is implemented in the Julia programming language [19].

VI. Post-Aerocapture Δ+
After the atmospheric pass, some amount of Δ+ is required to clean up the resulting orbit. This Δ+ is one of the



metrics by which we evaluate the performance of a trajectory. The concept of operations used depends on whether the
vehicle exits the atmosphere on an elliptical or hyperbolic trajectory.



For this study, the Δ+ does not include any burns to correct the orbital plane. The vehicle will perform a series of
flybys of the Uranus system to gradually adjust the orbit to the final science orbit.



A. Nominal Concept of Operations
If the trajectory exits the atmosphere on an elliptical orbit, then a sequence of two impulsive maneuvers is used to



achieve the desired orbit. First, after exiting the atmosphere, the vehicle coasts to apoapsis, at which point it performs a
burn to raise the periapsis out of the atmosphere to the desired value. The vehicle then coasts to this new periapsis, at
which point it performs a second burn to clean up any error in the apoapsis.

B. Hyperbolic Exit Concept of Operations
If the trajectory exits the atmosphere on a hyperbolic orbit, the concept of operations is more complicated, and there is



no simple conic approximation. Instead, we used Copernicus [20] to calculate higher fidelity Δ+ estimates. Copernicus
is a generalized trajectory optimization tool primarily used for exo-atmospheric trajectory design applications. With a
suite of built-in nonlinear solvers and optimizers, Copernicus allows users to efficiently define and solve constrained
nonlinear optimization problems.



For hyperbolic cases, the final state at atmospheric exit was extracted from Genesis and used to seed the initial state
in Copernicus simulations. A two-burn sequence was first proposed: the spacecraft would coast some short duration
before initiating Δ+-1, it would then coast for a variable amount of time, then Δ+-2 would be executed. The objective
function was to minimize the sum of the two Δ+ magnitudes, the optimization variables were the Δ+ vectors and the
coast time between them, and the constraints were on the semi-major axis and eccentricity of the final orbit to match the
size and shape of the target orbit. The rest of the orbital parameters were left unconstrained.



It was found that the optimal solution to the two-burn sequence placed Δ+-1 instantaneously after atmospheric exit
(i.e., zero coast time), and Δ+-2 at the apoapsis point. Though this concept of operations effectively tries to mimic a
Hohmann-like transfer, it is unable to as the atmospheric exit point (and therefore Δ+-1), occurs after passing periapsis.
A 3-burn sequence was proposed to address this shortcoming in the 2-burn sequence. When optimized, Δ+-1 of the
3-burn sequence would subtract just enough energy to place the spacecraft in a highly eccentric orbit. Δ+-2 would
again occur at apoapsis to raise periapsis altitude, and Δ+-3 would occur at the periapsis point to lower apoapsis to the
target altitude. This concept of operations represents the theoretical lower bound on Δ+ required for hyperbolic cases to
capture into a target orbit with desired apoapsis and periapsis altitudes. The hyperbolic Δ+ values shown in the results
of this paper use this 3-burn sequence.

VIII. Results



A. Undispersed



1. Open-Loop
We run open-loop cases to assess the flight-path angle corridor, which defines the range of flight-path angles within



which the vehicle has enough control authority to reach its target. For a particular constant bank angle, we can iterate
to find the flight-path angle at EI that results in the desired altitude of apoapsis at atmosphere exit. Flying full lift
up corresponds to the steepest possible flight path angle. Flying full lift down corresponds to the shallowest possible
flight-path angle. For this mission and vehicle, the flight path angle corridor at entry interface is from −11.36◦ to
−10.81◦.



2. Guided
Before we run a Monte Carlo analysis, we look at undispersed, guided cases, to ensure the guidance is properly



configured and performing well. Figure 3 shows the results. The bank angle profile is typical for FNPAG: phase 1 of
the guidance commands a bank angle of 15◦, and then FNPAG switches to phase 2 at approximately 200 s. The final
altitude of apoapsis being predicted by FNPAG tracks the target well for the majority of the trajectory. FNPAG’s lift and
drag estimators are adapting the on-board models as the vehicle flies through the atmosphere.



The late decrease in drag at 400 s, coupled with the loss of control authority as the vehicle pulls up out of the
atmosphere, results in the commanded bank angle saturating full lift down. This is expected behavior.

3. Observation Regarding Planetary Oblateness
FNPAG’s internal calculations use two versions of altitude. One version is simply the difference between the



magnitude of the vehicle’s position vector and the equatorial radius of the planet. The equations of motion in FNPAG are
non-dimensionalized, and because lengths are non-dimensionalized by the equatorial radius of the planet, this altitude
can by computed very easily as A − 1, where A is the non-dimensional magnitude of the vehicle’s position.



The second version of altitude is the planetodetic altitude, which uses a bi-axial ellipsoid model of the planet’s shape.
Originally, planetodetic altitude was only used for computing the local atmospheric density.



Past FNPAG studies have focused on Earth, Venus, and Mars. Venus is nearly spherical, but Earth and Mars are
somewhat oblate. However, the difference between their equatorial and polar radii are only about 20 km. Compared to
the celestial bodies studied previously, Uranus is both more oblate and larger. The difference between the equatorial and
polar radii is about 580 km, which is a significant portion of the EI altitude of 1000 km.



While running our initial undispersed cases for this study, we quickly realized that we needed to modify FNPAG. In
particular, we were more careful to use the planetodetic altitude when computing events (e.g., atmosphere exit) in the
predictor.



B. Dispersed
A 3,000-case Monte Carlo was performed to characterize the performance of FNPAG for this mission. Table 6



summarizes the statistics for the baseline Monte Carlo. There was a single case that exited on a hyperbolic trajectory. It
is included in all of the statistics except the Δ+ . The Δ+ of the hyperbolic case was computed separately in Copernicus
using the 3-burn sequence as described in Section VI.B and found to be 880 m/s. We will address these hyperbolic
cases further in Section VIII.C.2.



Figure 4 shows scatter plots and histograms of the baseline Monte Carlo results. The vertical dashed lines show the
steep and shallow flight-path angle corridor bounds. Note how the dispersed flight-path angle fills the entire corridor.



The first histogram plot shows the Δ+ of the first post-aerocapture burn. This burn has a non-zero mean because it
is raising the periapsis of the post-aerocapture orbit out of the atmosphere. The second histogram shows the Δ+ of the
second post-aerocapture burn. This burn corrects any apoapsis targeting error. Ideally, the guidance would hit the target
perfectly, and this burn would not be necessary. But of course in practice the aerodynamic and atmospheric dispersions
result in some apoapsis targeting error. The third histogram plot shows the total Δ+ of the two post-aerocapture burns.



The first scatter plot shows the post-aerocapture altitude of apoapsis. The results cluster around the desired altitude
of apoapsis across the dispersed range of flight-path angles, with a few outlier cases, especially on the shallow side.



The second scatter plot shows the duration of phase 1 of FNPAG, which is the phase when FNPAG commands lift
up. As expected, near the shallow boundary, FNPAG immediately computes a switching time of 0 s because it must fly
at a bank angle more lift down than q3 in order to hit the target. Towards the steeper end of the corridor, FNPAG is able
to utilize phase 1 and the optimal bang-bang structure.



The third scatter plot shows the total post-aerocapture Δ+ . Outliers occur at both the shallow and steep edges of the
corridor. Figure 5 explores the guidance performance of a representative outlier in more detail. Guidance activates at
approximately 180 s. The estimators see that drag is about 25% higher than expected early on. FNPAG adapts to this and
remains in phase 1, flying lift up. At approximately 300 s, the sensed drag and lift both decrease significantly, indicating
a sudden drop in atmospheric density. Now that it anticipates less drag for the remainder of the trajectory, FNPAG
switches to phase 2 and actually saturates at the maximum lift down bank angle command of ±165◦. The trajectory
exits in a very elliptical orbit, with an altitude of apopapsis far greater than the desired value. This kind of high-to-low
density gradient is a known difficulty for other guidance algorithms, such as skip-entry guidance.

IX. FNPAG Integration in POST2
The version of FNPAG used for this analysis was implemented in Julia. This was simple to use within Genesis,



which is also implemented in Julia. However, the runs for the record for this project are performed using POST2, which
is written in C. Rather than re-implement FNPAG in C, the team desired to call the Julia version directly.



It is straightforward to use the PackageCompiler.jl package to compile the FNPAG Julia code into a shared
library with a C API. Internally, the guidance is still being run by Julia, but the C API makes it easy to incorporate into
other tools like POST2.



In addition to the shared library, the guidance team delivers a CSV file containing the tuned guidance parameters and
a time history file containing all of the guidance inputs and outputs for a unit test case. The POST2 team exercises their
FNPAG interface with the unit test case and matches the outputs within a tight tolerance, giving the team confidence
that the guidance has been integrated correctly in POST2.



X. Conclusion
The application of a numeric predictor-corrector, bank control guidance for aerocapture at Uranus has been presented.



Nominal and Monte Carlo results indicate that bank angle control is capable of performing aerocapture at Uranus using
a low-L/D aeroshell. Guidance parameter studies identify potential trade offs between performance and robustness.
In particular, targeting the entry conditions steeper than the baseline can prevent cases from exiting the atmosphere
on hyperbolic trajectories. The Julia implementation of the FNPAG algorithm is compiled into a shared library for
integration into POST2.
