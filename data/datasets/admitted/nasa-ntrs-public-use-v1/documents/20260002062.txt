At the request of the National Transportation Safety Board, members of NASA Glenn
Research Center’s Icing Branch analyzed the available data associated with an accident involving
a Hawker 900XP. The analysis performed by the Icing Branch consisted of three distinct topics:
1) airfoil analysis, 2) meteorological and flight data analysis, and 3) a comparison to existing
experimental data. This analysis indicated that the Hawker 900XP utilized a front loaded airfoil
design that is known to be sensitive to leading edge contamination. Even though the potential
icing exposure was of a short duration, on the order of two minutes, comparable experimental
data shows that such an exposure can significantly reduce both the maximum coefficient of lift,
and the stall angle of attack. This type of short duration icing exposure may pose a significant
safety risk for a vehicle performing a stall test, such as the Hawker 900XP in this investigation.

I. Introduction
On February 7th, 2024 a Hawker 900XP (N900VA) was involved in a fatal accident near Westwater, UT [1]. During



the investigation the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR) provided the National Transportation Safety
Board (NTSB) a preliminary analysis of the meteorological conditions experienced by the Hawker 900XP during its
flight. This analysis indicated that the aircraft flew through atmospheric regions containing potential icing conditions
just prior to planned post maintenance stall testing.



At the end of May of 2025, the NTSB contacted NASA Glenn Research Center’s Icing Branch seeking subject
matter expertise in the area of in flight icing. NASA Glenn Research Center is home to the Icing Research Tunnel and
has been researching in flight icing since 1944. NASA agreed to be party to the investigation, analyze the available data,
and provide the resulting analysis to the NTSB. This document is the culmination of the analysis performed by NASA’s
Icing Branch.

II. Analysis



A. Airfoil Analysis
Due to the total loss of the aircraft, wing geometry data of the accident vehicle was unavailable. Additionally, the



investigation was unable to obtain design drawings of the wing from the airplane manufacturer. The geometry of the
wing used in this analysis was obtained via a laser scan of a similar Hawker 900XP. The scan was performed by West
Star Aviation on June 23rd, 2025 and was provided to NASA on June 26th, 2025. The results of the laser scan can be
seen in Fig. 1.



Fig. 1 Laser scan of a similar Hawker 900XP to the accident aircraft. Origin location denoted by red dot.



The scan was rotated about the origin by 24 degrees to remove the leading edge sweep, such that Cartesian slices of
the scan are perpendicular to the leading edge. Three cuts were made at the inboard, midspan and outboard region.
These cuts were 100 inches, 200 inches, and 300 inches respectively from the origin. Both a graphical depiction of the
slice cut locations and the normalized airfoil geometry can be seen in Fig. 2. The chord of the airfoil slices are depicted
in Table 2.

As mentioned previously, the investigation was unable to obtain design documents for this vehicle, so it is unclear
exactly what airfoil was used. However, Fig. 2 illustrate an airfoil design similar in profile to a NACA 23012 with
a thicker airfoil utilized in the inboard region, and a thinner airfoil used in the outboard region. The slice from the
raw scanned data created a highly resolved set of airfoil coordinates. A sparser set of airfoil coordinates was extracted
and then smoothed to reduce discontinuities in the airfoil curvature that were present due to small discrepancies in the
scanned geometry. This was done to generate a set of airfoil coordinates that could be utilized in a panel code, such as
XFOIL [2], to simulate and analyze airfoil loading. The raw data and defeatured coordinates can be seen in Fig. 3. As
can be seen, the sparser set of airfoil coordinates visually lie directly on the curve generated from the raw data.



The loading on both the Hawker Midspan airfoil, as well as the NACA 23012, were simulated in XFOIL [2].
The simulated pressure loadings can be seen in Fig. 4. Both of these airfoil are characterized as being highly front
loaded airfoils. In fact, the Hawker airfoil is even more front loaded when compared to the NACA 23012. Thus a
significant portion of lift is generated in the leading edge region of the wing. This results in desirable pitching moment
characteristics, however, it also results in a high sensitivity to leading edge contamination [3] as will be discussed later
in Section III.

C. Comparison to Existing Data
As discussed in Section II.A., the airfoil of the Hawker 900XP is similar to the NACA 23012 in both shape and



loading. Significant amounts of experimental testing was performed on a 72 inch chord NACA 23012 during the
SUNSET project (StUdies oN Scaling EffecTs due to ice), a joint effort by NASA, the Office National d’Etudes et
Recherches Aerospatiales (ONERA), and the University of Illinois. This experimental testing consisted of both ice
accretion testing in NASA’s Icing Research Tunnel (see Fig. 5), as well as aerodynamic performance testing in ONERA’s
F1 Subsonic Pressurized Wind Tunnel (see Fig. 6). Addy et al. documented the data collected from the SUNSET project
in a NASA Technical Publication [3]. A subset of that data is included and discussed here due to the comparability
between the airfoil geometries and the flight/wind tunnel conditions.

These ice accretions simulated in the IRT were recorded via castings, and these castings were used to generate
leading edge geometries that were installed on a 72 inch chord NACA 23012 and aerodynamically tested in ONERA’s
F1 Subsonic Pressurized Wind Tunnel. The results of EG1126 at a comparable Mach number to that experienced by
the Hawker 900XP are presented in Fig. 8. Note that even with such a short duration exposure, the stall angle was
reduced by over six degrees and the maximum lift coefficient decreased approximately 40%. This degradation is related
to the airfoil being highly front loaded, with any contamination on the leading edge having significant impacts on lift. It
should be noted here that these airfoil data will not translate to a 3D wing on a 1:1 basis. A fully 3D airplane wing with
sweep, taper and twist, would likely see less performance degradation than was measured on the NACA23012 airfoil
due to 3D effects. However, the data suggest that the Hawker 900XP may have experienced significant decreases in stall
angle and maximum lift from the short-duration icing exposure. Such degradations may be a significant safety risk for
an airplane undergoing stall testing.

III. Conclusion
NASA was contacted by the NTSB to provide icing related subject matter expertise for an accident involving a



Hawker 900XP airplane. This airplane potentially flew through a short duration icing encounter just prior to routine
post maintenance stall testing. The analysis conducted by NASA was composed of three parts: 1) airfoil analysis, 2)
meteorological and flight data analysis, and 3) a comparison to existing experimental data. This analysis indicated that
the Hawker 900XP utilized a front loaded airfoil design that is known to be sensitive to leading edge contamination.
Even though the potential icing exposure was of a short duration, on the order of two minutes, comparable experimental
data shows that such an exposure can significantly reduce both the maximum coefficient of lift, and the stall angle of
attack. This type of short duration icing exposure may pose a significant safety risk for an airplane performing a stall
test, such as the Hawker 900XP in this investigation.
