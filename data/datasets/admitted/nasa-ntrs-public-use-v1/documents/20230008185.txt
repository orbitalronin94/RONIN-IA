<p>Material Testing Results</p>
<p></p>
<p>In tensile testing at cryogenic and elevated temperatures up to 1400°F LP-DED NASA HR-1 exhibited good strengths and high ductility in both aging conditions
</p>
<p>Yield and ultimate strengths were lower than wrought NASA HR-1, while fracture elongation was significantly higher than wrought.  These trends were expected based on the composition changes made for LP-DED and the resulting microstructure.  The low cycle fatigue life across temperatures was also in a range good for targeted applications
</p>
<p>Tensile and low cycle fatigue tests in high pressure hydrogen exhibited no significant strength or ductility loss occurred in tensile testing and no loss of fatigue life was observed at 2% total strain for either aging cycle.  In low cycle fatigue, LP-DED NASA HR-1 outperformed wrought in a high-pressure hydrogen environment</p>

<p>AM Adaptation of NASA HR-1</p>
<p></p>
<p>NASA HR-1 is a high-strength Fe-Ni based superalloy designed to resist hydrogen environment embrittlement (HEE), oxidation, and corrosion</p>
<p>Derived from JBK-75 and A286, NASA HR-1 was designed to have higher strengths to meet the needs of liquid rocket engine applications</p>

<p>Material Characterization Through Heat Treatment</p>
<p>Post-processing heat treatments are key to obtaining optimal properties for AM alloys and characterization of the microstructure informs on how alloys will perform</p>
<p>Stress relief, homogenization, solution, and aging cycles were developed to optimize the microstructure and properties of LP-DED NASA HR-1</p>
<p>Temperatures and times were adjusted to obtain desirable microstructure, mitigate Ti segregation observed, and prevent the formation of η-phase that is detrimental to the performance of NASA HR-1 in hydrogen environments</p>
<p>Two aging cycles were developed to adjust performance of NASA HR-1:</p>
<p>2-Step age for improved HEE resistance and LCF life </p>
<p>1-Step age for higher strength</p>
<p>During characterization, various laser powers were examined to understand the impact of the LP-DED process parameters on the microstructure and properties to inform material application designs</p>
<div class="embedded" id="slide1_rId21"></div>
<p>-phase</p>
<p>-phase</p>
<p>Wrought NASA HR-1 was too expensive to produce traditionally, however additive manufacturing (AM) processes such as laser powder directed energy deposition (LP-DED) have enabled the material to be a feasible option for liquid rocket engine applications</p>
<p>The composition of NASA HR-1 was modified to adapt the alloy well to AM using PHACOMP analysis</p>
<p>Early in development of LP-DED NASA HR-1 it was observed that η-phase formed after aging, so the initial formulation was modified to the final formulation shown below to stabilize the matrix to help minimize η-phase formation and optimize properties</p>
<p>Modifications made in the final formulation were expected to improve fatigue life, thermal conductivity, ductility, and HEE resistance at the expense of strength</p>

<p>LP-DED NASA HR-1 in Application and Hot Fire Testing</p>
<p>Several manufacturing demonstration parts have been built using LP-DED NASA HR-1 to show versatility of the AM process and alloy</p>
<p>LP-DED NASA HR-1 nozzle extensions have been successfully hot fire tested from 2k lbf thrust class up to 35k under the RAMPT project and other test programs</p>
<p>Commercial space partners are actively building LP-DED NASA HR-1 nozzles for future engine applications</p>
<p>NASA HR-1 has also been used as a cladded jacket on combustion chambers and bimetallic joints between GRCop42 and LP-DED NASA HR-1 have been developed to expand the design space for combustion devices</p>
