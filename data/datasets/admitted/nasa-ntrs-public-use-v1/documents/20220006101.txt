Sustaining a human presence on the Moon, Mars, or deep space will require closing loops on many 

life support systems.  Some form of agriculture will be required because plants produce the 

vitamins, antioxidants, and essential oils in our diets that degrade over time in stored foods.  In 

addition, they provide dietary fiber, revitalize air and purify water.  It is estimated that 93 kg of 

plant nutrients are required to support one crew member per year. Growing plants will require 

recycling nutrients trapped in inedible plant stems, leaves and debris. We examined the effects of 

using thermal plasma and various carrier gases to breakdown inedible plant biomass and aid in 

nutrient recovery. Previous work demonstrated a thermally degrading environment such as a 

muffle furnace improved nutrient recovery from inedible biomass prior to an acid leaching 

process, however, a muffle furnace is an inefficient process. We tested the ability of a small scale, 

thermal plasma using carbon dioxide, nitrogen, or air as plasma carrier gases to degrade pelleted 

plant biomass. The gases were chosen to explore variations in recovery and potential chemical by-

products. Plasma treated and untreated biomass was leached with different concentrations of acid 

to determine the extraction of nutrients (potassium, magnesium, calcium, and. phosphorus) for 

reuse in the crop production cycle. Results are presented showing the impact of plasma processing 

prior to acid leaching on recovery of plant nutrients.

I. Introduction 

ustained  human habitation beyond low Earth orbit will require the use of crop plants for combined food production 

and air revitalization. Ultimately this will greatly reduce the resupply mass required to support deep space 

missions.1 Crops also have the ability to recycle human waste products (CO2, urea/ammonia in water) as part of a 

“closed” loop life support system. However, a substantial portion (50% for potatoes) of the nutrients used in each 

crop cycle ends up as inedible biomass, requiring recovery of the lost nutrients.1 A major component of plant biomass 

is cellulose and hemicellulose which ends up sequestering carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and some nitrogen (CHON) 

resources required for human survival.2,3,4 The plant cell wall matrix also restricts the availability of otherwise labile 

macro and micronutrients such as potassium, phosphorus, and magnesium, and calcium for successive crop cycles 

without significant processing, requiring large amounts of equivalent system mass (ESM). Identifying a process to 

easily “unlock” these nutrients for future crop cycles can greatly reduce resupply mass and the constrains placed on 

crew size by ECLSS loading. 

 On Earth, biomass is recycled by a consortium of micro-organisms. Plant materials are often aerobically degraded 

(composting) or broken down through anaerobic digestion.3,4 These processes break down the cellulosic biomass into 

simpler sugars that often ferment, producing volatile gases such as CO2, NH3, methane, and liquid organic acids. 

These processes meet the goals of a regenerative life support system but are slow and require large volumes. 

Furthermore, the difficulties of instrumenting, and the controlling biological processes in a microgravity environment 

are formidable. Modified approaches using aerobic CSTR (continuous stirred-tank reactor) bioreactors can speed up 

this process from months to days, but still lead to incomplete cellulose and hemicellulose conversion. Incomplete 

conversion prevents terminal carbon as well as macro and micronutrient recovery.2 

Recent studies have tried to combine various pretreatment methods of the inedible biomass with acid leaching to 

recover more essential nutrients.3 Under CSTR conditions, ground inedible biomass experienced greater leaching of 

relevant ions using stronger hydrochloric acid concentrations. This effect was improved with muffle furnace treatment 

(combustion) of the inedible biomass prior to leaching. Use of enzymatic cellulose digestion (cellulase produced from 

Trichoderma inoculum) and photo-oxidation effects failed to improve terminal leaching performance, likely due to 

the complexity of scaling down these processes and poor cellulose treatment efficiency. The need to reduce acid 

requirements to achieve inorganic nutrient recovery while simultaneously improving gasification and CHON 

recovery led to the consideration of advanced thermal methods. 

Previous work at KSC explored the use of plasma gasification as a method to reduce waste volume and produce 

usable product gases for applications in a spacecraft environment.5,6 These efforts originally focused on astronaut 

trash such as waste packaging, used clothing, and more organic wastes such as food scraps or human waste. With the 

major focus being volume reduction via gasification fraction and terminal recovery of these gases, the solid fraction 

was of lesser importance. However, key plant nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (NPK) can be 

liberated from these solid ash fractions, regardless of the source of the ash (plant, human, plastics). The resulting 

gases being similarly useful for plant growth and subsequent crop production. The results of previous efforts led 

researchers at KSC to pursue the application of thermal, direct current (DC) plasma discharge methods to process 

inedible biomass before leaching to maximize nutrient recovery.  

 Plasma is sometimes referred to as the fourth state of matter and possesses characteristics that allow treatment of 

materials for reactive chemistry, heat processing, volatile organic chemical abatement, agriculture, and medicine 

applications.7,8,9 Plasma was considered an ideal tool for the processing of inedible biomass in this work based on 

previous work where plasma properties allowed for temperatures greater than 1500 °C to be easily achieved. These 

elevated temperatures, in addition to the reactive species produced by the plasma, were expected to lead to increased 

degradation of the inedible biomass. 

  Solid nutrients remaining after the plasma process that are targets for leaching include potassium (K), calcium 

(Ca), phosphorus (P), magnesium (Mg), silicon (Si), and sodium (Na), with sulfur (S) typically being volatilized.  

Each of these are essential to the plant nutrient cycle. Re-acquiring these nutrients will further close the space crop

II. Materials and Equipment 

A. Plasma Processing Set-up and Design 
 The plasma system setup employed an AC-to-DC rectified, non-transferred arc discharge that generates a plume 

based upon gas flow through a nozzle. The power source is the Plasma Pilot PD-E-051, from Applied Plasma 

Technologies, Inc. Inedible biomass was dried, ground down, and pressed into pellet form for plasma plume 

processing. Plasma processing occurred within a quartz reaction cell with the inedible biomass pellet being held within

referred to previous works for a more in-depth report on the setup.5,6 An example of the treatment process and resulting 

pellet are seen in Figure 1. A unique aspect of plasma is the ability to drastically change the thermal properties and

B. Acid Leaching methodology 
The acid leaching methodology was developed in previous efforts at KSC.14,15 The acid leaching process consisted 

of water (0 M HCl), 0.01 M HCl, 0.1 M HCl, 1.0 M HCl, and plasma activated water (PAW). The PAW resulted in a 

pH of 2.7 after 15 minutes of DC atmospheric pressure plasma jet (APPJ) submerged in water with air as a carrier gas,

which corresponds to ≈ 0.01 M HNO3 solution. The leaching process took place over one hour. An amount of 0.25-

0.40 g of dried sample (corresponding to 1-gram original plant mass pre-gasification) was mixed with 10 mL of the 

above specified acids and stirred for 1 hour. The solution was transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube and rinsed by 

adding Nanopure water 3 times; first 10 mL, followed by 5 mL, and then another 5 mL. The total 30 mL volume was 

taken for centrifuging into an Allegra X-14R Centrifuge from Beckman Coulter at 4000 rpm for 10 minutes to get 

separation into a solid-waste pellet and an aqueous layer. The supernatant was separated, diluted to 50 mL via 

volumetric flask and filtered with 0.45 µm filter for analysis. The supernatant was analyzed using inductively coupled 

plasma – optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES).  

C. Microwave Digestion Methodology 
For microwave digestion, an amount of 150 mg of inedible biomass or plasma treated inedible biomass was 

weighed, to the nearest 1 mg. Each weighed sample was mixed with 15 ml of concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) in an 

EasyPrep Plus microwave-digestion vessel. All the sample solutions, pre-digestion, were kept in the open room 

temperature (≈30 mins) until the initial reaction stopped. Then the vessels were sealed, and the microwave-digestion 

proceeded. A maximum of twelve EasyPrep Plus vessels were used to perform a single microwave-digestion batch. 

The microwave-digestion was conducted using CEM Mars 5, manufactured by CEM Corp., USA. After the full acid 

digestion, the samples were left to cool down and then visually inspected for a possibility of incomplete digestion. 

The microwave-digestion process was followed by elemental quantification using an ICP-OES. Prior to ICP-OES 

analysis, aliquots of the digestates were further diluted 1:100 and 1:10, in 50 mL polypropylene tubes.  

D. Acid Digestion Methodology 
Dried inedible plant material (250 ± 0.01 mg) was digested in an open vessel system using 70 mL HD polyethylene 

vials using a graphite heating block (Mod Block PN 05-C0530, CPI International). Each sample was digested in 

duplicate. The material was digested at 95 °C using 8 mL of 70% nitric acid, trace metal grade, Fisher Scientific. The 

acid was carefully added to the samples and these were immediately placed inside the Mod Block and boiled for 2 

hours. The samples were allowed to cool for one hour to reach room temperature and 2.5 mL of 30% hydrogen 

peroxide (Fisher Scientific) was added to each vial.  When the peroxide reaction ceased, samples were re-heated for 

an additional hour. During the digestion, vials were covered by watch glasses. Samples were allowed to cool to room 

temperature once again and diluted to 50 mL with ultrapure DI water. Samples were stored inside the fridge and then 

processed for ICP-OES analysis. All samples were passed through a 0.45 µm filter and diluted with DI water in 

increments of 1:2, 1:10, and 1:100. A volume of 10 mL was prepared for each of the samples for analysis. 

E. Ash Characterization 
Understanding the ash content and structure is essential for improving nutrient recovery of ash leaching. Scanning 

electron microscope (SEM) Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS) was used to analyze the processed samples after 

being plasma treated with the various feedstock gases. Ash samples were taken from the processed pellet as well as a 

control of unprocessed, ground plant material and input into a JOEL JSM-7500F Field Emission-SEM with EDS 

capabilities. Several images of the different ash samples were taken with magnifications varying from 1000 to 10,000x 

magnification. Once a SEM image was selected, EDS was implemented to get the elemental analysis of specific points 

of the processed SEM image. A total of 4 to 5 points per image were selected for analysis. The resulting analysis of 

the data was an average of 20 data points. This number of points were chosen to try and mitigate the natural variability 

in the ground plant material or ash associated with the localized nature of the SEM-EDS analysis. The following 

parameters were used to obtain the EDS elemental analysis: 10 frames were taken per EDS point analysis at a 1024 x 

768 resolution and a dwell time of 12 ms per point.  The SEM was operated under vacuum conditions with 0.5-20 

µamps and 15 kV set on the system. 

ICP-OES was conducted using a Thermo Scientific™ iCAP™ 7600 ICP-OES Duo system and associated Thermo 

Scientific Qtegra™ Intelligent Scientific Data Solution (ISDS). An ICP OES is composed of a device for sample 

introduction (CETAC 580A auto sampler and a nebulizer), a radiofrequency electric power supply, a quartz torch, a 

polychromator, a detector, and a computer with Qtegra™ software for controlling the equipment, data acquisition and 

data treatment. The acidic sample solution is introduced into the ICP as a fine aerosol of droplets, produced by a 

nebulizer which aspirates the sample with high velocity argon to form a fine mist. When the aerosol reaches the argon 

plasma (operates at ~10,000 kelvin), the analytes are ionized, emitting light at known wavelengths with varying 

intensities. A high-resolution polychromator separates several emission lines and transfers resolved wavelengths for 

each analyte to the detector. Electromagnetic radiation signals, either in ultraviolet or in visible region, are converted

III. Results and Discussion 

A. Plasma Processing 
Using thermal plasma to degrade the inedible biomass pellets was expected to be more thermally efficient than the 

muffle furnace technique,14 while also being electrically more efficient. The muffle furnace is a slower heating process 

in which a large amount of energy is lost to the ambient surroundings. The plasma DC torch setup operates under 600 

Watts of electrical power with plasma temperature reaching well above 1,500 °C within the discharge region and 

thousands of degrees within the plume region. The plasma reaches these temperatures instantaneously upon discharge. 

Due to the localized nature of the plasma discharge, the energy is concentrated within the interaction region of the 

pellet for a more efficient process. Despite the improvements in efficiency, there is much room for optimization of the 

overall design. 

A plasma was produced, and the plume interacted with the pressed pellet for 20 minutes. The air plasma tends to 

follow similar reaction mechanisms to that of the gasification or vitrification process.16 In the air plasma case, the 

pellet is gasified while also being thermally degraded which leads to remaining ash content and some volatilization of 

compounds in the exhaust gas stream. The nitrogen and carbon dioxide gas streams lead to a pyrolysis-like process 

that proceeds in the absence of oxygen.16 The higher temperatures achievable in this process combined with better 

geometry (enabling more efficient mass and heat transfer) should allow plasma processing to achieve higher yields 

with less energy consumption.  

In addition to the plasma system being used to gasify the inedible biomass and leave remaining ash content, the 

same plasma source may be used to generate plasma activated water (PAW). PAW allows for pH control, nitrate 

production in water, conductivity manipulation, and production of low concentration of nitric acid that may also be 

used as a leaching agent.17 Researchers at KSC are actively investigating the uses of PAW for various purposes but it

C. Acid Digestion Results 
Acid and microwave digestion were done on both untreated and plasma treated pellets to determine if plasma 

treatment resulted in the loss of any of the mineral nutrients (K, Mg, Ca, and P). Pellets treated with N2 and CO2 

plasma had a lot of charred organic material (black/gray color). Even though the samples were left to digest for four-

hour periods, non-solubilized material was observed in the solution. This is expected when “burning” plant materials 

without oxygen.  This is essentially how you make charcoal.  Other likely biproducts are soot, tar, and ash dependent 

on the temperature.18,19 This black colored solution is not normally seen in the acid digestion process, samples usually 

turned clear when the digestion is completed. The solutions were allowed to settle, and the supernatant was filtered 

for ICP-OES analysis. 

 The averages of acid and microwave digestions results for four key plant mineral nutrients: Ca, K, Mg, and P are 

shown in Figure 4. CHON weren’t measured because they are in the gas stream for plasma treated pellets. Acid and 

microwave digestion are “total recovery” processes, so comparing the four mineral nutrients across pellets based on 

the pre-plasma pellet weights determines if these nutrients stayed in the pellets. Results indicate that approximately 

90% of the Ca, K, Mg, and P remained in the plasma treated pellets.  Differences between the plasma treatments are 

consistent with cumulative errors in the measurements. Important benefits of the plasma processing are that it allows 

for recovery of the gasified CHONs in the effluent gas stream and the remaining ash content requires a smaller waste 

storage volume than the unprocessed biomass.

Figure 5 shows the effects of plasma treating plant materials.  There are some striking differences between the 

treatments.  The first is that minerals were generally more extractable at lower acid concentrations than were the 

plasma treated pellets.  At 1M acid, however, the plasma treated pellets were more extractable (77 – 88% vs. 63%.)  

The other striking difference is that very little of the Ca, Mg, and P was extracted from the plasma treated pellets at 

acid concentrations below 1M.  This may indicate formation of compounds, for example CaCO3 and K2Ca(CO3)2 are 

known to form in 600 °C ash, but not at 1300 °C.19 These compounds would be acid soluble.  Other compounds 

containing Mg and P could explain their scarcity from the leachates below 1M acid.  The potassium was only locked 

up when air was used as the working gas and appears to have been more soluble when CO2 was the working gas.  In 

all cases, plasma treating pellets, in combination with 1M acid leaching resulted in higher recovery rates than not 

using plasma. 

Plasma treatment of the pellets has a large impact on pH and conductivity of the acid solution during the leaching 

process. Table 3 shows the pH of the leachate solution as a function of the plasma working gas and acid strength used 

during leaching. As expected, the pH of the solution decreased as the acid concentration used increased. The PAW is 

a nitric acid-based leach formed when the plasma generates nitric acid in the presence of water, whereas HCl was used 

to make up the other acid leaching solutions. 

The pH range in Table 3 shows that the ash content combined with an acid allows for pH control. Although not a 

direct need of the leaching process, other systems such as the urine process assembly or future hydroponic systems 

are sensitive to pH shifts. This work may also aid in pH control of space systems.

The experiments showed a difference in solid-to-gas conversion of the inedible biomass pellets based upon the 

plasma gas type used. When different gas types are used in the DC discharge, different plasma properties occur. This 

leads to a spread in processing temperatures within the plasma plume and altered chemical reaction mechanisms. The 

feedstock gases led to an average gasification of the inedible biomass with percentages of 77.9% (air), 61.1% (N2), 

and 57.6% (CO2). Visually, the air plasma process produced an ash pellet that was mostly grey in color with localized 

regions of green, yellow, and brown that could be the result from oxidation of minerals by plasma produced oxygen 

radicals. Observations of the nitrogen-plasma and carbon dioxide-plasma showed a few small (mm diameter) regions 

of the grey ash where the plasma plume was in direct contact with the pellet. Outside of this region, the pellets appeared 

to be formed of a black char. Acid and Microwave digestion of untreated and plasma treated pellets indicated that 

90% of the minerals remained in the plasma treated pellets.  The missing quantities may have escaped as fly ash or 

entered the gas phase. The knowledge gained here allows for an increase in space crop production loop closure. 

Although the system is in a low technology readiness level state, it provides insight to a self-sufficient system

IV. Conclusions  

The KSC team expected that the enhanced thermal processing provided by DC plasma discharges would allow for 

a greater recovery of minerals than previous methods. For the current plasma system, this was only true when used in 

combination with 1M acid leaching, apparently due to the formation of carbonates that require acids for solubilization.  

The combination of plasma treatment and leaching lead to a minimum of a 17% improvement in the recovery of 

mineral nutrients. An unexpected discovery was that the plasma produced a weak nitric acid solution when N2 or air 

was used as the carrier gas in the presence of water.  This may be a useful acid source in space, though we did not 

fully test its ability to recover the carbonates.  In theory large volumes of a dilute acid could do this, or the acid could 

be concentrated. 

Plasma degradation of the pellets enables recovery of CHON that are liberated to the gaseous exhaust stream. The 

exhaust stream may then be separated for other use such as water production, and reintroduction of the CO2 to space 

crop production, or separated for other uses. Overall, the work provides insight to the further development of plasma 

for thermally degrading wastes and for nutrient recovery via acid leaching. Further investigation of the acidification 

of water employing plasma would also eliminate the hydrochloric acid consumable and eliminate a supply chain 

bottleneck involving transport and storage of acid.

V. Future Work 

The utilization of thermal plasma for acid production for leaching is still in its infancy. Further research is needed 

to improve understanding of the plasma produced ash content to determine crystalline structures and molecular 

makeup. By optimizing the plasma set up, it may be possible to alter the plasma conditions to produce fewer 

carbonates, enhancing mineral recovery with less or no acids. In this work we used 1 M hydrochloric acid, which is a 

consumable that now requires the logistics of transportation, handling, use, and storage aboard any spacecraft or base. 

Nonetheless, the current findings are enabling for further space crop production cycle loop closure. One way to 

mitigate the logistics and costs is to generate nitric acid via plasma activated water. The ability to use the same power 

source to process the inedible biomass and generate the acid necessary to leach nutrients would be quite impactful to 

logistics reduction. It removes the need for shipping and any storage concerns as the materials needed for processing 

would be able to be generated on-demand. Additionally, the test stand presented will need significant modifications 

to improve the total capable throughput of inedible biomass. Improving the design of the processing equipment would 

also mitigate entrainment losses observed during the plasma process. The current process requires a mill, miniature 

hydraulic press, and a plasma reaction cell in three separate entities without complete gas capture for CHON recovery 

and gas containment for closed environments. Future infusion of the equipment will require a single system to be built 

that is capable of combining the process in an automated fashion to greatly reduce crew time with increased plant 

waste throughput. Since plasma activated water can use the same power source, systems engineering is capable of 

integrating the two activities into a single unit, reducing footprint and improving system versatility. Investigation of 

plasma activated water properties for maximum nutrient recovery is also desired to eliminate acid resupply. A more 

complete understanding of the insoluble precipitates at low acid concentration provides insight into using nutrient 

separation techniques such as ion exchange resins or electrostatic precipitators. Once a greater understanding of a 

higher TRL system is achieved, it is possible to perform estimates on the further closed space crop cycle based on the 

nutrients able to be recovered.
