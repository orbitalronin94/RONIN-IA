Propelling the New Generation of Manned Spaceflight


Why use CFD to model Oxygen-Methane combustion applications at NASA?


Liquid Oxygen (LOX) and Methane (CH4) propellant fueled engines have been considered for In-Space Propulsion and a number of developing programs


Mars Lander concepts, Potential RL-10 replacement, SpaceX Raptor


Methane offers several advantages over fuels traditionally used by NASA and others in the space flight industry


Denser than Hydrogen, More energy per volume than H2; Less corrosive than Hydrazine


Methane Ignition presents unique and difficult challenges and is a significant risk for In-Space Ignition


Ignition of Hydrocarbon fuels is complex and stable combustion has been historically difficult for engine design


RL-10 was a H2/O2 Engine and presented significant ignition challenges at altitude, Methane would present additional challenges


Methane stored as a cryogenic liquid like H2, experiences phase changes at ambient temperatures and pressures unlike some other hydrocarbons, and requires a separate oxidizer unlike monopropellants


Shallow base of design experience in Space Flight propulsion applications


Empirical test-based experience focused on hydrogen, RP and hypergolic fuels

Computational Challenges of Combustion Modeling


Why use CFD to model combustion?


CFD-guided design can be cheaper and safer than an empirically-guided test development program


Why is combustion modeling considered to be computationally expensive?


Non-reacting CFD simulations have a significant set of base equations


Numerical methods used to solve 5 base PDEs that comprise the Navier-Stokes equations, plus turbulence closure equations and a species balance equation for multi-species 


Finite-rate chemistry modeling of combustion adds additional equations


1 additional scalar equation for each additional species is added to the computation, practical chemistry models have 10-100+ species


Finite-rate models march slowly in physical time 


Simulations typically require small time step sizes to be numerically stable


Flamelet Models provide an efficiency gain for combustion CFD simulations


Chemical reactions are computed for a fuel and oxidizer combination and tabulated prior to the CFD simulation


Combustion modeling in the CFD simulation achieved using just 2 new PDEs 


Modest efficiency gain in time-step size 


Ultimately, complexity, resolution and completion time of the CFD simulations is limited by computational resource availability


ER42 uses the NAS HPC resources at NASA Ames: Pleiades, Electra and Aitken Supercomputing Clusters

Introduction to three modern combustion CFD simulations conducted to support In-Space propulsion development projects


Ignition modeling of gaseous oxygen and methane flow inside a linear array chamber using LOCI\Chem


Project goal was to study to onset of transverse combustion instabilities


Finite-rate chemistry model with 12 species and 20 chemical reactions used to provide accurate flame speed propagation through the chamber


Mainstage combustion modeling of liquid oxygen and methane flow in an engine design concept using shear flow injector elements


Project goal was to extract heat flux distribution to chamber walls for a range of conditions to ensure that enough heat was extracted from the chamber walls to drive the turbine 


Flamelet model utilized to enable efficient use of a large mesh and complex geometry


Mainstage combustion modeling of liquid oxygen and methane flow in an engine design concept using impinging flow injector elements


Project goal here was to quantify the heat release distribution in the primary combustion zone to quantify potential combustion instability modes


Flamelet model utilized to enable efficient use of a large mesh and complex geometry: Largest combustion CFD simulation completed by ER42 to date, >100 Injector Elements and 600+ Million Volume Cells

Liquid Oxygen and Methane Combustion Inside Concept Engine Chamber





Blue Iso-surface shows where flame rapidly transitions from majority unburned O2 and CH4 propellant to majority combustion products such as CO2, CO and H2O


The green-yellow wall surface plot shows contours of heat flux into the chamber wall. The hardware designed to have higher heat flux at the aft end (right side) of the chamber which is uncommon in most chamber design concepts. Traditional, empirically derived design tools are not capable of accurately predicting this type of axial heat flux distribution down the chamber wall. The CFD-predicted wall heat flux was used to develop the turbine power cycle. Note: The sudden wall heat flux change halfway through the animation was part of a parametric study to help thermal analysts understand model changes due to fluctuating temperate at the solid walls.

Liquid Oxygen and Methane Combustion Inside Chamber with Impinging Injectors











Blue Iso-surface shows where flame rapidly transitions from majority unburned O2 and CH4 propellant to majority combustion products such as CO2, CO and H2O





Cross-section shows temperature contours. Combustion gas temperature rapidly increases just downstream of blue iso-surface, vigorous combustion zone.





The green-yellow wall surface plot shows contours of heat flux into the chamber wall.


The impinging injector elements force rapid combustion of the propellants as indicated by the relatively short persistence of the blue iso-surface down the chamber. Peak combustion heat release occurs at a location just downstream of the iso-surface. Since the gases in the chamber are moving, this location has a time associated which is called sensible time lag. Sensible time lag is used in combustion stability calculations to predict frequencies and mode shapes of combustion instability events. CFD was able to provide this time lag parameter which is not well quantified in traditional combustion stability analysis methods for this injector type.

Next Steps in ER42 CFD Combustion Modeling


CFD Model Fidelity Improvements


Equation of State robustness improvements – Enable robust simulations of two liquid propellants at sub-critical conditions such as engine start


Hypergolic Flamelet Model – Enable modeling combustion of two liquid hypergolic propellants which spontaneously ignite on contact





Harnessing Additional Computational Resources


Enable finer mesh CFD models to improve model spatial accuracy and better simulation temporal resolution while maintain impactful analysis completion times


Include additional engine geometry such as propellant manifolds – Critical for a CFD-only prediction of combustion instability events
