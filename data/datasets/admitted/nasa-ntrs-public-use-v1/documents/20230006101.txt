<p>Lunar South Pole (LSP)
</p>
<p>• NASA intends to go to the LSP, this decade, with partners
</p>
<p>• Potential landing sites are south of 84° S latitude, so sun elevation in sky is 
</p>
<p>never higher than about 7°
</p>
<p>• This means sun is often in field of view during EVA, and even small objects 
</p>
<p>cast long shadows
</p>
<p>• The lighting at the LSP is very different than the Apollo experienced at mid-
</p>
<p>latitudes</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Lunar Surface Safety at the South Pole- Illumination
</p>
<p>• Clear vision is important to every task expected of Artemis Astronauts
- Lack of visibility could cause a fall, or damage to the suit, resulting in permanent injury or 
</p>
<p>death
</p>
<p>- Exploring the LSP region means that the human vision system will be put in the position of 
adapting to extremely bright light and very dark shadows
</p>
<p>- The ability to see clearly both close up (hands and feet) as well as the surrounding 
environment is essential to astronaut safety
</p>
<p>• Artemis astronauts will be exploring the lunar south pole (LSP), a region only seen 
through cameras and robots
</p>
<p>- Representative simulations can aid in preparing crew for the expedition
</p>
<p>• Illumination is influenced both by natural environments as well as any provided lighting 
equipment
</p>
<p>• Natural illumination ranges from much darker than earth experience to much brighter; 
both cause hazards
</p>
<p>• Providing integrated, adaptable tools and training mitigates the hazard to crew and can 
aid them in managing the LSP environment</p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>LSP environment provides unique challenges to human vision 
</p>
<p>• Mission success requires accomplishment of vision-based tasks
</p>
<p>• Ambient lighting is highly variable, from high brightness to deep shadow, and requires adaptable 
</p>
<p>solutions in artificial lighting and glare protection
</p>
<p>− The extent to which the shadows are lit depends on the position of Earth and sun 
</p>
<p>in the LSP sky and on the location of and distance to (and quality of) any source of reflected 
</p>
<p>light 
</p>
<p>• While deep shadows may be partially lit by Earthshine or reflections from terrain,  they 
</p>
<p>often will be so dark that the eye cannot discern large features (e.g., obstacles), especially 
</p>
<p>in transition from lit areas to shadow
</p>
<p>− Low sun angle will result in the sun being in astronauts’ field of view for tasks in its general 
</p>
<p>direction
</p>
<p>• Astronauts working near LSP will experience potentially painful or disabling glare, high 
</p>
<p>sunlight intensity, and deep shadows
</p>
<p>• Glare and intense light will make ambulation, driving, or near-field tasks in the direction of 
</p>
<p>the sun difficult or even impossible</p>
<p></p>

<p>LSP Surface Features – Terrain and Geology
</p>
<p>7
</p>
<p>• Current knowledge of rock and crater distribution and size primarily 
</p>
<p>from Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) (5m resolution)
</p>
<p>• Rocks are relatively rare on the lunar surface
</p>
<p>– Mainly found around geologically newer craters and sloughed off from escarpments
</p>
<p>– Rocks are twice as reflective as regolith
</p>
<p>• More craters at LSP than at most Apollo sites </p>
<p></p>

<p>Natural Environments: LSP Lighting
Position of sun in sky:
</p>
<p>• Never higher than 7° at potential landing regions
</p>
<p>• Sun moves across the sky at 12.86°/24h (= 1 Earth day)
</p>
<p>• Shadows are long and move extensively within a 6-day mission
</p>
<p>8</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Low angle of incidence and extensive 
</p>
<p>shadowing will result in much 
</p>
<p>colder surface temperatures 
</p>
<p>than Apollo experienced. These 
</p>
<p>will create hazards: 
</p>
<p>Mean temperature at 85° latitude 
</p>
<p>is ~60 K (-214 °C) at 
</p>
<p>“3 AM equivalent” of lunar night.  
</p>
<p>Permanently shadowed craters 
</p>
<p>are colder. This will create risk 
</p>
<p>if astronaut falls in a shadow and 
</p>
<p>is in contact with the surface.*
</p>
<p>9
</p>
<p>* The time when one can safely be in contact with 
the lunar surface depends on location (permanently 
shadowed regions (PSRs) are coldest) and suit 
capabilities, such as heating. The existing suit 
requirement to maintain a stable thermal condition 
does not seem to address such anomalies as an 
astronaut being prone on the surface.
</p>

<p>Apollo Program Experience
</p>
<p>Apollo missions landed at equatorial sites
• Fewer craters, boulders in terrain than in other lunar regions
</p>
<p>Higher sun angles
• Sun angles during Apollo 12’s EVA-1 closest analog to 
</p>
<p>Artemis missions
</p>
<p>− Apollo 12 EVA-1: 7.5° to 9.5° (over 4-hour duration)
</p>
<p>− Artemis missions: ~2° to ~6° (depending on “season” and landing location)
</p>
<p>Low sun angle impacted Apollo 12 mission task 
</p>
<p>performance
• Color camera disabled when its sensor was inadvertently 
</p>
<p>pointed at the sun
</p>
<p>• Sun’s brightness was commented upon five times in EVA-1 
</p>
<p>transcript; moved into a shadow several times to improve task 
</p>
<p>visibility
</p>
<p>− “Wait until I get in this shadow. Because I can’t see what I’m doing looking right 
</p>
<p>into the sun.” (Pete Conrad, EVA-1 Transcript, p. 25)
</p>
<p>− “Now, I can’t see it (camera he was lowering) on account of the sun, so tell me 
</p>
<p>when it’s over the handrail.” (Pete Conrad, EVA-1 Transcript, p. 29)
</p>
<p>− “I think we need to modify the visor so that you have a center-top shield that you 
</p>
<p>can pull down and blink (sic) the Sun out.” (Pete Conrad, 1969 Technical 
</p>

<p>Human Visual System
</p>
<p>Human vision provides the primary sensory input for situation awareness in most physical and 
</p>
<p>many cognitive EVA tasks
</p>
<p>This is especially true given that other senses have no (hearing, smell) or extremely limited 
</p>
<p>(touch) environmental input during EVA
</p>
<p>Human vision is highly capable (e.g., dynamic range greatly exceeds cameras’) and reliable; 
</p>
<p>however, it will be subject to: 
</p>
<p>Glare*, making it difficult to distinguish near-field objects (craters, rocks, designed systems)
</p>
<p>Temporary blindness (2-30 min), when exposed to intense light 
</p>
<p>Light adaptation delays and limits (our system is tuned for terrestrial, not lunar, environment)
</p>
<p>4/19/2023
12
</p>
<p>*glare reduces visual performance and visibility and often is accompanied by distracting discomfort</p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>Brightness Adaptation
On Earth, light intensities range across 9 orders of magnitude
</p>
<p>A piece of pure white paper can be 1,000,000,000 times brighter in full sunlight than on a 
</p>
<p>moonless night.
</p>
<p>But for a given lighting condition, light ranges over 2-3 orders of magnitude
</p>
<p>If we were sensitive to the entire range all the time, we’d be unable to discriminate lightness 
</p>
<p>levels in the current scene.
</p>
<p>Instead, the human visual system calibrates its dynamic range to match ambient light levels:
</p>

<p>A Quick Overview of Visual Receptors
Human retina contains rods (which supports monochromic vision) and three* types of cones 
</p>
<p>(which support chromatic vision)
</p>
<p>Cone density higher than rods in Macular region, with highest density in the Foveal and 
</p>
<p>Parafoveal regions.
</p>
<p>4/19/2023
14
</p>
<p>*Roughly 1% of the population have four types of cones (tetrachromats) </p>

<p>Adaptation Mechanisms
</p>
<p>4/19/2023
</p>
<p>First, the pupil can contract or expand in diameter (~2-8 mm range)
</p>
<p>Accomplished in seconds (slightly longer to expand than contract)
</p>
<p>This buys us about two orders of magnitude
</p>
<p>Second, our vision shifts its 
balance between photopic 
(cone-based) and scotopic (rod-
based)
</p>
<p>Third, both cones and rods 
</p>
<p>adjust their sensitivity
</p>
<p>Cones adjust more quickly 
</p>
<p>(~10 minutes), but have 
</p>
<p>smaller adjustment range
</p>
<p>Rods have much larger 
</p>
<p>adjustment range, but take longer (30 min for max)

<p>LSP Lighting – Likely Effects on Vision-Based Tasks
</p>
<p>Intensity &amp; Glare
</p>
<p>• Will be a factor any time the sun (or reflective surface) is in the astronauts’ 
field of view
</p>
<p>Shadow
</p>
<p>• Sources: 
</p>
<p>− Designed systems: self (suit), lander, and eventually rover and habitat
</p>
<p>− Natural systems: rocks, craters, slopes
</p>
<p>− Depth of shadows will be highly variable as there will often be reflected light
</p>
<p>Changing light
</p>
<p>• Over the course of a single surface mission, natural light will change 
dramatically; artificial lighting and other vision support will need to adapt 
to support tasks; e.g., translocation
</p>
<p>17</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Artemis illumination and vision protection will be more 
difficult to attain than in Apollo
</p>
<p>• Sun will never be higher than 7° elevation at 
</p>
<p>Artemis landing sites (three fingers at arms 
</p>
<p>length)
</p>
<p>• Whenever the sun is in the astronauts’ visual 
</p>
<p>field, it MUST be occluded or otherwise 
</p>
<p>attenuated (while avoiding complete visual 
</p>
<p>blackout)
</p>
<p>• Whenever the astronaut is working in shadow, 
</p>
<p>additional illumination MUST be provided 
</p>
<p>(lights, reflected/diffused sunlight, etc.)
</p>

</p>

insufficient for LSP sun angles). Note lack of any suit lights.
</p>
<p>Apollo suit design did not deal with vision protection and illumination in ways that 
are sufficient for Artemis</p>

<p>The real problem:
The human eye, when adapted to one state - in light or darkness – cannot see outside its 
</p>
<p>natural dynamic range range (box, in previous slides).  The moon’s extreme lighting/darkness 
exceeds earth-bound experience. 
</p>
<p>• Head above shadow:  cannot see terrain in 
</p>
<p>front of feet because of dynamic range of eye
</p>
<p>4/19/2023
19
</p>
<p>• Head in shadow (i.e., step into shadow):  
</p>
<p>cannot see terrain in front of feet because of 
</p>
<p>adaptation time, for visual range to shift to dark 
</p>
<p>and be able to use artificial light</p>
<p></p>

<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Conclusions
</p>
<p>• Vision will be impaired during even the simplest of tasks, and all EVA tasks will rely on vision
</p>
<p>• Artemis landing regions provide more challenging environments than Apollo landing sites; operational terrain 
</p>
<p>is believed to be more rugged than even that at equatorial highlands (Apollo 16)
</p>
<p>• The sun will always be near horizon, creating glare/bright light and extensive shadowing
</p>
<p>• Lunar surface operations of 50 years ago were never conducted with sun angles as low as Artemis crews 
</p>
<p>will encounter on all missions.
</p>
<p>• While Apollo astronauts reported difficulty due to glare, they did not experience the dynamic lighting 
</p>
<p>conditions of LSP;  those astronauts were advised to not look at sun, but this advice will be nearly 
</p>
<p>impossible to follow at LSP
</p>
<p>• Very deep, persistent shadows will be created by terrain and human systems, and astronauts will need to be 
</p>
<p>able to enter/exit them frequently
</p>
<p>• Human vision is adapted to earth conditions and severely challenged by lighting conditions of LSP; the 
</p>
<p>dynamic LSP lighting will create glare and shadow that will overwhelm  the ability of astronauts’ vision to 
</p>
<p>adapt
</p>

<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>What is needed
</p>
<p>An integrated designed solution, addressing both vision protection (helmet) and lighting, is required; these
</p>
<p>solutions must address:
</p>
<p>• Protection of human vision from being overwhelmed by sun’s brightness and glare
</p>
<p>• Highly-integrated lighting architecture to support all human tasks, through the dynamic natural environment
</p>
