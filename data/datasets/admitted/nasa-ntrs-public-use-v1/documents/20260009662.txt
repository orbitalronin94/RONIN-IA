Volume filtered subgrid closures for upscaled
modeling of flow in a porous matrix



By P. Nilket†, D. Fust†, B. Dias‡, AND S. V. Apte†



Accurate Eulerian prediction of flow over a porous matrix requires closure models for
fluid-solid momentum exchange that capture strong local spatial heterogeneities. Pore-
resolved simulation data for flow through random sphere packings over a wide range of
global porosities and particle Reynolds numbers are used to assess local closures for drag
and subfilter stress by volume-filtering at scales proportional to particle size. Applying
the Tenneti correlation with local filtered porosity and Reynolds number produces sub-
stantial local errors. Re-optimizing the correlation separately for each filter width reduces
the error at low and moderate Reynolds numbers, but provides limited improvement at
high Reynolds number and porosity, where local drag becomes increasingly non-collinear
with the filtered velocity. Application of the standard closure to turbulent flow over a
porous matrix also shows large deviations near the fluid-porous interface, but substantial
improvement is obtained when trained with data in the interfacial region. These results
indicate that local porosity and Reynolds number alone are insufficient for a general Eu-
lerian force closure and motivate investigation of additional directional descriptors of the
local flow and pore geometry. Finally, a nonlinear gradient Clark model provides good
prediction for the subfilter stress.



1. Introduction



Accurate prediction of momentum exchange between a fluid and a porous material
is important in applications ranging from hydrological porous-surface flows to ablative
thermal protection systems (TPS). In ablative TPS, pyrolysis and oxidation progressively
modify the material microstructure and porosity, while aerodynamic loading and internal
gas flow produce spatially heterogeneous stresses within the porous matrix. These local
variations are relevant to material recession and mechanical removal processes such as
spallation. Consequently, predictive models must capture not only the mean momentum
exchange across a porous material, but also its local spatial variation.
Recent reviews of ablative TPS modeling have emphasized the need to couple the ex-



ternal flow, transport within the porous material, and evolving material response using
a unified framework (Mansour et al. 2024). A convenient approach is to represent the
fluid and porous material within a single computational domain using volume-averaged
Navier–Stokes equations (Whitaker 1996). In this formulation, the governing conserva-
tion laws are applied throughout the domain without explicitly tracking a fluid–porous
boundary, making the approach attractive for evolving materials. Recent unified flow–
material formulations for TPS have similarly demonstrated the benefits of solving the
flow and material response within the same computational domain, while highlighting
the importance of appropriate effective properties in the interface region (Dias et al.

2024). In upscaled continuum formulations, the effective drag force due to unresolved
porous microstructure is represented through Ergun-type macroscopic closures (Ergun,
S. 1952) with the appropriate free-fluid and porous-flow limits recovered as the local
porosity approaches unity or the flow becomes viscously dominated. The accuracy of the
single-domain formulation therefore depends critically on the closure used to represent
fluid–solid momentum exchange.
Conventional drag correlations provide this closure in terms of bulk or averaged quanti-



ties. For example, the correlation of Tenneti et al. (2011) predicts the mean fluid–particle
drag in random assemblies of spheres as a function of solid volume fraction and Reynolds
number. Such correlations are useful for representing the average resistance of a sta-
tistically homogeneous porous medium, but they do not account for the strong spatial
variation of force generated by the local pore-scale geometry. Near a porous interface,
protruding solid structures and local variations in porosity produce highly nonuniform
pressure and viscous stresses that are not represented by a closure based only on the
local mean porosity. Particle-resolved simulations have demonstrated that these local
force variations are not described by the global parameters used in conventional drag
correlations (Karra et al. 2023).
Recent studies have shown that local flow and structural information obtained from



volume-filtered or particle-resolved simulations can improve force closures beyond corre-
lations based solely on global or ensemble-averaged quantities (van Wachem et al. 2025).
Here, we follow this general idea while focusing on a different modeling objective: predict-
ing the local fluid–solid momentum exchange within a single-domain Eulerian formulation
of flow over a porous matrix. We examine established macroscopic closures for drag force
and subfilter stress, and investigate how their predictive capability changes when the
global porous-medium descriptors are replaced by local measures of porosity and its spa-
tial variation. The resulting models are evaluated against volume filtered particle-resolved
simulations with different filter widths to determine whether conventional macroscopic
correlations can be systematically modified to capture the strong spatial heterogeneity
of drag near a porous interface. This approach provides a simple closure framework that
retains the structure and limiting behavior of established models while extending them
to local Eulerian predictions.

4. Results



With the pore-resolved database validated and the filtering procedure established, we
first examine whether macroscopic drag correlations can represent the spatially varying
fluid–solid momentum exchange when evaluated using local filtered quantities.
Figure 3 shows scatter plots comparing the drag force in the streamwise direction



from the filtered DNS data with model predictions using (i) Ergun-type permeability
model (Ergun, S. 1952), and (ii) Tenneti drag force model (Tenneti et al. 2011) for parti-
cle assemblies. Both correlations were derived for the bulk drag force of a homogeneous
assembly as a function of its global porosity obtained using the entire domain as the repre-
sentative elementary volume. In the present work, these model predictions are evaluated
locally, using filter widths proportional to the particle size and using the filtered porosity
and velocity fields that vary strongly on the particle scale. Both models show significant
departure from local filtered DNS data; the Tenneti model provides closer baseline and
is therefore selected for subsequent optimization to improve local drag prediction.

Despite this favorable a priori agreement, coupling the Clark model with the optimized



drag closure in a volume-averaged solver produced numerical instabilities. Clipping and
a mixed formulation reduced but did not eliminate the instability, whereas the drag clo-
sure alone produced stable and improved filtered velocity fields. The gradient form of
the Clark model permits local backscatter and, in the present variable-porosity formula-
tion, includes terms proportional to porosity gradients. These features provide plausible
mechanisms for destabilizing the a posteriori solution and warrant further analysis of
the modeled subfilter-energy transfer.



4.3. Interfaces with external flows



The drag force closure model was evaluated on turbulent flows over a randomly packed
monodispersed spheres at εγ,0 = 0.41 and permeability Reynolds number of 2.56 (Reτ ∼
270), corresponding to the DNS data of Karra et al. (2023). A three-dimensional quartic
kernel with σ = 2dp in the periodic axial and spanwise directions and and a narrower
filter of σ = 0.5dp in the bed-normal direction is used to avoid overly diffusing the
interface, while retaining a smooth porosity field deeper within the bed. Figure 7(a)
compares normalized filtered DNS drag force to standard Tenneti model prediction. The
baseline model overpredicts the drag force by an order of magnitude, particularly near
the interface region. Optimized drag closure based on triply periodic particle assemblies
offers a modest improvement as shown in Figure 7(b), but transfers poorly to interfacial
region where the local Reynolds number extends beyond the training range, and exposed
particles experience strong form drag and force–velocity misalignment.
When interfacial samples are included during optimization, the model can fit the inter-



facial force distribution much more closely. Adding 100000 samples from the interfacial
case to the training set substantially reduces the error as shown in Figure 7(c). This
demonstrates that the chosen functional form can represent interfacial behavior but does

5. Conclusions



Pore-resolved simulation data on flow through triply periodic randommatrix of monodis-
persed spheres for a range of global porosity (0.4–0.95) and Reynolds numbers (0.1–100)
was analyzed with varying filter widths proportional to particle size to evaluate the local
drag force. The Tenneti drag closure, originally formulated in terms of global porosity
and Reynolds number for macroscopic prediction, produces substantial local errors when
applied to filtered fields, with relative L2 errors of 0.42 on average and up to 1.08. Re-
optimizing the model separately for each filter width reduces the error, with the largest
improvements at low and moderate Reynolds numbers and high porosity, but limited
improvement at high Reynolds number. At Rep = 100, the pore-scale flow is unsteady,
producing strong transverse velocity fluctuations and local drag that is frequently non-
collinear with the filtered velocity, exposing a structural limitation of the scalar drag
formulation. The closure trained on triply periodic assemblies also transfers poorly to

with interfacial samples substantially improves the prediction. These results indicate
that local porosity and Reynolds number alone are insufficient to represent the local
drag over the full range of conditions considered, and motivate for future closure models
incorporating additional directional descriptors of the flow and pore-scale geometry.
