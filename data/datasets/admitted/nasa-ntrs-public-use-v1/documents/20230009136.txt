<p>Comparative Analysis for EMU Fleet Latent Loading 
</p>
<p>Characterization in Support of US EVA 80 Failure 
</p>
<p>Noah Andersen1 
</p>
<p>HX5, LLC, JSC Engineering Technology and Science (JETS2), Houston, TX, 77058 
</p>
<p>During United States Extravehicular Activity 80 (US EVA 80), water was observed in the 
</p>
<p>helmet of an Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU) during cabin repressurization. One of the 
</p>
<p>primary mechanisms that can cause water in the helmet of an EMU is integrated 
</p>
<p>performance induced sublimator carryover. The sublimator is a heat exchanger that 
</p>
<p>removes heat and humidity from the ventilation loop. Water vapor is condensed from the 
</p>
<p>gas and removed by slurper holes in the sublimator. Sublimator carryover is caused by the 
</p>
<p>inability of the EMU sublimator to remove all of the condensed water vapor, resulting in 
</p>
<p>liquid water entering the helmet. To determine if sublimator carryover was a likely cause of 
</p>
<p>the US EVA 80 failure, a comparative analysis of numerous historical EVAs was conducted 
</p>
<p>to calculate the total latent load (total water vapor generated by the crewmember) for 
</p>
<p>numerous historical EVAs and ground tests using the Systems Improved Numerical 
</p>
<p>Differencing Analyzer EMU (SINDA EMU) model. 
</p>
<p>The analysis showed that US EVA 80 was associated with a comparatively high latent 
</p>
<p>load when compared to other EVAs that did not present water in the helmet. Further, this 
</p>
<p>analysis showed that other historical EVAs which had visible water in the helmet were also 
</p>
<p>associated with higher latent loads. This analysis provides evidence that the likely cause of 
</p>
<p>the US EVA 80 water in the helmet event was not the failure of an individual component, but 
</p>
<p>rather sublimator carryover caused by excessive production of water vapor by the 
</p>
<p>crewmember. The evidence from this analysis agrees with results from the Test, Teardown, 
</p>
<p>and Evaluation (TT&amp;E) of the EMU which did not show any failure of individual 
</p>
<p>components of the EMU that would lead to water in the helmet. 
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>URING the repressurization phase of the United States Extravehicular Activity 80 (US EVA 80) on 23 March 
</p>
<p>2022, liquid water was observed in the helmet of an Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU). Water in the helmet 
</p>
<p>poses a significant risk to the crewmember as it may restrict their ability to see and breathe. The risk associated with 
</p>
<p>liquid water in the helmet of an EMU resulted in an investigation into the failure experienced during US EVA 80. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
Figure I-1: EMU Schematic1 
</p>
<p> Figure I-1 shows a schematic of the EMU Portable Life Support System (PLSS)1. In this analysis, two loops will 
</p>
<p>be discussed: the ventilation loop (in yellow) which is responsible for providing oxygen gas (O2) and removing 
</p>
<p>humidity and carbon dioxide (CO2), and the transport loop (in red) which is responsible for cooling the 
</p>
<p>crewmember. Figure I-2 shows a simplified diagram of the ventilation loop. During an EVA, the crewmember adds 
</p>
<p>water vapor to the ventilation loop through respiration, evaporation of sweat, and diffusion through the skin. The 
</p>
<p>sum of these three sources of water vapor is called the latent heat (also referred to as the latent load). This is 
</p>
<p>calculated by multiplying the heat of vaporization of water by the mass flow rate of the water vapor being added to 
</p>
<p>the ventilation loop. Once this water is evaporated into the ventilation loop gas, it travels to the sublimator which 
</p>
<p>cools off the gas and allows liquid water to condense. The sublimator contains numerous slurper holes which are 
</p>
<p>designed to remove the condensed, liquid water from the sublimator. Sublimator carryover occurs when the slurper 
</p>
<p>holes are unable to remove all of the liquid water, allowing some of the water to exit the sublimator and make its 
</p>
<p>way to the helmet. The amount of sublimator carryover depends on the latent load sent to the sublimator as well as 
</p>
<p>the performance of the sublimator. For this analysis, the latent load experienced during US EVA 80 will be 
</p>
<p>compared to numerous historical EVAs and chamber tests to identify trends that may exist between US EVA 80 and 
</p>
<p>other EVAs/tests that presented some form of water in the helmet.  
</p>

<p> 
Figure I-2: Simplified Diagram of Interactions Between Crew and Ventilation Loop 
</p>
<p>In addition to the ventilation loop, the EMU contains a transport loop which is responsible for removing excess 
</p>
<p>heat from the crewmember using liquid water. The amount of cooling received by the crewmember depends on the 
</p>
<p>position of the Thermal Control Valve (TCV). The TCV has 13 settings (H,0,1,2,…9,10,C) with higher settings 
</p>
<p>providing more cooling to the crewmember. At TCV settings below 3, the flowrate through the Liquid Cooling and 
</p>
<p>Ventilation Garment (LCVG) drops below nominal flowrate to further reduce cooling. This is called LCVG bypass 
</p>
<p>mode. At TCV settings greater than 3, the LCVG water flowrate is constant. Cooling levels are based on the portion 
</p>
<p>of water that is sent to the sublimator. Higher TCV settings correspond with a larger split of the flowrate being sent 
</p>
<p>to the sublimator. The position of the TCV is adjusted manually throughout an EVA. The TCV position directly 
</p>
<p>impacts the cooling received by the crewmember which impacts their heat storage (measurement of the deviation in 
</p>
<p>body temperature from neutral). The heat storage of the crewmember will impact the sweat production rate and 
</p>
<p>ultimately affect the latent load. For this reason, the TCV setting is critical towards calculating the latent load. 
</p>
<p>II. Model Description 
</p>
<p>A. SINDA EMU Background 
</p>
<p>For this analysis, the latent load during an EVA was 
</p>
<p>calculated using the Systems Improved Numerical 
</p>
<p>Differencing Analyzer EMU (SINDA EMU) Model. This is 
</p>
<p>a system-level model of the crewmember and EMU PLSS 
</p>
<p>(See Figure II-1). The model was initially developed in the 
</p>
<p>1970s and was correlated to EMU certification runs in 
</p>
<p>Chamber B at Johnson Space Center (JSC)2,3. After the 
</p>
<p>initial development of SINDA EMU, the model was 
</p>
<p>improved through implementation of the 41-node metabolic 
</p>
<p>man model (METMAN)4. METMAN models the blood, 
</p>
<p>muscle, fat, and skin of the crewmember and calculates all 
</p>
<p>three sources of latent load (sweat, respiration, diffusion). 
</p>
<p>During the 1990s, SINDA EMU was re-correlated to the 
</p>
<p>EMU performance verification tests5. Many of the 
</p>
<p>adjustments made during this correlation effort were readdressed during this analysis based on new test data (see 
</p>
<p>Section II.B). Finally, SINDA EMU was upgraded to FORTRAN 90 in 20166.  
</p>
<p>B. SINDA EMU Re-correlation 
</p>
<p>Prior to this analysis, the most recent correlation effort on SINDA EMU was in the 1990s. The 1990s correlation 
</p>
<p>effort resulted in five significant changes to the SINDA EMU model5. 
</p>
<p>1. Limit suit outlet dew point to never exceed LCVG water outlet temperature. 
</p>
<p>2. Sweat runoff (generated sweat that is not immediately evaporated into gas) is re-evaporated into the 
</p>
<p>vent loop when possible. 
</p>
<p>3. Calculation of LCVG undergarment temperature was adjusted to depend on LCVG water temperature. 
</p>
<p>4. LCVG heat transfer coefficient correlation was adjusted. 
</p>
<p>5. Conductance between the sublimator and valve module was adjusted. 
</p>

<p>In the current analysis, these changes were investigated to determine if they should be kept in SINDA EMU. 
</p>
<p>Changes (4) and (5) were addressed through adjustments based on newer data in Section II.C and for this reason will 
</p>
<p>not be investigated in the re-correlation effort. To assess the validity of changes (1), (2), and (3), SINDA EMU was 
</p>
<p>used to model eleven tests from the Exploration EMU (xEMU) PLSS 2.0 Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) test series7,8. 
</p>
<p>The PLSS for xEMU is different from the EMU PLSS. However, the LCVG used during this test series is the same 
</p>
<p>LCVG used during EVAs. For this reason, the PLSS portion of SINDA EMU was neglected (inputs to the suit came 
</p>
<p>directly from test data) and crewmember side of the model was validated.  
</p>

</p>
<p>The Root-Mean-Square (RMS) average error across all eleven tests is tabulated in Table II-1. Cases with the 
</p>
<p>highest error are colored red and cases with the lowest error are colored green. There is no particular iteration of 
</p>
<p>changes that performs best in all the different temperatures and heat rates. For this analysis, the most important 
</p>
<p>metric to calculate is the latent heat rate and total latent load. Based on Table II-1, implementing change 1 (limiting 
</p>
<p>suit outlet dew point) and change 3 (adjusting LCVG temperature calculation) increase the error in latent heat rate 
</p>
<p>and total latent load. Change 2 (allowing for re-evaporation) has a minimal impact on the error. During the HITL 
</p>
<p>test series, the test subjects were able to ‘precool’ prior to periods of high metabolic rate, and for this reason, there 
</p>
<p>was minimal sweat runoff expected during this test. This explains why allowing for re-evaporation only affected the 
</p>
<p>results slightly. Going forward, the only change that will be maintained is Change 2. Change 4 (adjusting LCVG 
</p>
<p>heat transfer coefficient correlation) was not investigated since the coefficient was further adjusted based on more 
</p>
<p>recent test data (See Section II.C). Change 5 (changing sublimator conductance) was not adjusted since this re-
</p>
<p>correlation effort neglected the PLSS side of SINDA EMU.

<p>C. SINDA EMU Adjustments 
</p>
<p>In addition to the adjustments based on the re-correlation effort documented in Section II.B, additional 
</p>
<p>adjustments to SINDA EMU were made based on updated test data. These adjustments were validated individually 
</p>
<p>rather than through a system-wide validation check like was conducted during the re-correlation effort. 
</p>
<p>• Adjusted the sublimator model to use flight data as an input. This improves the accuracy of the water 
</p>
<p>temperature calculation in the sublimator by grounding the model to actual flight data. 
</p>
<p>• Adjusted the CO2 removal component in model to account for the transition in technology from Lithium 
</p>
<p>Hydroxide (LiOH) to Metal Oxide (Metox) using Metox qualification tests9. 
</p>
<p>• Adjusted the LCVG heat transfer correlations in the model based on data from the Exploration xEMU 
</p>
<p>PLSS 2.0 HITL test series. The PLSS for xEMU is different from the EMU PLSS. However, the LCVG 
</p>
<p>used during this test series is the same LCVG used during EVAs. 
</p>
<p>• Updated the relationship between thermal control valve (TCV) position and water flow split based on 
</p>
<p>average TCV flow split10. 
</p>
<p>• Improved the capabilities of SINDA EMU to model transient EVAs. 
</p>
<p>Implementing all of these changes, in addition to the re-correlation effort documented in the previous section, 
</p>
<p>results in a SINDA EMU model which is able to accurately model current EVAs and determine the latent load 
</p>
<p>generated by the crewmember. 
</p>

<p>III. Analysis Approach and Assumptions 
</p>
<p>The primary objective of this analysis was to use SINDA EMU to model historical EVAs and chamber tests to 
</p>
<p>compare the latent load from historical EVAs and tests to the latent load from US EVA 80. The following list 
</p>
<p>outlines the steps taken during this analysis: 
</p>
<p>1. Use SINDA EMU to run a parametric of EVAs to determine the impact of heat storage on latent load. 
</p>
<p>2. Generate a list of historical EVAs and tests that will be modeled. These 67 EVAs and tests were 
</p>
<p>selected based on the following criteria: 
</p>
<p>a. All EVAs conducted by the EMU PLSS that was used on US EVA 80. 
</p>
<p>b. All EVAs conducted by the EMUs that were on the International Space Station (ISS) during 
</p>
<p>US EVA 80. 
</p>
<p>c. All EVAs that had some form of water in the helmet at some point during the EVA. 
</p>
<p>d. All EVAs that had a CO2 sensor latch. CO2 sensor latching is an indicator of water 
</p>
<p>downstream of the sublimator that did not make its way to the helmet. 
</p>
<p>e. Numerous ground tests from Chamber B at Johnson Space Center. 
</p>
<p>3. Collect data that will be fed into SINDA EMU as inputs. 
</p>
<p>a. Flight data was used to determine the metabolic rate of the crewmember, temperature of the 
</p>
<p>water exiting the sublimator, and pressures on the inside and outside of the suit. 
</p>
<p>b. Thermal modeling was used to determine the sink temperature. Sink temperature represents the 
</p>
<p>thermal environment outside of the suit and is used to determine the heat leak through the suit 
</p>
<p>based on thermal analysis11. 
</p>
<p>c. Heat storage of the crewmember was maintained by dynamically adjusting the TCV position. 
</p>
<p>The desired heat storage was calculated based on the following methodology: 
</p>
<p>i. If console logs indicated TCV positions, the model was first run with the actual TCV 
</p>
<p>positions. The resultant heat storage from this preliminary run was used as the desired 
</p>
<p>heat storage. 
</p>
<p>ii. If no TCV position data was available, the desired heat storage was calculated based 
</p>
<p>on a reported crewmember’s heat storage bias. This was based on looking at historical 
</p>
<p>tests by the crewmember and their tendency to run the suit either hot, neutral, or cold. 
</p>
<p>These biases were not based on quantitative data, but rather insight from discussion 
</p>
<p>with crewmembers and operation/test team members. The quantitative heat storage 
</p>
<p>goal was assumed based on typical deviations from neutral heat storage, and the 
</p>
<p>uncertainty in this value was addressed through a parametric approach (step 4).  
</p>
<p>iii. If no crewmember heat bias was available, zero heat storage was assumed. 
</p>
<p>4. Model each EVA nine times to account for uncertainty in the size of the crewmember (+/-10 lbm [4.5 
</p>
<p>kg]) and heat storage (+/- 0.2 Btu/lbm [0.47 kJ/kg]). 
</p>
<p>5. Compare SINDA EMU outputs to identify trends in EVAs and tests. 
</p>
<p>D. Critical Assumptions of Analysis 
</p>
<p>Most of the assumptions in this analysis are based on significant uncertainty in the model inputs. The critical 
</p>
<p>assumptions made in this analysis are: 
</p>
<p>• TCV position was controlled by maintaining a desired heat storage. In reality, the TCV position will not 
</p>
<p>change as frequently as was modeled since it requires a manual adjustment. Unfortunately, for most 
</p>
<p>EVAs TCV positions were not reported so an assumed heat storage goal had to be used. Comparison 
</p>
<p>between running the model with TCV position vs desired heat storage for EVAs that had TCV position 
</p>
<p>resulted in comparable latent loads, despite differences in the control method for the transport loop. 
</p>
<p>• Sink temperature was based on thermal modeling. Thermal modeling generates 8 parametric start times 
</p>
<p>for the EVA (based on ISS position in orbit). The parametric case that best aligns with the actual start 
</p>
<p>time and task progression from the EVA was used. 
</p>
<p>• For Chamber B tests, the sink temperature was assumed to be -80 °F (-62.2 °C) for all cases. 
</p>
<p>• The ventilation loop of the EMU has a flowrate of 6 ft3/min (2.83 L/s) at the suit inlet. 
</p>
<p>• Transport loop (cooling loop) has a flowrate of 240 lbm/hr (109 kg/hr). 
</p>
<p>o For this analysis, the LCVG bypass was neglected. This assumption is reasonable provided the 
</p>
<p>model maintains a desired heat storage. This heat storage will be maintained even if the bypass 
</p>
<p>is not physically modeled.  </p>

<p>IV. Results 
</p>
<p>E. Parametric Investigation on the Impact of Heat Storage on Latent Heat  
</p>
<p>Prior to running the 67 EVAs and Chamber B tests, a parametric investigation was conducted to determine the 
</p>
<p>impact of crew heat storage on latent load. For this investigation, the EVAs conducted using the EMU PLSS used on 
</p>
<p>US EVA 80 were modeled multiple times with SINDA EMU maintaining different heat storages for each run. The 
</p>
<p>total latent heat for each EVA is plotted in Figure IV-1. The total latent heat is normalized to reflect the total if the 
</p>
<p>EVA were to last for 7 hours, which removes dependency of the results on EVA length. Below a neutral heat 
</p>
<p>storage, the total latent heat is relatively constant with respect to the average heat storage. At neutral and negative 
</p>
<p>heat storage, there is minimal sweat production so the latent heat production is primarily from respiration and 
</p>
<p>diffusion through the skin. These mechanisms do not depend significantly on heat storage which explains the low 
</p>
<p>slope below zero heat storage. At positive heat storage, the crew will begin to produce sweat to remove the excess 
</p>
<p>heat from their body. This will significantly impact the latent heat with warmer crewmembers producing more 
</p>
<p>sweat. This parametric investigation shows that heat storage is critical in determining the latent heat since it directly 
</p>
<p>impacts sweat production. The spread in data at higher heat storages is likely caused by additional factors such as 
</p>
<p>varying metabolic rate and thermal environments. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>F. Modeling of Historical EVAs and Chamber Tests 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>1. Model Inputs 
</p>
<p>The metabolic rates during the modeled EVAs and chamber tests were calculated and plotted in Figure IV-2. The 
</p>
<p>line color is based on if water droplets, fogging, or motor-boating (gurgling sound caused by water at the helmet 
</p>
<p>inlet which has only occurred during chamber tests) occurred in the helmet. US EVA 80 EV2 and US EVA 35 EV1 
</p>
<p>were highlighted since both of these EVAs presented significant amounts of water in the helmet. Comparing the data 
</p>
<p>shows that there are no easily discernable trends between the metabolic rate of the crew and the presence of some 
</p>
<p>form of water in the helmet. Both US EVA 80 and US EVA 35 had periods of relatively high metabolic rates, but 
</p>
<p>they were not outliers when compared to the other EVAs. Based on this data, there is no immediate evidence that 
</p>
<p>high metabolic rates increase the risk of water in the helmet. 
</p>
<p> The additional model inputs are thermal environment, sublimator water temperature, suit pressures, and heat 
</p>
<p>storage. These inputs alone do not provide any meaningful conclusions so they will not be discussed in detail in this 
</p>
<p>paper.

<p>3. Total Latent Heat (Total Latent Load) 
</p>
<p>Aside from general trends, it is still difficult to identify many discernable trends from looking at graphs of the 
</p>
<p>latent heat rate. To further the analysis, the total latent load is plotted in Figure IV-4. Total latent load is calculated 
</p>
<p>by integrating the latent heat rate over the entire EVA time. The cases are sorted along the x-axis by total latent load. 
</p>
<p>Figure IV-4 contains significant data which will be described in the following bullets:  
</p>
<p>• The color of the data indicates the heat storage that was assumed for each EVA/test. Data that has an 
</p>
<p>asterisk in the EVA name (along the x-axis) indicates an EVA where the assumed heat storage goal was 
</p>
<p>based on running SINDA EMU with the actual TCV settings. Cases without an asterisk in the name are 
</p>
<p>cases where the heat storage goal was based on the crew’s bias. Cases that are colored grey did not have any 
</p>
<p>crew bias and were assumed to have zero heat storage.  
</p>
<p>• The symbol type indicates whether there was any type of water (droplets, fogging, motor-boating) reported 
</p>
<p>in the helmet during the EVA/test. Any data point that is not a circle represents an EVA/test with some form 
</p>
<p>of reported water. 
</p>
<p>• The lines indicate the bounds of the parametric study for each case. To address uncertainty, each case was 
</p>
<p>run with variability in crew mass and heat storage. The bounds of this parametric study are presented as 
</p>
<p>lines in the graph. These lines only account for uncertainty from the parametric study and do not address 
</p>
<p>error within the model. 
</p>
<p>• The text ‘CO2 Latch’ on the top of some of the parametric bars indicates cases where the CO2 sensor latched 
</p>
<p>during the EVA (likely due to water downstream of the sublimator). 
</p>
<p>• The type of lines indicate whether a case is an EVA (solid lines) or chamber test (dashed lines). 
</p>

<p>Numerous trends become apparent when looking at the total latent load across all modeled cases. Higher positive 
</p>
<p>heat storages are typically associated with higher latent loads. This is why most of the data on the far right of Figure 
</p>
<p>IV-4 are red and pink. This is because high heat storage results in a higher sweat rate by the crewmember which 
</p>
<p>significantly increases the latent load. Additionally, at higher heat storages, the bounds of the parametric study 
</p>
<p>become greater (interpreted as larger bars on data). This can be explained by the behavior seen in Figure IV-1. At 
</p>
<p>high positive heat storage, the latent heat rate depends significantly on the heat storage, so small changes in heat 
</p>
<p>storage will result in significant changes in latent load. By contrast, at negative heat storages the latent load is 
</p>
<p>relatively independent of the heat storage so changes in heat storage do not affect the latent load. Additionally, this 
</p>
<p>chart shows that most of the cases at the highest latent load have some form of either water in the helmet or a CO2 
</p>
<p>sensor latching. The highest latent load without any water is US EVA 79 EV2 which had the 8th highest latent load 
</p>
<p>of all cases investigated. Despite the prevalence of water at high latent loads, there is also a significant number of 
</p>
<p>EVAs with lower latent loads that still presented some form of water in the helmet. These cases can be explained by 
</p>
<p>another phenomena that is not present in this chart: sublimator performance. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>4. Sublimator Performance 
</p>
<p>The presence of significant sublimator carryover will depend on two primary factors: the latent load sent to the 
</p>
<p>sublimator and the ability of the sublimator to remove liquid water from the ventilation loop. Figure IV-4 showed 
</p>
<p>that the high latent loads increased the likelihood of sublimator carryover but could not explain why some low latent 
</p>
<p>load cases also presented water in the helmet. Figure IV-5 colors the data by the sublimator used on each EVA to 
</p>
<p>highlight the performance of each sublimator. This chart shows that nearly all of the EVAs and tests that had some 
</p>
<p>form of water at low latent loads were conducted with Sublimator A (blue). This indicates that Sublimator A had 
</p>
<p>some performance issues which prevented it from properly removing liquid water, even at lower latent loads which 
</p>
<p>do not present issues for other sublimators. Sublimator A is no longer in operation. By contrast, Sublimator E (light 
</p>
<p>green) completed many EVAs at comparatively low and average latent loads (1000 – 1500 Btu) without presenting 
</p>
<p>water and only experienced water when challenged with a comparatively high latent load. Sublimators B,C,D, and E 
</p>
<p>correspond with sublimators that were on ISS during US EVA 80. These data show that all four of these sublimators 
</p>
<p>are able to deal with low and average latent loads and for this reason the water events from US EVA 80 and US 
</p>
<p>EVA 35 were likely due to comparatively high latent loads, not sublimator performance issues.  
</p>

<p>5. Performance of Additional Components 
</p>
<p>In addition to checking if the sublimator performance could impact the ability of the EMU to keep water out of 
</p>
<p>the helmet, the data was used to determine if there were other components that were responsible for most of the 
</p>
<p>water events in EMU history. The components that could impact the ability to remove condensate are the 
</p>
<p>sublimator, fan/pump/separator (F/P/S), pitot-actuated valve, CO2 sensor, and gas trap. Figure IV-7 represents the 
</p>
<p>combination of components used for each EVA and chamber test. Each grey rectangle represents a different serial 
</p>
<p>number for a particular component. Each line represents a single EVA/test and the combination of components that 
</p>
<p>were used for this particular EVA/test. Thicker lines indicate more EVAs conducted with a particular combination 
</p>
<p>of components. The reason there are thick lines passing through multiple components is because the components are 
</p>
<p>not frequently changed out between EVAs so many EVAs use the same combination of components. Red lines 
</p>
<p>indicate EVAs/tests where there was either water droplets or motorboating reported. For this data all EVAs/tests that 
</p>
<p>used Sublimator A were neglected since it has already been established that Sublimator A was an underperformer 
</p>
<p>which frequently caused water in the helmet. </p>

<p>Tracking the red lines in Figure IV-7 shows that there is not a single component that is responsible for most of 
</p>
<p>the water events. For instance, if all of the red lines had passed through a particular gas trap or F/P/S, then that 
</p>
<p>component would be identified as a potential cause. The biggest similarity between all of the red lines is that they all 
</p>
<p>have a relatively warm heat storage and high latent load. This confirms that the most likely cause of the US EVA 80 
</p>
<p>EV2 water in the helmet failure was caused by an elevated latent load, not a particular component failure. This 
</p>
<p>conclusion agrees with the results of the Test, Teardown, &amp; Evaluation (TT&amp;E) which found that there were no 
</p>
<p>individual component failures that could be responsible for the water levels seen in the helmet during US EVA 80. 
</p>
<p>Instead, the failure was likely caused by the integrated performance of the entire EMU/crewmember system. 
</p>
<p>V. Discussions and Future Work 
</p>
<p>Using SINDA EMU to model historical EVAs provided significant insight into the cause of water in the helmet 
</p>
<p>during US EVA 80. These insights can be summarized into three main takeaways: 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>1. High latent load generated by the crewmember is generally associated with most EVAs/tests that 
</p>
<p>present some form of water downstream of the sublimator (droplets, motor-boating, or a latched CO2 
</p>
<p>sensor). Two of the highest profile water in the helmet events (US EVA 80 and US EVA 35) had the 2nd 
</p>
<p>and 5th highest latent loads of 67 EVAs and chamber tests. Neglecting a known sublimator outlier, all of 
</p>
<p>the EVAs/tests that had some form of water downstream of the sublimator were in the upper half of 
</p>
<p>modeled latent loads. High latent loads result in an increased likelihood of water in the helmet because 
</p>
<p>they are providing a larger challenge to the sublimator. Higher latent loads result in more water 
</p>
<p>condensing out in the sublimator, which increases the difficulty of removing condensation from the 
</p>
<p>sublimator. Due to the high latent load experienced on US EVA 80, it is likely that integrated 
</p>
<p>performance induced sublimator carryover as a result of a high latent heat load was the primary cause of 
</p>
<p>the water in the helmet. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>2. The latent heat rate can be reduced by the crewmember through adjustment of the TCV. For all 
</p>
<p>potential metabolic loads, the transport loop of the PLSS contains enough heat removal capabilities to 
</p>
<p>maintain a neutral crew member. However, this requires the manual adjustment of the TCV by the 
</p>
<p>crewmember. Through a parametric study, it was shown that the most effective way to reduce the latent 
</p>
<p>load is to decrease the heat storage of the crewmember. In the past, crew training has not emphasized 
</p>
<p>the importance of thermal neutrality and for this reason, crew may have been tolerant of higher heat 
</p>
<p>storages and not acted to adjust their TCV when getting too warm. Adjusting the TCV is not always a 
</p>
<p>simple task, but improving awareness of the importance of thermal neutrality will likely reduce the time 
</p>
<p>spent at high latent loads on future EVAs. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>12 
</p>
<p>International Conference on Environmental Systems 
 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>3. Underperforming sublimators can cause sublimator carryover even at low latent heat loads. EVAs/tests 
</p>
<p>conducted with Sublimator A often presented water downstream of sublimator even at low latent heat. 
</p>
<p>This analysis does not give sufficient data to determine why Sublimator A was an underperformer, 
</p>
<p>however it does highlight its underperformance and the impact of it. There were no other sublimators or 
</p>
<p>components that were identified through this analysis as potential underperformers. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The impact of this analysis is future work which can be used to further reduce the risk of integrated performance 
</p>
<p>induced sublimator carryover. As of the time of this paper, many of the future work recommendations are already in 
</p>
<p>progress: 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>1. Model additional historical EVAs to ensure that the conclusions drawn from this analysis also apply to 
</p>
<p>a larger subset of EVAs. The 67 EVAs modeled in this analysis only represent a small subset of the 
</p>
<p>total EVAs completed, so expanding the dataset will increase confidence in the conclusions from this 
</p>
<p>analysis. 
</p>
<p>2. Model future EVAs using SINDA EMU to expand understanding of the performance of the EMU. If 
</p>
<p>future EVAs are presenting water in the helmet at lower latent loads it could be an indicator of an 
</p>
<p>underperforming sublimator or another component. 
</p>
<p>3. Development of a warning system that could be used to alert the crewmember when they are at a high 
</p>
<p>latent load and subsequently at a high risk of sublimator carryover. This system would be created by 
</p>
<p>running SINDA EMU (or some other model) live during an EVA to get real-time predictions of the 
</p>
<p>latent load during an EVA. 
</p>
<p>4. Adjust training of crewmembers to emphasize the importance of thermal neutrality. Training the crew 
</p>
<p>to adjust TCV prior to periods of high metabolic load or warm thermal environments will reduce the 
</p>
<p>periods of time spent at high latent heat rates.  
</p>
<p>5. Develop new sublimator screening methods to prevent another underperforming sublimator (e.g. 
</p>
<p>Sublimator A) from ever reaching the ISS. 
</p>
<p>6. Conduct additional chamber tests of EMU to further improve the correlation of SINDA EMU. Limited 
</p>
<p>latent load data is available for chamber tests on the EMU. Additional data would greatly improve the 
</p>
<p>accuracy of SINDA EMU. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Each of these steps would buy down risk of another water in the helmet failure by preventing underperforming 
</p>
<p>sublimators from flying, educating the crew on the importance of remaining at a low latent load, and providing a 
</p>
<p>backup warning system to alert flight directors when the risk for sublimator carryover is higher. This risk of liquid 
</p>
<p>water in the ventilation loop will also be reduced in the development of next-generation spacesuits. The xEMU will 
</p>
<p>not use a condensing heat exchanger to remove water vapor from the ventilation loop which will buy down the risk 
</p>
<p>of liquid water entering the helmet12. 
</p>
<p>VI. Conclusion 
</p>
<p>To investigate the cause of water in the helmet during US EVA 80, a system-level model of the EMU and 
</p>
<p>crewmember (SINDA EMU) was used to model 67 historical EVAs and chamber tests. The 67 cases were selected 
</p>
<p>to investigate the history of the EMU from US EVA 80, EVAs conducted with EMUs on ISS at the time of US EVA 
</p>
<p>80, EVAs that presented some form of water in the helmet, and numerous chamber tests. The model was used to 
</p>
<p>determine the latent load which represents the amount of water vapor sent to the sublimator and condensed out of 
</p>
<p>the ventilation loop.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>This analysis found that EVAs and chamber tests that had an elevated latent load were more likely to have some 
</p>
<p>form of water downstream of the sublimator (water droplets, motor-boating, or a latched CO2 sensor). US EVA 80 
</p>
<p>had the second highest latent load of all modeled EVAs and chamber tests which provides significant evidence that 
</p>
<p>sublimator carryover caused by a high latent load was responsible for the water in the helmet from US EVA 80. 
</p>
<p>Latent load can be reduced by stressing the importance of thermal neutrality to the crewmember which will result in 
</p>
<p>better thermal management with the TCV. This analysis also identified a sublimator which consistently produced 
</p>
<p>water in the helmet, even at low latent loads. This data indicates that sublimator carryover can occur even at low 
</p>
<p>latent load if an underperforming sublimator (e.g. Sublimator A) is used. Through modeling of all sublimators on </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>13 
</p>
<p>International Conference on Environmental Systems 
 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>ISS after US EVA 80 it was determined that none of these sublimators presented water at low latent loads. This 
</p>
<p>indicates that none of the sublimators on ISS at the time of US EVA 80 are underperforming. This analysis also 
</p>
<p>considered the possibility that individual components other than the sublimator may be responsible for water in the 
</p>
<p>helmet. Through a component based analysis of the data, it was found that there was not a single component 
</p>
<p>responsible for numerous water events (other than Sublimator A). For this reason, it can be concluded that the water 
</p>
<p>in the helmet on US EVA 80 was not due to a particular component failure, but rather the integrated performance of 
</p>
<p>the EMU and crewmember producing a high latent load, leading to sublimator carryover.
