Comparative Analysis for EMU Fleet Latent Loading 



Characterization in Support of US EVA 80 Failure 



Noah Andersen1 



HX5, LLC, JSC Engineering Technology and Science (JETS2), Houston, TX, 77058 



During United States Extravehicular Activity 80 (US EVA 80), water was observed in the 



helmet of an Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU) during cabin repressurization. One of the 



primary mechanisms that can cause water in the helmet of an EMU is integrated 



performance induced sublimator carryover. The sublimator is a heat exchanger that 



removes heat and humidity from the ventilation loop. Water vapor is condensed from the 



gas and removed by slurper holes in the sublimator. Sublimator carryover is caused by the 



inability of the EMU sublimator to remove all of the condensed water vapor, resulting in 



liquid water entering the helmet. To determine if sublimator carryover was a likely cause of 



the US EVA 80 failure, a comparative analysis of numerous historical EVAs was conducted 



to calculate the total latent load (total water vapor generated by the crewmember) for 



numerous historical EVAs and ground tests using the Systems Improved Numerical 



Differencing Analyzer EMU (SINDA EMU) model. 



The analysis showed that US EVA 80 was associated with a comparatively high latent 



load when compared to other EVAs that did not present water in the helmet. Further, this 



analysis showed that other historical EVAs which had visible water in the helmet were also 



associated with higher latent loads. This analysis provides evidence that the likely cause of 



the US EVA 80 water in the helmet event was not the failure of an individual component, but 



rather sublimator carryover caused by excessive production of water vapor by the 



crewmember. The evidence from this analysis agrees with results from the Test, Teardown, 



and Evaluation (TT&E) of the EMU which did not show any failure of individual 



components of the EMU that would lead to water in the helmet.

I. Introduction 



 



URING the repressurization phase of the United States Extravehicular Activity 80 (US EVA 80) on 23 March 



2022, liquid water was observed in the helmet of an Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU). Water in the helmet 



poses a significant risk to the crewmember as it may restrict their ability to see and breathe. The risk associated with 



liquid water in the helmet of an EMU resulted in an investigation into the failure experienced during US EVA 80. 



 



 
Figure I-1: EMU Schematic1 



 Figure I-1 shows a schematic of the EMU Portable Life Support System (PLSS)1. In this analysis, two loops will 



be discussed: the ventilation loop (in yellow) which is responsible for providing oxygen gas (O2) and removing 



humidity and carbon dioxide (CO2), and the transport loop (in red) which is responsible for cooling the 



crewmember. Figure I-2 shows a simplified diagram of the ventilation loop. During an EVA, the crewmember adds 



water vapor to the ventilation loop through respiration, evaporation of sweat, and diffusion through the skin. The 



sum of these three sources of water vapor is called the latent heat (also referred to as the latent load). This is 



calculated by multiplying the heat of vaporization of water by the mass flow rate of the water vapor being added to 



the ventilation loop. Once this water is evaporated into the ventilation loop gas, it travels to the sublimator which 



cools off the gas and allows liquid water to condense. The sublimator contains numerous slurper holes which are 



designed to remove the condensed, liquid water from the sublimator. Sublimator carryover occurs when the slurper 



holes are unable to remove all of the liquid water, allowing some of the water to exit the sublimator and make its 



way to the helmet. The amount of sublimator carryover depends on the latent load sent to the sublimator as well as 



the performance of the sublimator. For this analysis, the latent load experienced during US EVA 80 will be 



compared to numerous historical EVAs and chamber tests to identify trends that may exist between US EVA 80 and 



other EVAs/tests that presented some form of water in the helmet.

Figure I-2: Simplified Diagram of Interactions Between Crew and Ventilation Loop 



In addition to the ventilation loop, the EMU contains a transport loop which is responsible for removing excess 



heat from the crewmember using liquid water. The amount of cooling received by the crewmember depends on the 



position of the Thermal Control Valve (TCV). The TCV has 13 settings (H,0,1,2,…9,10,C) with higher settings 



providing more cooling to the crewmember. At TCV settings below 3, the flowrate through the Liquid Cooling and 



Ventilation Garment (LCVG) drops below nominal flowrate to further reduce cooling. This is called LCVG bypass 



mode. At TCV settings greater than 3, the LCVG water flowrate is constant. Cooling levels are based on the portion 



of water that is sent to the sublimator. Higher TCV settings correspond with a larger split of the flowrate being sent 



to the sublimator. The position of the TCV is adjusted manually throughout an EVA. The TCV position directly 



impacts the cooling received by the crewmember which impacts their heat storage (measurement of the deviation in 



body temperature from neutral). The heat storage of the crewmember will impact the sweat production rate and 



ultimately affect the latent load. For this reason, the TCV setting is critical towards calculating the latent load. 



II. Model Description 



A. SINDA EMU Background 



For this analysis, the latent load during an EVA was 



calculated using the Systems Improved Numerical 



Differencing Analyzer EMU (SINDA EMU) Model. This is 



a system-level model of the crewmember and EMU PLSS 



(See Figure II-1). The model was initially developed in the 



1970s and was correlated to EMU certification runs in 



Chamber B at Johnson Space Center (JSC)2,3. After the 



initial development of SINDA EMU, the model was 



improved through implementation of the 41-node metabolic 



man model (METMAN)4. METMAN models the blood, 



muscle, fat, and skin of the crewmember and calculates all 



three sources of latent load (sweat, respiration, diffusion). 



During the 1990s, SINDA EMU was re-correlated to the 



EMU performance verification tests5. Many of the 



adjustments made during this correlation effort were readdressed during this analysis based on new test data (see 



Section II.B). Finally, SINDA EMU was upgraded to FORTRAN 90 in 20166. 



B. SINDA EMU Re-correlation 



Prior to this analysis, the most recent correlation effort on SINDA EMU was in the 1990s. The 1990s correlation 



effort resulted in five significant changes to the SINDA EMU model5. 



1. Limit suit outlet dew point to never exceed LCVG water outlet temperature. 



2. Sweat runoff (generated sweat that is not immediately evaporated into gas) is re-evaporated into the 



vent loop when possible. 



3. Calculation of LCVG undergarment temperature was adjusted to depend on LCVG water temperature. 



4. LCVG heat transfer coefficient correlation was adjusted. 



5. Conductance between the sublimator and valve module was adjusted.

C. SINDA EMU Adjustments 



In addition to the adjustments based on the re-correlation effort documented in Section II.B, additional 



adjustments to SINDA EMU were made based on updated test data. These adjustments were validated individually 



rather than through a system-wide validation check like was conducted during the re-correlation effort. 



• Adjusted the sublimator model to use flight data as an input. This improves the accuracy of the water 



temperature calculation in the sublimator by grounding the model to actual flight data. 



• Adjusted the CO2 removal component in model to account for the transition in technology from Lithium 



Hydroxide (LiOH) to Metal Oxide (Metox) using Metox qualification tests9. 



• Adjusted the LCVG heat transfer correlations in the model based on data from the Exploration xEMU 



PLSS 2.0 HITL test series. The PLSS for xEMU is different from the EMU PLSS. However, the LCVG 



used during this test series is the same LCVG used during EVAs. 



• Updated the relationship between thermal control valve (TCV) position and water flow split based on 



average TCV flow split10. 



• Improved the capabilities of SINDA EMU to model transient EVAs. 



Implementing all of these changes, in addition to the re-correlation effort documented in the previous section, 



results in a SINDA EMU model which is able to accurately model current EVAs and determine the latent load 



generated by the crewmember.

III. Analysis Approach and Assumptions 



The primary objective of this analysis was to use SINDA EMU to model historical EVAs and chamber tests to 



compare the latent load from historical EVAs and tests to the latent load from US EVA 80. The following list 



outlines the steps taken during this analysis: 



1. Use SINDA EMU to run a parametric of EVAs to determine the impact of heat storage on latent load. 



2. Generate a list of historical EVAs and tests that will be modeled. These 67 EVAs and tests were 



selected based on the following criteria: 



a. All EVAs conducted by the EMU PLSS that was used on US EVA 80. 



b. All EVAs conducted by the EMUs that were on the International Space Station (ISS) during 



US EVA 80. 



c. All EVAs that had some form of water in the helmet at some point during the EVA. 



d. All EVAs that had a CO2 sensor latch. CO2 sensor latching is an indicator of water 



downstream of the sublimator that did not make its way to the helmet. 



e. Numerous ground tests from Chamber B at Johnson Space Center. 



3. Collect data that will be fed into SINDA EMU as inputs. 



a. Flight data was used to determine the metabolic rate of the crewmember, temperature of the 



water exiting the sublimator, and pressures on the inside and outside of the suit. 



b. Thermal modeling was used to determine the sink temperature. Sink temperature represents the 



thermal environment outside of the suit and is used to determine the heat leak through the suit 



based on thermal analysis11. 



c. Heat storage of the crewmember was maintained by dynamically adjusting the TCV position. 



The desired heat storage was calculated based on the following methodology: 



i. If console logs indicated TCV positions, the model was first run with the actual TCV 



positions. The resultant heat storage from this preliminary run was used as the desired 



heat storage. 



ii. If no TCV position data was available, the desired heat storage was calculated based 



on a reported crewmember’s heat storage bias. This was based on looking at historical 



tests by the crewmember and their tendency to run the suit either hot, neutral, or cold. 



These biases were not based on quantitative data, but rather insight from discussion 



with crewmembers and operation/test team members. The quantitative heat storage 



goal was assumed based on typical deviations from neutral heat storage, and the 



uncertainty in this value was addressed through a parametric approach (step 4). 



iii. If no crewmember heat bias was available, zero heat storage was assumed. 



4. Model each EVA nine times to account for uncertainty in the size of the crewmember (+/-10 lbm [4.5 



kg]) and heat storage (+/- 0.2 Btu/lbm [0.47 kJ/kg]). 



5. Compare SINDA EMU outputs to identify trends in EVAs and tests. 



D. Critical Assumptions of Analysis 



Most of the assumptions in this analysis are based on significant uncertainty in the model inputs. The critical 



assumptions made in this analysis are: 



• TCV position was controlled by maintaining a desired heat storage. In reality, the TCV position will not 



change as frequently as was modeled since it requires a manual adjustment. Unfortunately, for most 



EVAs TCV positions were not reported so an assumed heat storage goal had to be used. Comparison 



between running the model with TCV position vs desired heat storage for EVAs that had TCV position 



resulted in comparable latent loads, despite differences in the control method for the transport loop. 



• Sink temperature was based on thermal modeling. Thermal modeling generates 8 parametric start times 



for the EVA (based on ISS position in orbit). The parametric case that best aligns with the actual start 



time and task progression from the EVA was used. 



• For Chamber B tests, the sink temperature was assumed to be -80 °F (-62.2 °C) for all cases. 



• The ventilation loop of the EMU has a flowrate of 6 ft3/min (2.83 L/s) at the suit inlet. 



• Transport loop (cooling loop) has a flowrate of 240 lbm/hr (109 kg/hr). 



o For this analysis, the LCVG bypass was neglected. This assumption is reasonable provided the 



model maintains a desired heat storage. This heat storage will be maintained even if the bypass 



is not physically modeled.

IV. Results 



E. Parametric Investigation on the Impact of Heat Storage on Latent Heat 



Prior to running the 67 EVAs and Chamber B tests, a parametric investigation was conducted to determine the 



impact of crew heat storage on latent load. For this investigation, the EVAs conducted using the EMU PLSS used on 



US EVA 80 were modeled multiple times with SINDA EMU maintaining different heat storages for each run. The 



total latent heat for each EVA is plotted in Figure IV-1. The total latent heat is normalized to reflect the total if the 



EVA were to last for 7 hours, which removes dependency of the results on EVA length. Below a neutral heat 



storage, the total latent heat is relatively constant with respect to the average heat storage. At neutral and negative 



heat storage, there is minimal sweat production so the latent heat production is primarily from respiration and 



diffusion through the skin. These mechanisms do not depend significantly on heat storage which explains the low 



slope below zero heat storage. At positive heat storage, the crew will begin to produce sweat to remove the excess 



heat from their body. This will significantly impact the latent heat with warmer crewmembers producing more 



sweat. This parametric investigation shows that heat storage is critical in determining the latent heat since it directly 



impacts sweat production. The spread in data at higher heat storages is likely caused by additional factors such as 



varying metabolic rate and thermal environments.

Numerous trends become apparent when looking at the total latent load across all modeled cases. Higher positive 



heat storages are typically associated with higher latent loads. This is why most of the data on the far right of Figure 



IV-4 are red and pink. This is because high heat storage results in a higher sweat rate by the crewmember which 



significantly increases the latent load. Additionally, at higher heat storages, the bounds of the parametric study 



become greater (interpreted as larger bars on data). This can be explained by the behavior seen in Figure IV-1. At 



high positive heat storage, the latent heat rate depends significantly on the heat storage, so small changes in heat 



storage will result in significant changes in latent load. By contrast, at negative heat storages the latent load is 



relatively independent of the heat storage so changes in heat storage do not affect the latent load. Additionally, this 



chart shows that most of the cases at the highest latent load have some form of either water in the helmet or a CO2 



sensor latching. The highest latent load without any water is US EVA 79 EV2 which had the 8th highest latent load 



of all cases investigated. Despite the prevalence of water at high latent loads, there is also a significant number of 



EVAs with lower latent loads that still presented some form of water in the helmet. These cases can be explained by 



another phenomena that is not present in this chart: sublimator performance. 



 



4. Sublimator Performance 



The presence of significant sublimator carryover will depend on two primary factors: the latent load sent to the 



sublimator and the ability of the sublimator to remove liquid water from the ventilation loop. Figure IV-4 showed 



that the high latent loads increased the likelihood of sublimator carryover but could not explain why some low latent 



load cases also presented water in the helmet. Figure IV-5 colors the data by the sublimator used on each EVA to 



highlight the performance of each sublimator. This chart shows that nearly all of the EVAs and tests that had some 



form of water at low latent loads were conducted with Sublimator A (blue). This indicates that Sublimator A had 



some performance issues which prevented it from properly removing liquid water, even at lower latent loads which 



do not present issues for other sublimators. Sublimator A is no longer in operation. By contrast, Sublimator E (light 



green) completed many EVAs at comparatively low and average latent loads (1000 – 1500 Btu) without presenting 



water and only experienced water when challenged with a comparatively high latent load. Sublimators B,C,D, and E 



correspond with sublimators that were on ISS during US EVA 80. These data show that all four of these sublimators 



are able to deal with low and average latent loads and for this reason the water events from US EVA 80 and US 



EVA 35 were likely due to comparatively high latent loads, not sublimator performance issues.

5. Performance of Additional Components 



In addition to checking if the sublimator performance could impact the ability of the EMU to keep water out of 



the helmet, the data was used to determine if there were other components that were responsible for most of the 



water events in EMU history. The components that could impact the ability to remove condensate are the 



sublimator, fan/pump/separator (F/P/S), pitot-actuated valve, CO2 sensor, and gas trap. Figure IV-7 represents the 



combination of components used for each EVA and chamber test. Each grey rectangle represents a different serial

were used for this particular EVA/test. Thicker lines indicate more EVAs conducted with a particular combination 



of components. The reason there are thick lines passing through multiple components is because the components are 



not frequently changed out between EVAs so many EVAs use the same combination of components. Red lines 



indicate EVAs/tests where there was either water droplets or motorboating reported. For this data all EVAs/tests that 



used Sublimator A were neglected since it has already been established that Sublimator A was an underperformer 



which frequently caused water in the helmet.

Tracking the red lines in Figure IV-7 shows that there is not a single component that is responsible for most of 



the water events. For instance, if all of the red lines had passed through a particular gas trap or F/P/S, then that 



component would be identified as a potential cause. The biggest similarity between all of the red lines is that they all 



have a relatively warm heat storage and high latent load. This confirms that the most likely cause of the US EVA 80 



EV2 water in the helmet failure was caused by an elevated latent load, not a particular component failure. This 



conclusion agrees with the results of the Test, Teardown, & Evaluation (TT&E) which found that there were no 



individual component failures that could be responsible for the water levels seen in the helmet during US EVA 80. 



Instead, the failure was likely caused by the integrated performance of the entire EMU/crewmember system. 



V. Discussions and Future Work 



Using SINDA EMU to model historical EVAs provided significant insight into the cause of water in the helmet 



during US EVA 80. These insights can be summarized into three main takeaways: 



 



1. High latent load generated by the crewmember is generally associated with most EVAs/tests that 



present some form of water downstream of the sublimator (droplets, motor-boating, or a latched CO2 



sensor). Two of the highest profile water in the helmet events (US EVA 80 and US EVA 35) had the 2nd 



and 5th highest latent loads of 67 EVAs and chamber tests. Neglecting a known sublimator outlier, all of 



the EVAs/tests that had some form of water downstream of the sublimator were in the upper half of 



modeled latent loads. High latent loads result in an increased likelihood of water in the helmet because 



they are providing a larger challenge to the sublimator. Higher latent loads result in more water 



condensing out in the sublimator, which increases the difficulty of removing condensation from the 



sublimator. Due to the high latent load experienced on US EVA 80, it is likely that integrated 



performance induced sublimator carryover as a result of a high latent heat load was the primary cause of 



the water in the helmet. 



 



2. The latent heat rate can be reduced by the crewmember through adjustment of the TCV. For all 



potential metabolic loads, the transport loop of the PLSS contains enough heat removal capabilities to 



maintain a neutral crew member. However, this requires the manual adjustment of the TCV by the 



crewmember. Through a parametric study, it was shown that the most effective way to reduce the latent 



load is to decrease the heat storage of the crewmember. In the past, crew training has not emphasized 



the importance of thermal neutrality and for this reason, crew may have been tolerant of higher heat 



storages and not acted to adjust their TCV when getting too warm. Adjusting the TCV is not always a 



simple task, but improving awareness of the importance of thermal neutrality will likely reduce the time 



spent at high latent loads on future EVAs.

3. Underperforming sublimators can cause sublimator carryover even at low latent heat loads. EVAs/tests 



conducted with Sublimator A often presented water downstream of sublimator even at low latent heat. 



This analysis does not give sufficient data to determine why Sublimator A was an underperformer, 



however it does highlight its underperformance and the impact of it. There were no other sublimators or 



components that were identified through this analysis as potential underperformers. 



 



The impact of this analysis is future work which can be used to further reduce the risk of integrated performance 



induced sublimator carryover. As of the time of this paper, many of the future work recommendations are already in 



progress: 



 



1. Model additional historical EVAs to ensure that the conclusions drawn from this analysis also apply to 



a larger subset of EVAs. The 67 EVAs modeled in this analysis only represent a small subset of the 



total EVAs completed, so expanding the dataset will increase confidence in the conclusions from this 



analysis. 



2. Model future EVAs using SINDA EMU to expand understanding of the performance of the EMU. If 



future EVAs are presenting water in the helmet at lower latent loads it could be an indicator of an 



underperforming sublimator or another component. 



3. Development of a warning system that could be used to alert the crewmember when they are at a high 



latent load and subsequently at a high risk of sublimator carryover. This system would be created by 



running SINDA EMU (or some other model) live during an EVA to get real-time predictions of the 



latent load during an EVA. 



4. Adjust training of crewmembers to emphasize the importance of thermal neutrality. Training the crew 



to adjust TCV prior to periods of high metabolic load or warm thermal environments will reduce the 



periods of time spent at high latent heat rates. 



5. Develop new sublimator screening methods to prevent another underperforming sublimator (e.g. 



Sublimator A) from ever reaching the ISS. 



6. Conduct additional chamber tests of EMU to further improve the correlation of SINDA EMU. Limited 



latent load data is available for chamber tests on the EMU. Additional data would greatly improve the 



accuracy of SINDA EMU. 



 



Each of these steps would buy down risk of another water in the helmet failure by preventing underperforming 



sublimators from flying, educating the crew on the importance of remaining at a low latent load, and providing a 



backup warning system to alert flight directors when the risk for sublimator carryover is higher. This risk of liquid 



water in the ventilation loop will also be reduced in the development of next-generation spacesuits. The xEMU will 



not use a condensing heat exchanger to remove water vapor from the ventilation loop which will buy down the risk 



of liquid water entering the helmet12. 



VI. Conclusion 



To investigate the cause of water in the helmet during US EVA 80, a system-level model of the EMU and 



crewmember (SINDA EMU) was used to model 67 historical EVAs and chamber tests. The 67 cases were selected 



to investigate the history of the EMU from US EVA 80, EVAs conducted with EMUs on ISS at the time of US EVA 



80, EVAs that presented some form of water in the helmet, and numerous chamber tests. The model was used to 



determine the latent load which represents the amount of water vapor sent to the sublimator and condensed out of 



the ventilation loop. 



 



This analysis found that EVAs and chamber tests that had an elevated latent load were more likely to have some 



form of water downstream of the sublimator (water droplets, motor-boating, or a latched CO2 sensor). US EVA 80 



had the second highest latent load of all modeled EVAs and chamber tests which provides significant evidence that 



sublimator carryover caused by a high latent load was responsible for the water in the helmet from US EVA 80. 



Latent load can be reduced by stressing the importance of thermal neutrality to the crewmember which will result in 



better thermal management with the TCV. This analysis also identified a sublimator which consistently produced 



water in the helmet, even at low latent loads. This data indicates that sublimator carryover can occur even at low 



latent load if an underperforming sublimator (e.g. Sublimator A) is used. Through modeling of all sublimators on

ISS after US EVA 80 it was determined that none of these sublimators presented water at low latent loads. This 



indicates that none of the sublimators on ISS at the time of US EVA 80 are underperforming. This analysis also 



considered the possibility that individual components other than the sublimator may be responsible for water in the 



helmet. Through a component based analysis of the data, it was found that there was not a single component 



responsible for numerous water events (other than Sublimator A). For this reason, it can be concluded that the water 



in the helmet on US EVA 80 was not due to a particular component failure, but rather the integrated performance of 



the EMU and crewmember producing a high latent load, leading to sublimator carryover.
