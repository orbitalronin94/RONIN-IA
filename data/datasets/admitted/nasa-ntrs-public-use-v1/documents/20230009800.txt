<p>• Consideration of liquid hydrogen as a propulsion fuel dates to at least 1945
• Originally rejected as a fuel for rocket propulsion, it has been utilized for many 
</p>
<p>rockets since
• The NACA (NASA’s predecessor) original interest in hydrogen as an aircraft fuel 
</p>
<p>was motivated by its combustion performance at high altitude
</p>
<p>• Later interest in LH2 and/or LNG was motivated by the desire for greater domestic 
fuel security, concerns about future “peak oil,” or higher aircraft performance (e.g., 
long endurance)
</p>
<p>• The sustainability motivation is new, but challenges and opportunities are 
similar to the past
</p>
<p>NASA History with Hydrogen Aircraft
</p>
<p>Liquid-hydrogen fuel system for one engine of a B-57 flew successfully in 
1957. Image from NASA SP-4404, “Liquid Hydrogen as a Propulsion Fuel, 
1945-1959”
</p>
<p>From Silverstein and Hall, "Liquid Hydrogen as a Jet Fuel for High-Altitude 
Aircraft," 1955.

hydrogen related research activities.

<p>NASA SBIR Ignite Aircraft
</p>
<p>Problem Statement:  The purpose of this topic is to stimulate U.S. 
entrepreneurship in the emerging markets of hybrid electric 
aircraft.  Components are sought for integration, ground, and potential flight 
testing for a 1500 lbs. class hybrid electric drone.  Proposals should support 
market introduction to the existing large UAV or the emerging electric and hybrid 
electric aircraft markets. The topic seeks solutions applicable for Unmanned 
Aerial Vehicle (UAV) and Aircraft in the 1500 to 5000 lbs size class with near term 
market entry onramp.</p>

<p>• As Commercial Aviation pushes towards greater sustainability and a “net-zero” future air 
transportation system, cryogenic alternative fuels have been postulated as possible solutions
</p>
<p>• LH2 aircraft concepts are currently being developed by industry, with significant programs funded 
in Europe and the U.K.
</p>
<p>• “Green LNG” could also be a possible approach, and ideas to utilize liquid methane or natural 
gas for aircraft have also been postulated in the past
</p>
<p>• NASA Aeronautics does not currently have significant investment in developing cryogenic aircraft 
technologies, but NASA does have significant expertise and capabilities with cryogenic fuels from 
its space exploration activities  
</p>

<p>Prospects for a Cryogenic Fuel Aviation Future
</p>
<p>1. Meeting attendees had a broad range of opinions on future aviation fuels and the 
challenges of cryogenic fuels – from “no problem, we can do it now” to “we are many 
decades away”
– Part of the discrepancy may be attributable to the application domain (small vehicles vs. large 
</p>
<p>commercial transports)
– Many see a future “H2 economy” as inevitable and therefore aviation’s participation in the economy is 
</p>
<p>certain
2. The capital investments required just to make the fuel available at airports are 
</p>
<p>staggering (not including the introduction of new vehicles)
– Other sustainability approaches, such as SAF, also require large investments
</p>
<p>3. Given the large amount of renewable electricity required to produce “green” hydrogen 
for aviation, aviation may have to build a dedicated electricity/fuel infrastructure 
instead of tapping into the grid 
– Decarbonizing aviation by using renewable energy to produce green cryogenic fuels before first 
</p>
<p>making the overall electricity grid 100% green may result in a net negative climate impact
4. No consensus was reached on the prospects for green LNG/LCH4
</p>
<p>– LNG challenges are not as severe as LH2
</p>
<p>– Some pointed to the ability of LNG to leverage the existing natural gas infrastructure throughout the 
world (as with LH2, “greening” of the LNG supply is still necessary)
</p>
<p>– Others felt zero “tailpipe” CO2 emissions was absolutely necessary
– Strongest advocacy for LNG was for DoD applications
</p>

<p>External Perspective on NASA Research Investment 
Opportunities
</p>
<p>• Materials
– Additive manufacturing and composites for cryo temperatures, 
</p>
<p>cycle fatigue, and hydrogen permeation
– Seals, insulation, embrittlement, thermal expansion
</p>
<p>• Structures
– Fuel tank, pressurized structure, conformal pressure 
</p>
<p>vessels, impact absorbing structures
</p>
<p>• Testing Capabilities and Techniques
– Hydrogen enabled facilities, crashworthiness, impact 
</p>
<p>testing, icing
</p>
<p>• Operations
– ConOps development, fuel system purging, fueling/defueling 
</p>
<p>operations, tarmac hold, failure analysis, contingency 
scenarios unique to cryogenics
</p>
<p>• Systems Studies
– Concept studies, fuel cell vs hydrogen combustion vs 
</p>
<p>combination, fuel cell as secondary power (APU 
replacement), impact of current vs future grid, exploration of 
acceptable boil-off, tank design trades
</p>
<p>• Propulsion and Powertrain
– Combustor design research (fundamental computational 
</p>
<p>and experimental capability development), superconducting 
electrical components, fuel cell technology development, 
engine controls with phase changes, thermal management 
(especially heat exchangers), exploration of innovative 
turbine cycles
</p>
<p>• Fuel Pumps and Valves
• Standards Development Support
• Cryo Technology Roadmap Development
• Sensor Technology
</p>
<p>– Tank level gauging, hydrogen fire detection, hydrogen leak 
detection
</p>
<p>• Climate Research
– Contrails modeling, simulation, and mitigation, cryo fuel 
</p>
<p>leakage impacts at ground/altitude, low particulate impacts 
on contrail characteristics

<p>NASA Overall Perspective
</p>
<p>• NASA is interested in the development of liquid hydrogen aircraft.
– But NASA must develop plan forward that includes existing activities planned.
– Address the full life-cycle of the aircraft
– Address safety and operational activities around the aircraft
</p>
<p>• In aeronautics, NASA doesn’t develop the full solution on its own.
– NASA partners with industry to both to leverage industry’s resources/expertise and 
</p>
<p>ensure better infusion of results into operational systems.
– Focus of US government resources to support US capability.
– Lower funding than the space areas and pragmatic about changing investment 
</p>
<p>focus.
• NASA doesn’t believe there is one solution that will meet all of he 
</p>
<p>energy needs by itself.
– A suite of solutions is needed with each solution needing it’s own R&amp;D
– Enables earlier and phased impact toward a goal of “green” aviation.</p>
