Abstract



The parasitic loss incurred by deflagrative preburning is considered to be one of the key challenges to the effective
implementation of rotating detonation engine (RDE) systems. Thus far, there have been relatively limited high-
fidelity, spatio-temporally resolved measurements of the preburning process as a function of the reactant conditions
and composition within an RDE. In this work, simultaneous high-repetition-rate broadband OH* chemilumines-
cence and OH planar laser-induced fluorescence (PLIF) imaging are employed to investigate deflagrative burning
dynamics in a fully optically accessible CH4-O2 RDE as compared to a H2-air systems under the same condi-
tions. A custom-built optical parametric oscillator (OPO) is coupled with a high-repetition-rate burst-mode laser
to generate the 284 nm source for the excitation of the Q1(9) transition of the OH radical. Significant deflagrative
burning is observed throughout the chamber as a consequence of the oxygen-rich environment. Trailing wave
systems that consume unburned reactants in the region immediately following the primary detonation wave are
observed. The formation of a product gas recirculation zone that entrains and combusts incoming reactants is
observed, and quantitative analysis is performed to gain valuable insight into deflagration characteristics. At an
oxidizer mass flux of Gox ≈ 350kg/m2/s, it is found that the levels of pre-wave burning in CH4-O2 are a factor of
∼2-3 times higher than in H2-air in the region directly ahead of the detonation wave at a global equivalence ratio
Φ ≈ 1.0. This study highlights the key roles of ignition delay and turbulent mixing of the combustion product and
reactant fields on the levels of preburning and establishes a methodology for further investigation, such as for the
effects of various RDE inlet configurations.



Keywords: Pressure Gain Combustion; Deflagration; Rotating Detonation; Planar Laser-Induced Fluorescence

1. Introduction



The past several decades have seen a large rise
in the interest of applying pressure gain combustion
(PGC) as a replacement to modern constant-pressure
combustors, particularly towards the development of
the rotating detonation engine (RDE), a constant-
volume detonation cycle based system. Thermody-
namic cycle analysis studies have shown the theoret-
ical capability for RDEs to offer an approximately
20% increase in thermal efficiency compared to typ-
ical constant-pressure devices operating based on the
Brayton cycle [1, 2]. Additionally, RDE architec-
tures feature a compact design enabling simple yet
effective conversion of chemical energy into kinetic
energy and useful work output. While considerable
work has been accomplished experimentally and nu-
merically to characterize RDE performance and deto-
nation wave characteristics [1, 3–6], significant chal-
lenges still hinder development of RDEs as a viable
replacement to modern-day deflagrative combustors
as net pressure gain in an RDE – which serves as the
fundamental premise of the system – has yet to be
seen in the open literature.



To understand the complex RDE flow-field, a
large effort has been dedicated to optimizing injec-
tor design and investigating injector dynamics [7, 8],
studying reactant mixing and the effects of “para-
sitic deflagration” on operability [9–15], and ana-
lyzing heat transfer characteristics and implementing
cooling schemes to accommodate considerable ther-
mal loads present in RDEs [16, 17]. Laser-based
and general optical diagnostics such as chemilumi-
nescence imaging, planar laser-induced fluorescence
(PLIF), laser absorption spectroscopy (LAS), and co-
herent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS) allow
for visualization of the turbulent combustion product
field and non-intrusive in-situ measurements of tem-
perature and species concentration, and considerable
work has been accomplished in the development of
these techniques in RDE environments for gas phase
reactants, most commonly for air-breathing systems
with gaseous H2 as fuel [7, 18–20]. Use of reac-
tant mixtures more applicable to rocket systems (i.e.,
pure oxygen and hydrocarbon-based fuels) leads to
greater mixture reactivity. Consequently, autoigni-
tion timescales tend toward the detonation wave cy-
cle time, incurring greater levels of pre-wave deflagra-
tion that reduces the heat release available to support
detonative combustion. As such, an understanding of
these processes is crucial in mitigating the effects of
non-idealities in RDEs.



This study investigates deflagration within a ro-
tating detonation rocket engine (RDRE) fueled by a
CH4-O2 reactant mixture. The methodology involves
the simultaneous application of orthogonal measure-
ments, capturing OH* chemiluminescence in the r−θ
plane and OH-PLIF measurements in the r− x plane
at a high acquisition rate of 500 kHz. This dual-
plane imaging allows for comprehensive assessment
of the multi-dimensional characteristics of detonation



wave structures and heat release zones as they traverse
the annular geometry. In order to benchmark these
findings, we conduct a comparative analysis with re-
sults from a prior H2-air investigation [11]. This in-
volves maintaining identical combustor geometry and
oxidizer mass flux in both experiments, facilitating a
direct one-to-one comparison between air-breathing
and rocket systems. The challenges and diagnos-
tic accomplishments associated with employing high-
speed laser-based measurements in an RDRE under
more realistic conditions are explored. Additionally,
a methodology for ongoing investigation is outlined
to further enrich our understanding of the intricate dy-
namics inherent in these systems.



2. Methodology



2.1. Experimental setup and conditions



The experimental test rig is described in detail in
Athmanathan et al. [11, 21] and a brief description is
provided here for continuity. Fig. 1 shows a cross-
section view of the RDE flow path. The outer radius
of the RDE annulus is ∼68 mm with a channel length
of ∼90 mm. The oxidizer (air or gaseous oxygen) is
introduced into the combustor through an converging
diverging slot injector with a throat gap of 1.42 mm at
ambient temperature of ∼298 K. Downstream of the
throat at an area ratio (ϵ) of 1.18, fuel is injected ra-
dially outward in a jet-in-cross-flow manner through
a fuel injector ring featuring 100 evenly spaced ori-
fices, each with an effective hydraulic diameter of
0.93 mm (same dimension per [11]). The inlet con-
tinues to expand until it reaches ϵ = 1.81, at which
point a sudden expansion through a backward-facing
step (BFS) occurs prior to the constant area section
(ϵ = 7) forming the detonation channel. Flow of pro-
pellants is metered using sonic nozzles (ASME MFC-
3M) with an uncertainty of ∼2% in the mass flow rate.
To perform optical and laser-based diagnostics, a ∼20
mm thick fused quartz outer-body cylinder (Heraeus
TSC3) is installed in the RDE with optical access ex-
tending from the oxidizer inlet plenum to the exhaust
plane. The RDE is operated remotely using auto-
mated valves and a camera/laser system controlled by
a 5 kHz NI PXIe data acquisition and control system.
Prior to ignition, fuel and oxidizer flows are started
and the reactants prime the detonation channel. Igni-

tion is initiated by a H2-O2 predetonator charge which

A single oxidizer mass flux is selected for
this study to showcase the effectiveness of spatio-
temporally resolved assessments of deflagration
within the detonation channel. A comparative analy-
sis is conducted with an experimental setup for high-
speed OH-PLIF measurements in a H2-air RDE, de-
tailed in Athmanathan et al. [11]. While the pre-
vious study employed air and H2 as reactants, the
current investigation utilizes O2 and CH4 for oxi-
dizer and fuel, respectively. Consistency is main-
tained in the overall RDE geometry and the fuel in-
jection scheme. The OH-PLIF measurements are then
analyzed to compare deflagration levels between air-
breathing and rocket systems. In both cases, the oxi-
dizer mass flux is held at approximately 350 kg/m2/s
with a global equivalence ratio of Φ ≈ 1.0, and tests
are repeated for statistical significance. To prevent
damage to the quartz outer-bodies, systematic sweeps
of operational parameters are limited, taking into con-
sideration destructive failure modes observed during
testing with CH4-O2. An additional challenge is ad-
dressing the potential misconception surrounding au-
toignition studies. This study aims to clarify the rele-
vance of autoignition limits in RDEs, and the applied
methodology not only provides experimental insights
into deflagrative loss mechanisms but also contributes
valuable information on limits of autoignition studies
and their applicability during the reactant refill pro-
cess. The subsequent sections delve into the specifics
of the measurement techniques applied and the quan-
titative analysis methods employed.

In the interest of identifying product gas species

plied to gain multidimensional understanding of the
pre-wave deflagrative heat-release. This includes 1)

For the OH-PLIF measurements, the Q1(9) excita-

tion of the OH radical is targeted and excited by 284
nm light outputted from an optical parametric oscil-

tunable OPO is shown in Fig. 2 with readers directed
to [22] for additional details on the OPO, which is
configured to generate ∼ 250µJ/pulse of 284 nm light
at 500 kHz. The beam is routed to the test cell and fo-
cused down to a ∼35 mm long laser sheet into the
channel of the RDE test article. The simultaneous
OH* chemiluminescence images are taken to corre-
late the OH-PLIF data with the location of the deto-
nation wave relative to the PLIF plane. A high-speed
camera (Shimadzu HPV-X2 + 45 mm UV lens) cou-
pled to a Lambert Instruments UV HiCatt image in-
tensifier with a 320 ± 20 nm band-pass filter to selec-
tively capture the ground state hydroxyl (OH) radical
emission in the r − x plane. While the intensifier
is gated down to 25 ns to reduce background OH*
chemiluminescence during wave passage through the
PLIF plane, bleed OH* signal due to high number
density in the wake of detonation persists. Spectral
separation between OH* and emission from OH-PLIF
is not possible since the emission from OH* and the
PLIF signal occur at the same spectral wavelength.
Consequently, a few frames exhibit cross-talk OH*
signals in the OH-PLIF camera (∼ 3−5 frames at 500
kHz). Serendipitously, the current analysis focuses on
the refill process, where background OH* is minimal,
rather than the frames containing OH* cross-talk dur-
ing the wave passage. This ensures that their broad
conclusions remain highly relevant.



A second high-speed camera (Phantom TMX 7510
+ 45 mm UV lens) is coupled to a Lambert Instru-
ments UV-Visible HiCatt image intensifier to cap-
ture broadband OH* chemiluminescence from the
detonation wave in the axial-azimuthal x − θ plane.
While the signal intensities are averaged in the ra-
dial direction, these images provide valuable insight
into the azimuthal structure and propagation dynam-
ics of the detonation wave. The captured OH-PLIF
images complement the chemiluminescence data by
providing radially resolved characteristics of heat re-
lease and combustion product zones. In tandem,
these simultaneous measurements allow for multi-
dimensional temporally and spatially resolved heat
release dynamics of the detonation wave in the chan-
nel, and each imaging plane is shown in Fig. 3. A
third supplementary camera provides axially aver-
aged aft-end chemiluminescence images in the radial-
azimuthal (r-θ) plane for observation of the operating
characteristics of the system.

3.1. Relevant key findings from prior work



To provide a baseline for comparison, the results
of [11] are discussed briefly, as the data for this work
are collected in the same RDE test platform. Readers
are directed to this study for additional details. Us-
ing only a H2-air reactant mixture, the general detona-
tion wave structure features a primary reaction front
that consumes a majority of the fresh reactant mix-
ture and leaves behind a pocket of vitiated unburned
reactants mixed with burned gas products. Through
a close-coupled trailing azimuthal shock system, the
unburned reactants are shock-compressed and com-
busted through an azimuthal reflected shock combus-
tion (ARSC) wave, which has been shown to be a
detonation in its own capacity. After the detonation
wave system passes, OH-PLIF images in the r-x plane
reveal the presence of hot products in the injection
nearfield near the BFS. Post-wave deflagrative hot



gases, characterized by the presence of OH, are ev-
ident. However, during the subsequent refill process,
there is no observed production of OH at either the
contact surface or the shear layer. This absence is at-
tributed to autoignition delays, which are an order of
magnitude larger than the detonation cycle time, con-
sidering a mean burned gas product temperature of
800-1200 K, according to concurrent computational
measurements. Following this, the product gases are
advected axially downstream towards the exhaust as
fresh reactant refill occurs after the inlet stream re-
covers from the significant pressure impulse induced
by the detonation wave.



3.2. Detonation wave morphology



With CH4 and pure O2 serving as the reactants in
this work, the operating mode of the combustor paral-
lels that of the H2-air system at Φ ≈ 1.0. Aft-end
images enable identification the operating mode of
the system, e.g., number of wave-heads, detonation
wave speed, and propagation direction, and both mix-
tures exhibit single-wave limit-cycle behavior. A 2-
D cross-correlation and Fast Fourier transform is ap-
plied to the aft-end images to extract detonation cy-
cle frequencies and detonation wave velocities, and
analysis shows an increase from ∼1800 m/s (∼250
µs detonation wave period) to ∼2100 m/s (∼190 µs
detonation wave period).



The morphology of the non-premixed CH4-O2 det-
onation wave is depicted in Fig. 4 from x-θ plane
radially-averaged OH* chemiluminescence images.

front, contrasting with the far-field (Zone II), where

subsonic reactants result in higher static properties

minosity. Additionally, a wave bifurcation leading
to ARSC is observed in the injection near-field, at-

Immediately preceding the leading detonation

trailing ARSC wave to promote full combustion of the
incoming reactant stream. This behavior is observed
for both CH4/O2 and H2/air reactant mixtures, albeit
to a more significant extent for the former. Follow-

These images and aforementioned analysis pro-
vide several key insights into deflagrative burning
within the region immediately neighboring the deto-
nation wave front. First, contact-surface deflagration
is not readily observed despite direct interaction be-
tween cold reactants and product gas from the deto-
nation, either due to extremities in the local equiva-
lence ratio forming a local buffer zone or low ignition
Damkohler numbers to initiate reactions at the con-
tact point. This does not imply that contact burning
is not occurring at the axial edge of the fresh reac-
tant stream, but that it may have a lower impact on
detonation efficiency compared to other deflagrative
loss mechanisms. Second, levels of pre-wave burn-
ing in the CH4-O2 case are significantly higher than
for H2-air, and pressure traces record minor change to
the mean chamber pressure during operation (differ-
ences on the order of 10−1 bar). Thus, it is presumed
that the primary driving physical mechanism for this
effect is shorter timescale reaction-rate kinetics asso-
ciated with combustion in pure-oxygen environments
rather than increased pressures, which marks a con-
sideration for the use of various reactant mixtures for



rocket applications. As the point of interest in this
work is primarily preburning ahead (10’s of µs) of
the detonation wave, further quantitative analysis of
deflagrative signal levels is carried out and detailed
for the latter half of the wave cycle in the following
section to provide a one-to-one comparison with H2-
air at Φ ≈ 1.0 and matching oxidizer mass fluxes.



3.3. Image processing for quantitative analysis



To quantify the level of deflagrative burning in the
channel throughout the detonation wave cycle, two re-
gions of interest (ROIs) are selected for image pro-
cessing as shown below in Fig. 6. Both ROIs extend
35 mm from the BFS, approximately matching the ax-
ial detonation wave height, and each ROI is 5.35 mm
in height – half of the detonation channel. ROI 1 en-
compasses the upper portion of the image along the
outer radius of the channel to understand deflagrative
burning of in the fresh reactant stream throughout the
reactant refill process. ROI 2 contains the region to-
wards the inner radius of the channel and the BFS
to investigate deflagrative burning and reactant en-
trainment within the product gas recirculation zone.
The image processing algorithm includes a rolling-
ball background subtraction and corrections for spa-
tial intensity variations. Subsequently, a 10% thresh-
old binarization is applied. The mean intensity value
in each ROI in the binarized image is computed, giv-
ing the “signal fraction” i.e., the fraction of the ROI
that contains OH. While noise is unavoidable, partic-
ularly when the detonation wave passes through the
PLIF plane due to intense background OH* chemi-
luminescence, the binarization script captures effec-
tively captures detailed structures in the images pre-
ceding the detonation wave. Additionally, a sensitiv-
ity analysis is performed on the length of each ROI as
well as the thresholding level, and the general trends
and communication of the underlying physics remain
unchanged with respect to these parameters.

4. Conclusions



Within the scope of the current study, several note-
worthy conclusions can be drawn. The extent of
pre-wave deflagration is quantified and compared be-
tween reactant mixtures pertinent to air-breathing and
rocket systems, and it is shown that rocket RDEs
may experience a factor of 2-3 greater level of defla-
gration throughout the detonation cycle compared to
their air-breathing counterparts with the same geome-
try and operating conditions. In both cases, deflagra-
tion (presence of OH) persists far behind the leading
detonation wave front – up to half of the detonation
cycle period. As the incoming reactant stream dy-
namically responds to the detonation wave impulse,
the transient refill process causes a recirculation zone
to form in the injection near-field due to shear layer
interactions in the geometry of this particular RDE
test article. Subsequent fresh reactant entrainment
and OH production in this region indicates pre-wave
deflagration directly ahead of the detonation wave.
Through numerical studies and limited experimental
work, the role of a recirculation zone near the injector
for certain geometries has been stipulated to promote
flameholding and sustain stable limit-cycle detonation
in RDEs [12, 15]. To the authors’ knowledge, this is
the first experimental visualization of this recircula-
tion zone acting as a flame-holder, specifically for the
case of of a CH4-O2 reactants.



7











As a precursory study in establishing a refined
methodology for the investigation of deflagration dy-
namics in RDEs, this work demonstrates the potential
of the outlined approach in enabling temporally and
spatially resolved in-situ measurements for enhanced
understanding of fundamental physics. Additionally,
the application of laser-based diagnostic techniques
in an RDE operating with a CH4-O2 reactant mix-
ture provides a pathway to explore relevant systems
under more realistic conditions that typically extend
beyond the practical capabilities of academic labora-
tory settings. Consequently, a continuation of the cur-
rent work will investigate a broader range of operating
conditions to provide further physics-based insights
into the effects of pre-wave and post-wave deflagra-
tion on detonation efficiency and RDE operability.
