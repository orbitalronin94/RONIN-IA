<div class="slide-content"><p>Immersion Thermocouples bought off the shelf</p>
<p>E-type for cryo temperature applications</p>
<p>K-type for hot temperature applications</p>
<p>Come in different lengths and thicknesses (typ. 6”, 12”; 1/16”, 1/8”, ¼”)</p>
<p>Installed with a swagelok fitting for the appropriate length</p>
<p>Need to perform protuberance analysis for critical applications</p>
<p>Use UTR RTD to know the temperature of the transition point</p>
<p>from T/C wire to copper  (120 ft of T/C cable and ~180ft of copper to signal conditioning)</p>
<p>Signal conditioning equipment receives mV(DC) from sensor</p>
<p>TCs do not get calibrated at a lab, use thermocouple curves for EU conversion. </p>
<p>Use a voltage substitution calibration to verify amplifier and signal conditioning operation</p>
<p>PROS - Inexpensive (unless used for LOX environments, cleaned locally)</p>
<p>CONS - At LH temperatures may not scale correctly, noisy if tip T/C junction grounded</p>
<p></p>

<p>More accurate way to measure temperature </p>
<p>Resisistive element at the tip of the sensor (resistance changes with temperature)</p>
<p>Signal conditioning circuit applies a 1mA constant current through the RTD .</p>
<p>	  (~300 ft overall distance from Signal conditioning)</p>
<p>Signal conditioning reads voltage and scales appropriately</p>
<p>Use ITS90 coefficients from the sensor calibration report for scaling</p>
<p>Bought for an appropriate length. Not one size fits all. Military style connector</p>
<p>Mostly use 1256 Ohms ice points  and sometimes 1000 or 100 Ohm ice point.</p>
<p>Use a precision resistor substitution calibration to verify amplifier/scaling operation</p>
<p>PROS – More rugged construction than a T/C, high degree of accuracy (0.1 deg R)</p>
<p>CONS – Long lead time to procure, Expensive ($5K+)</p>

<p>Pressure transducers bought off the shelf (some may have extra requirements)</p>
<p>Mounted near the point of interest using a sense line.  (~300 ft overall distance from Signal conditioning)</p>
<p>Military style connector (6 or 10 pin)</p>
<p>Wheatstone bridge circuit (350 Ohm)</p>
<p>Shunt resistor inside the transducer (80% of range)</p>
<p>Output 3mV/V</p>
<p>Signal conditioning equipment provides excitation, opens and closes shunt relay for calibration verification and reads mV(DC) from sensor</p>
<p>mV received are scaled to EUs with information from the sensor calibration report</p>
<p>Bought for an appropriate pressure. Not a one size fits all.</p>
<p>Most can be disassembled and serviced/cleaned</p>
<p>PROS – behave well in most environments (&lt;1% uncertainty)</p>
<p>CONS – susceptible to moisture intrusion, long lead times, expensive to clean and calibrate</p>

<p>Typically use pressure or temperature(shown)</p>

<div class="slide-content"><p>Vibration measurements at NASA Stennis are common. </p>
<p>Accelerometer data helps validate structural models and provides a way to monitor turbopump health. </p>
<p>To avoid damage to hardware or test stands, rocket engine tests can be set to terminate automatically if excess vibration levels are detected. </p>
<p>The most common type of accelerometers used at NASA Stennis are piezoelectric charge output accelerometers. </p>
<p>These accelerometers output in picoCoulombs per G. Many installations use inline charge amplifiers, but at SSC, high impedance voltage amplifiers are commonly used. </p>
<p>With our setup, capacitance (C), largely in the cabling, converts charge (Q) to voltage (V). </p>
<p>Sensitivities can be converted using the equation </p>
<p></p>
<p>PROS – Able to withstand hot environment installations (special washers)</p>
<p>CONS - Routine capacitance measurements are required for system calibrations. 	(although automating this issue with new signal conditioning equipment.)</p>
<p></p>
<p></p>
<p>Accelerometers – Piezoelectric Charge type</p>
<div class="embedded" id="slide11_rId3"></div>
</div>
<div class="slide-master-content"><p></p>
<p>Stennis Space Center</p>

<p></p>
<p>Stennis Space Center</p>
<div class="slide-content"><p>IEPE (Integrated Electronics Piezo-Electric) sensors have built-in charge converters. These devices convert charge to voltage and send a high-level voltage output to downstream instrumentation. </p>
<p>IEPE accelerometers require special signal conditioning to power their built-in electronics. They are unable to withstand high temperatures as well as charge output accelerometers. </p>
<p>A hybrid technique uses charge output sensors, but with inline charge converters (which have the IEPE circuitry).</p>
<p>Triaxial accelerometers measure 3 directions with one sensor (but still require 3 digitizer channels). </p>
<p>Variable Capacitance (Micro Electro Mechanical System) accelerometers (unlike other types) can measure all the way down to 0 Hz.(DC output) </p>

<p>In Place Flow Meter Calibration – Why?</p>
<p>Large Flow Rates – Difficult to duplicate 17K GPM(LH)  and 6K GPM (LOX)</p>
<p>If other facilities available would require flow meter removal – Potential test impact</p>
<p>Difficulty in maintaining precision using other methods – Currently able to achieve &lt; .1% uncertainty</p>
<p>The calibration is accomplished during ground test “hot-fire” test runs.</p>

<p>LIQUID LEVEL GAGE</p>
<p>Kronos Tanks</p>
<p>Used to calibrate tank volumes between tank switch gage switches</p>
<p>Uses tap water for calibration medium then corrected for cryogenic propellant</p>
<p>Water flows from tank through flow meter into a volumetric standard (Kronos) where the volume is precisely measured, then it is dumped</p>
<p>Calibrator volumes are NIST traceable</p>
<p>Consists of two calibrated volumes in which the water flows alternately through</p>
<p>Measures the number of complete calibrator tank volumes and partial tank volumes that exists between run tank volume switch intervals</p>
<p>Data is reduced and corrected for cryogenic propellants</p>
<p>Tank Shrinkage correction factor is also used due to differences in volume between a tank at ambient temperature and a tank with propellants</p>
<p></p>
<p></p>
<p></p>
<p>Run Tank Water Calibration</p>
</div>
<div class="slide-master-content"><p></p>
<p>Stennis Space Center</p>
</div>
<p></p>
<p>Stennis Space Center</p>
<div class="slide-content"><p></p>
<p></p>
<p></p>
<p>Flow Meter is calibrated each time a non-transfer portion of a test is run at a constant power level</p>
<p>We typically calibrate the flowmeters at 111% and check at other power levels depending on the thrust profile</p>
<p>Do not want to run when propellants are being transferred since this will add to tank volume</p>
<p>Do not want to use data covering transition periods because the flow meter inertia will be affected</p>
<p></p>
<p>Run Calibration of Flowmeter</p>
</div>
<div class="slide-master-content"><p></p>
<p>Stennis Space Center</p>

<p></p>
<p>Stennis Space Center</p>
<div class="slide-content"><p>As propellant passes through the flow meter on the way to engine the flow meter turns in proportion to the volumetric flow</p>
<p>An induction sense coil produces a pulse (pip) each time a magnet on the periphery of the turbine passes the sense coil</p>
<p></p>
<p></p>
<p></p>
<p>The “pip” is sent to the acquisition system and is reduced as pips/sec</p>
<p>During the same time period the acquisition system records tank volume switch pick ups</p>
<p></p>
<p>Run Calibration of Flowmeter</p>
<div class="embedded" id="slide19_rId2"></div>
<p>pips</p>
</div>
<div class="slide-master-content"><p></p>
<p>Stennis Space Center</p>
</div>
<p></p>
<p>Stennis Space Center</p>
<div class="slide-content"><p>This tank volume of flow during the time interval becomes the volumetric flow rate expressed in gal/sec</p>
<p>The gal in each tank volume determined from the water calibration must be corrected for tank shrinkage in arriving at gal/sec</p>
<p>The pips/sec from the flow meter divided by the gal/sec derived from the tank interval equals pips/gal</p>
<p>This pips/gal is the KF (or flow meter constant) for the flow meter</p>
<p></p>
<p>Run Calibration of Flowmeter</p>
</div>
<div class="slide-master-content"><p></p>
<p>Stennis Space Center</p>

<p>Stennis Space Center</p>
<div class="slide-content"><p>The requirements that we follow for in-place flowmeter calibrations are:</p>
<p></p>
<p>a)  No propellant transfer from barge(s) to run tank</p>
<p>b)  No sooner than 25 seconds after engine start</p>
<p>c)  No power level changes within switch interval</p>
<p>d)  At least 5 seconds after finishing a power level change</p>
<p>e)  A minimum of three switch intervals (LOX) or four switch intervals (LH), and</p>
<p>f)  No other changes which affect the engine's propellant consumption</p>
<p>	1) Facility -- e.g. a fuel repress toggle,</p>
<p>	2) Engine Hardware - e.g. a nozzle tube rupture,</p>
<p>	3) Engine Control System - e.g. electrical lockup.</p>
<p></p>
<p>As you can see, we wait 25 seconds after start and 5 seconds after a power level change for the system to stabilize.</p>
<p></p>
