Pressure transducers bought off the shelf (some may have extra requirements)


Mounted near the point of interest using a sense line. (~300 ft overall distance from Signal conditioning)


Military style connector (6 or 10 pin)


Wheatstone bridge circuit (350 Ohm)


Shunt resistor inside the transducer (80% of range)


Output 3mV/V


Signal conditioning equipment provides excitation, opens and closes shunt relay for calibration verification and reads mV(DC) from sensor


mV received are scaled to EUs with information from the sensor calibration report


Bought for an appropriate pressure. Not a one size fits all.


Most can be disassembled and serviced/cleaned


PROS – behave well in most environments (<1% uncertainty)


CONS – susceptible to moisture intrusion, long lead times, expensive to clean and calibrate

Vibration measurements at NASA Stennis are common. 


Accelerometer data helps validate structural models and provides a way to monitor turbopump health. 


To avoid damage to hardware or test stands, rocket engine tests can be set to terminate automatically if excess vibration levels are detected. 


The most common type of accelerometers used at NASA Stennis are piezoelectric charge output accelerometers. 


These accelerometers output in picoCoulombs per G. Many installations use inline charge amplifiers, but at SSC, high impedance voltage amplifiers are commonly used. 


With our setup, capacitance (C), largely in the cabling, converts charge (Q) to voltage (V).

PROS – Able to withstand hot environment installations (special washers)


CONS - Routine capacitance measurements are required for system calibrations. (although automating this issue with new signal conditioning equipment.)








Accelerometers – Piezoelectric Charge type











Stennis Space Center







Stennis Space Center



IEPE (Integrated Electronics Piezo-Electric) sensors have built-in charge converters. These devices convert charge to voltage and send a high-level voltage output to downstream instrumentation. 


IEPE accelerometers require special signal conditioning to power their built-in electronics. They are unable to withstand high temperatures as well as charge output accelerometers. 


A hybrid technique uses charge output sensors, but with inline charge converters (which have the IEPE circuitry).


Triaxial accelerometers measure 3 directions with one sensor (but still require 3 digitizer channels). 


Variable Capacitance (Micro Electro Mechanical System) accelerometers (unlike other types) can measure all the way down to 0 Hz.(DC output)

In Place Flow Meter Calibration – Why?


Large Flow Rates – Difficult to duplicate 17K GPM(LH) and 6K GPM (LOX)


If other facilities available would require flow meter removal – Potential test impact


Difficulty in maintaining precision using other methods – Currently able to achieve < .1% uncertainty


The calibration is accomplished during ground test “hot-fire” test runs.

Kronos Tanks


Used to calibrate tank volumes between tank switch gage switches


Uses tap water for calibration medium then corrected for cryogenic propellant


Water flows from tank through flow meter into a volumetric standard (Kronos) where the volume is precisely measured, then it is dumped


Calibrator volumes are NIST traceable


Consists of two calibrated volumes in which the water flows alternately through


Measures the number of complete calibrator tank volumes and partial tank volumes that exists between run tank volume switch intervals


Data is reduced and corrected for cryogenic propellants


Tank Shrinkage correction factor is also used due to differences in volume between a tank at ambient temperature and a tank with propellants

Flow Meter is calibrated each time a non-transfer portion of a test is run at a constant power level


We typically calibrate the flowmeters at 111% and check at other power levels depending on the thrust profile


Do not want to run when propellants are being transferred since this will add to tank volume


Do not want to use data covering transition periods because the flow meter inertia will be affected





Run Calibration of Flowmeter

Stennis Space Center



As propellant passes through the flow meter on the way to engine the flow meter turns in proportion to the volumetric flow


An induction sense coil produces a pulse (pip) each time a magnet on the periphery of the turbine passes the sense coil











The “pip” is sent to the acquisition system and is reduced as pips/sec


During the same time period the acquisition system records tank volume switch pick ups

This tank volume of flow during the time interval becomes the volumetric flow rate expressed in gal/sec


The gal in each tank volume determined from the water calibration must be corrected for tank shrinkage in arriving at gal/sec


The pips/sec from the flow meter divided by the gal/sec derived from the tank interval equals pips/gal


This pips/gal is the KF (or flow meter constant) for the flow meter

The requirements that we follow for in-place flowmeter calibrations are:





a) No propellant transfer from barge(s) to run tank


b) No sooner than 25 seconds after engine start


c) No power level changes within switch interval


d) At least 5 seconds after finishing a power level change


e) A minimum of three switch intervals (LOX) or four switch intervals (LH), and


f) No other changes which affect the engine's propellant consumption


 1) Facility -- e.g. a fuel repress toggle,


 2) Engine Hardware - e.g. a nozzle tube rupture,


 3) Engine Control System - e.g. electrical lockup.





As you can see, we wait 25 seconds after start and 5 seconds after a power level change for the system to stabilize.
