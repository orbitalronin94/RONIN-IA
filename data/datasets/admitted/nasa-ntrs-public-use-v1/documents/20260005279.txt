The SLD Challenge



Quantifying Liquid Water Content (LWC) in Supercooled Large Droplet (SLD)
environments is critical for aircraft certification.



Thermal anemometry remains the standard for in-situ measurements, but it struggles in
large-droplet regimes.



The core problem: Impact-induced mass loss (splashing and aerodynamic stripping)
causes severe signal deficits, frequently exceeding 40% for standard cylindrical elements.

The NASA Icing Research Tunnel (IRT) is a closed-loop, refrigerated wind tunnel.



Capable of simulating both 14 CFR Part 25 Appendix C and Appendix O (SLD)

Features an array of air-atomizing nozzles with independent control over water and air

This architecture crucially allows for the decoupling of LWC from the droplet Median

Thermal Anemometry Fundamentals



Hot-wire probes infer LWC from the electrical power required to evaporate impinging
water.



The sensing elements are maintained at a constant target temperature (approximately
140 ◦C).



The Fundamental Assumption: This measurement principle assumes that all impacting
water remains on the wire long enough to vaporize. If droplets splash or shed, the sensor
systematically under-reads the true LWC.

Geometry Matters



Testing spans three specific micro-scale element geometries:



021 Wire: A 0.53mm cylindrical wire.
083 Wire: A 2.11mm or 2.4mm reverse half-pipe (convex face to flow).
Half-Pipe: A concave element optimized for retention.



Different probe housings inherently alter the local aerodynamic flow fields approaching
the wires:



The Multi-Element (MW) shroud induces a bluff-body deceleration.
The Ice Crystal Detector (ICD) airfoil induces local flow acceleration.

Breaking Calibration Circularity



Standard practice often calibrates hot-wires against facility cloud standards that were
originally derived from other master hot-wires.



To break this circularity, this experiment utilizes the Isokinetic Probe 2 (IKP2).



The IKP2 uses total evaporation thermodynamics (rather than convective heat transfer)

The Efficiency Framework



The overall collection efficiency of a hot-wire sensor is driven by three distinct physical
probabilities:



Etot = Ecoll · Eret · Eevap



Ecoll: Aerodynamic collision efficiency.



Eret: Mechanical mass retention efficiency.



Eevap: Thermodynamic evaporation efficiency.



To understand the true mechanics of retention (Eret), we must mathematically isolate it
from aerodynamic capture and thermal evaporation.

Validating Aerodynamics & Thermodynamics



Aerodynamics (Ecoll):
Verified via 3D Lagrangian particle tracking.
For large droplets (MVD > 50 µm), inertia easily overcomes flow streamlines, resulting in
Ecoll ≈ 1.0. Aerodynamic bypass is not the cause of the signal deficit.



Thermodynamics (Eevap):
Verified via a high-fidelity 1-D electro-thermal model.
A simulated “stress test” at extreme liquid loading (3.5 gm−3) confirms the sensors maintain
a high thermal margin, ensuring Eevap ≈ 1.0.

Isolating Mass Retention (Eret)



With aerodynamics and thermodynamics mathematically decoupled, we can isolate the
residual efficiency.



Because the droplets successfully impact the sensor (Ecoll ≈ 1.0), and the sensor possesses
the thermal capacity to vaporize them (Eevap ≈ 1.0), the observed SLD deficit is strictly
mechanical.



High-inertia droplets physically splash or are stripped off the wire by aerodynamic shear
before a complete phase change can occur.

Weber vs. Sommerfeld Scaling



The Weber Number (We):
Scales disruptive kinetic energy against restorative surface tension.
Successfully predicts droplet deformation but is insufficient for predicting mass ejection. A
droplet can flatten at a high We without splashing if viscous forces dampen it.



The Sommerfeld Number (K):



Defines the complete physical descriptor: K = We1/2Re1/4



Successfully captures the physical splashing threshold by incorporating the stabilizing effect
of viscous dissipation within the spreading lamella.

The Splashing Threshold (Kcrit)



The critical splashing threshold (Kcrit) is not a universal constant; it maps directly to
probe geometry and local aerodynamics.



Airfoil Housings (ICD): Flow acceleration over the airfoil increases the effective impact
energy of the droplet.



Bluff-Body Housings (MW): The shroud decelerates local flow, reducing impact energy.



Consequently, droplets reach the splashing threshold at a significantly lower freestream
Kcrit on the airfoil compared to the bluff body.

The Hazard of “Artifact Knees”



A nonlinear least-squares solver will unconditionally return a breakpoint (x0) for a
piecewise model, because the model has no valid state without one.



When data are purely linear or too scattered, the resulting breakpoint is an artifact: it is
physically meaningless and statistically insignificant.



The Diagnostic Signature: These artifact knees “chase” the initial guess across the
data range rather than converging to a stable value.

Auditable Model Selection



To separate real physics from fitting artifacts, we require unanimous agreement across
three independent criteria:



1 F -test for Nested Models: Does the piecewise model reduce the residual sum of squares
more than chance allows? (p < 0.05).



2 Breakpoint Stability Test: Does the breakpoint converge to the same value regardless of
the optimizer’s starting guess? (sx0 < 0.15).



3 Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC): Does the model improvement justify the extra
parameter penalty? (∆BIC < 0).



Any disagreement yields a conservative linear selection flagged as “indeterminate”.

The Verdict & Uncertainty Propagation



The Five-Element Verdict:
Two Piecewise: MW-HP and ICD-083 (clean, stable breakpoints).
One Linear: MW-083 (clean linear unanimous selection).
Two Indeterminate: MW-021 and ICD-HP (disagreement between F -test and stability;
defaulted to linear).



Hybrid Uncertainty Propagation:
Taylor expansion (Kline–McClintock) is used by default for speed and reproducibility.
The logic dispatches to Monte Carlo only near the breakpoint, where the discontinuous
derivative invalidates a Taylor expansion.
