Previous investigations have extensively explored crew missions to and from Mars, with a prevailing approach termed the 'conjunction' class, necessitating an approximately 500-day stay at Mars for a cost-effective return. However, this configuration results in a mission duration exceeding 1000 days (roughly 3 years). In order to reduce crew health and surface operations risks, a shortened mission duration of 850 days is being explored by using a short ~30 day Mars surface stay and a ‘opposition’ class approach. A non-nuclear approach is possible by combining solar electric propulsion and a chemical stage. While past mission concepts for the conjunction missions also used solar electric propulsion they limited the SEP power to <700 kW. In order to achieve the 850 day round trip more SEP power is needed. A 1MW class SEP-Chem vehicle was found sufficient to perform the 850 day mission. The biggest challenge for the design will be the large, high power solar arrays, mainly from their deployment and stability. This work describes the design of the SEP-Chem vehicle, assembled in a Near Rectilinear Halo Orbit (NRHO) and employing super-heavy class and the Space Launch System (SLS) launchers. 


Introduction


Study Background and Assumptions


Many previous studies have examined sending crews to and from Mars. The most economical involved a conjunction-class mission, whereby the crew spends around 500 days on Mars waiting for a ‘cheap’ return. The total mission time results in an over 1000-day mission duration (about 3 years). Given the current in-space crew experience level of only one year on the ISS, it is of interest to reduce that time to only two years to reduce risk and minimize the required Mars surface infrastructure. The Mars Transportation Assessment Study [1] sought to achieve this ~ two year mission using nuclear propulsion solutions, both nuclear thermal and combined nuclear electric and chemical (NEP-Chem).


This design assumed a 2039 opposition piloted Mars mission as a driving case. The ∆V required to perform such a mission in only 850 days (for the 2039 opportunity at least) is about three times that of the three-year conjunction mission. The rocket equation shows that this mission would then require several times the propellant of the three-year mission. Based on the need of lunar campaigns, a limit of two SLS launchers with 8.4 m fairings was imposed for the piloted transportation portion of the mission, limiting the size of the system. The propellants for the mission were to be launched with super-heavy class launchers.


The combination of low thrust solar electric propulsion (EP) and high thrust chemical was found to be useful in previous design studies, albeit for longer trip times [2]. Such a combination allowed the low-thrust system to provide a significant change in velocity (∆V) during the interplanetary portions of the mission. This notably reduced the ∆V required by the high thrust system to capture and depart from the Mars gravity well, where the high thrust ‘impulsive’ system is more efficient due to the Oberth Effect [3].








Fig. 1 1 MW SEP-Chem Piloted Mars Transit Vehicle


The 850 day round trip mission required 1 MWe of solar arrays (at Earth distance) to power the EP system and a separate cryogenic chemical stage. Fig. 1 shows the overall assembled vehicle with the SEP stage, the habitat, and the chemical stage at Mars.


Many of the systems were the same or scaled from the NEP-Chem vehicle [4], e.g., chemical stage, xenon Hall thruster propulsion system and the habitat. The main challenge was to develop a concept of a 1 MWe solar power system to integrate with the other elements. This paper highlights key design trades and aspects of the 1 MWe Piloted Opposition Class SEP-Chem concept and discusses an alternative design using krypton Hall thrusters. Finally, the SEP-Chem vehicle is compared with a NEP-Chem vehicle with the same 850 day trip time. While there are many missions which could use this SEP vehicle alone to send cargo, pre-position chemical propellants/stages, or send back-up return vehicles, they will be described in other publications. 


Concept of Operations (CONOPS) and Mission Design


Due to the multiple launch and staging phases of the SEP-Chem vehicle, it is important to layout the concept of operations (CONOPS) to ensure all subsystems are sufficiently designed for all mission phases. The crewed vehicle CONOPS is described briefly below to support a piloted 2039 mission. A graphical representation is shown in Fig. 2. The main in-space transportation elements were designed based on the requirements of this driving-case mission. Other mission options, for other year opposition and conjunction mission opportunities, are similar but may require more or less propellant. 





Fig. 2 Visual Representation of Mission Concept of Operations (CONOPS)


The NRHO was selected as the staging and assembly orbit to maintain commonality with the Artemis architecture, to avoid multi-year spirals and their associated solar radiation damage (as much as 5-10 %), and make it easier to refuel/refurbish the SEP-Chem systems for follow-on missions. By using the SLS and super-heavy launchers, both the large chemical stage and the 1 MW SEP vehicle could be placed in NRHO, with five chemical and two xenon super-heavy tankers to fill up the vehicle. The habitat was ground ruled to be in NRHO, pre-outfitted and ready to be integrated into the stack. Once fueled and assembled, the 1 MW SEP-Chem vehicle would transit to a 10-day high elliptic orbit around the Earth where it meets up with the crew, launched on Orion by an SLS. The vehicle then flies the crew to and from Mars, using the chemical burns in the gravity wells and the SEP primarily in deep space. Returning to the NRHO, the vehicle can be refueled and reused.


With the increase to an 850-day trip limit from the shorter 760-day limit, no Venus flyby is needed, thus avoiding the thermal impacts of getting as close to Venus as other opposition missions. In fact, the phasing for the return leg goes out further than Mars orbit (Fig. 3). The SEP-Chem vehicle was also assessed for other opportunities through 2050. Some of these are more difficult but only the 2043 opportunity was difficult enough to require additional chemical stages.


Launch, Fueling and Assembly Phase (~ 18 months)


Readying a SEP-Chem transfer vehicle for a Mars trip involves a multi-stage process spanning launch, transit, fueling, and assembly. The central components include a habitat, a chemical stage, and an SEP element, each contributing to the comprehensive architecture.


To initiate the mission, the habitat module is launched ahead of time to the NRHO, where it is used for other preparatory missions. This habitat is resupplied as required, ensuring the sustenance and well-being of the crew during the mission. The operational empty mass of the reusable habitat is 26.4 tons with 20 kW of power required and a trash dump of 11.6 kg/day assumed during the transit to and from Mars.


The chemical stage is launched partially fueled by the SLS to cislunar space. It endures a 3-month ballistic transit to the NRHO, deploying its arrays and initiating cryocooler operations to eliminate boil-off enroute. Upon arrival, the chemical stage is inserted into the NRHO, positioning itself for the upcoming assembly phase. Facilitating the fueling process, a series of five superheavy cryogenic propellant tankers, modeled as shortened versions of the chemical stage, are dispatched on similar 3-month transits to NRHO. Each of these tankers carries a substantial payload of 25 tons of LOX/LCH4 or 24 tons of xenon in addition to the propellant required to transit itself to the NRHO. Docking with the chemical stage, they transfer their propellant loads to the chemical stage. Once the propellants are transferred, the tankers are disposed of into deep space.


Next, the SEP module is launched, also utilizing an SLS, to a C3 of -2 km2/s2. During its 3-month journey to the NRHO, the SEP module undergoes a gradual process of deployment of its large solar arrays, alongside commissioning its EP systems. Upon arrival in the NRHO, the SEP module is ready for final fueling by two tankers. Lastly, the assembly phase is executed as the fully fueled SEP module, the Habitat Module, and the fully fueled chemical stage are carefully docked together in the NRHO, ready to transit to the highly elliptic Earth orbit, meet up with the crew, and depart for Mars. 











Mars Mission (~ 2 years) Phase


The Mars vehicle's operational phases encompass a calculated sequence of high thrust and low thrust operations. The mission commences as the vehicle stack (still unmanned) transits from the NRHO to the lunar-distant high Earth orbit (LDHEO), using the SEP system. This process spans approximately three months. 


Once the SEP-Chem vehicle stack is in the 10-day LDHEO, a crewed Orion is launched to meet up with the vehicle. The subsequent orbital rendezvous and docking between the Orion spacecraft and the habitation module take place, facilitating crew transfer and logistical resupply whereby the crew are transferred and await the correct time to fire the chemical stage and depart for Mars. During this waiting time the orbital maintenance maneuvers are performed by the SEP system. Each of these maneuvers, requires about 10 m/s of ∆V per orbit.


The Orion spacecraft, having disengaged from the habitation module, returns to earth. The crewed SEP-Chem vehicle now prepares for Earth departure. Preceding the departure burn, the main solar arrays undergo a strategic feathering procedure, minimizing the loads and potential stresses on the large solar arrays from the upcoming chemical stage burn.


The departure burn is executed by the chemical stage, using a single engine (a cold spare engine is included), taking into consideration gravity losses on the order of approximately 50 m/s. Stability during the burn is provided by the chemical stage and the stack’s reaction control system (RCS), carefully maintaining the vehicle's orientation.





Fig. 3 Earth - Mars - Earth Trajectory


During this departure burn, the acceleration experienced by the stack remains below 0.1 g, easing the loads on the solar arrays and other deployed equipment. Following the departure burn the control moment gyros (CMGs) take over vehicle slewing, orienting both arrays and electric thrusters during the voyage to Mars.


During the transit to Mars, the CMGs continue their role, overseeing vehicle attitude during propulsion phases. During thrusting, the solar array rotary joint gimbals maneuver to maintain optimal solar array alignment. For coasting phases, the CMGs hold the vehicle steady, ensuring stability and conserving propellant resources. As the vehicle approaches Mars, yet another slew maneuver ensues, repointing the vehicle in preparation for the Mars chemical capture burn, a critical action that captures the vehicle in Mars orbit.


Upon Mars arrival, the SEP system is reactivated, performing the final phasing maneuvers to align the vehicle with the awaiting lander. The subsequent rendezvous and docking between the lander and habitat side port occur, allowing for crew transition. Subsequently, two crew members embark on the descent to the Martian surface while the remaining two remain in the orbiting habitat module.


Following a 30 sol stay on Mars, the two crew members ascend using the Mars Ascent Vehicle (MAV) to dock with the habitat side port, marking their return to the orbiting SEP-Chem stack. After separating the MAV, the SEP-Chem vehicle and solar array positioning are re-oriented for the chemical trans-Earth injection burn. This maneuver begins the process of Earth return. SEP is then used exclusively in interplanetary space to return to Earth. 


As the vehicle enters Earth’s gravity well a capture burn is performed by the chemical stage, culminating in a rendezvous with another Orion spacecraft in LDHEO. The crew disembarks from the SEP stack, concluding their mission, while the unmanned SEP-Chem stack proceeds back to the NRHO. Here, it undergoes decoupling into three distinct components: the SEP module, the habitation module, and the chemical stage, each destined for refueling, resupply, and necessary refurbishment. Once resupplied the SEP-Chem stack can be reassembled and reused for another Mars mission about 2 years later. The SEP-Chem vehicle is designed for three separate Mars missions.


A more robust description of the mission design, trades, and history can be found in the mission design focused paper by Laura Burke, et al being presented at this conference [5].

SEP Module Design


The SEP module consists of two parts, the solar array truss, which includes the ISS derived Solar Alpha Rotary Joint (SARJ) gimbals and provides spacing for the deployed solar arrays from the habitat and thruster plumes, and the SEP bus, which holds the 50-t-capable xenon propellant tank, the EP direct drive units (DDUs), the power conditioning elements, and the CMGs. The radiators for the highly efficient DDUs and support systems are body mounted. International docking system standard (IDSS)-based docking systems are placed at both ends of the SEP module allowing docking with the habitat and refueling simultaneously. The cylindrical bus structure also contains the bus avionics, communications systems, and the attitude determination and control (AD&C) system including the monomethyl hydrazine (MMH)/ nitrogen tetroxide (NTO) RCS, (see Fig. 8). Control of such a large vehicle will require large CMGs (similar to ISS). The SEP module is the passive docking element, while the habitat or cargo elements will be the active element. The thrusters are placed on small pallets to enable pointing them and the solar arrays are deployed to minimize plume impingement. 





Fig. 8 SEP Module (zoomed in on transparent bus)








For the SEP module to fit within the fairing the large solar arrays need to be folded. Each of the two EP thruster booms consists of two boom sections that provide a total boom length of 6.88 m. At the end of each of the thruster booms is a two-axis gimbal that will position the thruster platform that contains five 50 kWe Hall thrusters and five low pressure xenon flow controllers (Fig. 9). 





Fig. 9 SEP Module in SLS Fairing





The final layout was one of many configurations traded. While a ‘farm’ of smaller arrays, such those employed on ISS, would allow for the use of nearer term arrays, packaging such structural trusses (along with the mass) would not allow for launching in a single SLS and would require on-orbit assembly just like ISS. Mass would also be sub-optimal. Neither packaging nor mass were major concerns for the ISS space station since it was built using many shuttle flights. For this Mars transfer vehicle mass and packaging were both major concerns. Thus, a two, large deployable solar array configuration was chosen. Additional design constraints were the chemical stage loads of ~ 0.1g on the arrays as well as the vehicle dynamic loads that occur during chemical stage startup/shutoff, other element dockings, and slewing the vehicle for sun/thruster pointing. 


Fortunately, a recent study by Mikulas developed a 500 kW array wing with a first modal frequency around 0.1Hz. [7] This is similar to those of the ISS which is representative of a large space vehicle like the SEP module. These compact telescoping arrays (CTAs) have an excellent packaging efficiency at 60 kW/m3 or greater. The SEP module arrays were based on these CTAs and initial, independent finite element analyses showed a first modal frequency of 0.11 Hz which matches the Mikulas study sufficiently for a conceptual design. In addition, the array design could withstand the chemical stage’s thrusting at 0.1g with sufficient margin, but buckling should be checked in further analyses. Recent work by Northrup Grumman Innovation Systems have built the CTA systems, albeit at smaller sizes [8]. Alternative designs for large, easily stowable, solar arrays are many including the MegaFlex. These should be considered in future trades. 


While the arrays can provide 1 MW at Earth orbit distance, the EP system is sized for only ~400 kW power consumption for the mission. The enlargement of the solar power system is made so that, with the increased distance from the sun and the reduction in solar flux (1/R2), the 1 MW array at Earth can still supply ~400kW when the SEP-Chem vehicle reaches Mars. These requirements are aligned with the broader mission objectives that encompass a 15-year operational lifespan, equivalent to three separate Mars missions. The mission architecture is structured around a two-year period for launch to NRHO assembly and intervals between missions. The operational timeline is geared towards the 2039 mission timeframe.


The power management and distribution system includes a primary high-voltage bus as well as a secondary 120 VDC bus (Fig. 10). The high-voltage bus is powered by a solar array shunt regulator and utilizes a variable bus voltage, ranging from 330 VDC at Earth up to 400 VDC as the vehicle approaches Mars. This variable voltage design maximizes the power at Mars by increasing the voltage as the solar cells get colder (gain additional voltage capability) near Mars. The high-voltage bus directly powers the EP system through DDUs to maximize power system efficiency. Additionally, the high-voltage bus feeds power into the 120 VDC bus through DC-to-DC converter units that transfer power to both the habitat and the chemical stages. When solar array power is not available (such as in eclipse), the 120 VDC bus utilizes the energy storage system through a battery charge/discharge regulator. However, the DC-to-DC converter units are not bi-directional, so the energy storage cannot be used to provide power to the high-voltage bus (thus, no EP thrusting in shadow). A single-fault tolerant architecture is implemented to enhance system reliability.


The energy storage system is designed to provide power during mission phases where solar array power is not available. A 6-hour energy reserve is allotted from launch to solar array deployment. During the mission, the system is designed for a maximum eclipse time of 90 minutes while in NRHO, and a slightly extended 110-minute eclipse period during Mars operations. Additionally, it was assumed that the batteries could support chemical burns lasting up to 30 minutes as each of these burn scenarios may not allow the solar arrays to be fully pointed at the sun.


The solar array design utilizes next generation, high-efficiency solar cells which are expected to be in production in the late 2030s. The IMM-β solar cells in development by SolAero by Rocketlab were chosen for this design and provide 36 percent efficiency near Earth, in standard conditions (Beginning of Life (BOL), 28°C, 1 AU). The solar cells are arranged in a string of 95 in series to produce the 330 VDC near Earth and 400 VDC near Mars. Each string of cells is combined in parallel to produce the 400+ kW required for the power system. A total of 1,000 strings are included per wing, organized into 8 parallel strings per circuit, aggregating into 125 circuits for each wing which power the solar array shunt regulator. The solar array is designed to meet power requirements at the minimum solar flux at both Earth (1.017 AU) and Mars (1.666 AU) distances. Near Earth, the solar array performance is projected to be 916.6 kW at End of Life (EOL) conditions (70°C, 1.017 AU) and 971.4 kW at BOL conditions (60°C, 1.017 AU). Near Mars, the projected solar array performance is 400.7 kW at EOL (5°C, 1.666 AU).


When deployed, each wing boasts an area of 1637.1 m2 with 1235.0 m2 of active solar cell area, equating to a packing efficiency of about 75 percent. Each solar array string is designed and built as a modular unit called a solar power module (SPM), which consists of 19 solar cells across the width and 5 rows along the length. Within the solar array wing, a total of two blankets are integrated, positioned on either side of the main truss. Each blanket accommodates four SPMs aligned across the width (per row) with a total of 125 rows of SPMs along the length of the wing. The z-folding pattern aligns along each row of SPMs, stacking all 125 SPMs when stowed. The specific power of the entire wing is estimated to be around 108 W/kg, encompassing the solar cells and electrical components, structural aspects, mechanisms, and harnessing. 


Such a large ~80 m solar array will be a challenge to test on the ground. Even the Space Simulation Chamber at NASA Armstrong Test Facility is limited to a 30 m long CTA. Thus, it would be challenging to qualify the solar array design as no vacuum facility exists which would allow for a full-scale qualification test. Instead, the qualification approach could leverage sub-scale testing of components or sub-scale blanket sizes or utilize a technology demonstration mission for a full-scale deployment. As for acceptance testing of the flight wings, it is preferred to fully deploy the wings on the ground, prior to launch. This allows for verification of the deployment mechanisms through the deployment sequence as well as the opportunity to inspect and test the solar cells on the wing. Fully deploying the wing requires a 20+ m tall by 82+ m long clean room facility (not including deployment off-loader ground support equipment) but not necessarily a vacuum facility. Such a non-vacuum facility should be affordable and could be constructed to handle both engineering and acceptance testing. 





Fig. 10 SEP Module Electrical Power System Schematic


The EP system is designed around 50 kWe xenon Hall thrusters that are mounted on gimballed pallets located at the end of the bus as shown in Fig. 11. These pallets are oriented so that the Hall thruster plumes are projected away from the rest of the vehicle and the solar arrays. The 500 kW arrays layout made this simpler than ‘farms’ of smaller arrays. There are ten Hall thrusters in total, five on each pallet, that are in an 8+2 configuration. These thrusters are magnetically shielded, have a nominal Isp of 2,380 s at 400 V with xenon propellant, and are a single channel design with a center mounted cathode. The thrusters have an estimated propellant throughput capacity of 15 t, which equates to approximately 34,000 hours of operation; this should allow them to perform three Mars missions. These thrusters are based on previous high-power and long life designs that have already been developed, such as the NASA 457 Hall [9] and the advanced electric propulsion system thrusters [11].


The DDU serves as a crucial component in the system, receiving high voltage power directly from the array regulator and power distribution unit and having an approximate efficiency of 99 percent. The DDUs are further equipped with an integrated digital control interface unit (DCIU) that interfaces with the propellant flow controller. 





Fig. 11 SEP Module Propulsion Systems


The xenon propellant for the Hall thrusters is stored in a single high pressure, door-knob shaped tank. The tank is a composite overwrapped pressure vessel with a mounting flange at the equator. The tank has a titanium alloy liner and T-1000 overwrap to withstand the 7.6 MPa on the tank walls at 300 K. It is sized to hold all of the xenon required by the Hall thrusters during the mission, which is estimated to be 50 t. 


Chemical Stage Design


The final element is the chemical stage, which can be topped off and refueled on-orbit for the 2039 mission. The chemical stage is used for the gravity well maneuvers that must be completed within a narrow time window, and thus require a much higher thrust level than delivered by the EP system. Due to the large propellant load, the SLS was chosen as a representative way to launch the chemical stage for the mission. The stage will be fueled using the NRHO tanker. For some easier opposition and conjunction missions, the stage might be oversized, and smaller stages could be used. This suggests that using multiple stages for even the 2039 opposition mission is possible but integrating those stages was outside the scope of this study. In addition, given the commercial capabilities of chemical stages, it is recommended that other chemical stage configurations be explored.


The chemical stage is a long duration, dockable, free flying LOx/LCH4 stage that provides the high thrust burns both at Earth and Mars. It also provides sufficient cryocooler systems to eliminate propellant boiloff and is refuellable. The bus structure for the chemical stage is comprised of a long cylindrical tube with a conical section and endcap at one end that reduces the diameter down to the interface diameter of the payload launch adaptor, and another conical section and endcap at the other end that acts as the thruster structure for the main chemical thrusters. The diameter of the bus structure was driven by the 5 m diameter of the chemical propellant tanks contained inside. A common bulkhead tank design was utilized to ensure the tanks could carry the required propellant loading while allowing the chemical stage to fit within the payload fairing. 


The thermal control for the LOx/LCH4 cryogenic system is a major aspect of the overall thermal design for the chemical stage. Active, in-space cryogenic propellant storage with cryocoolers to maintain zero-boiloff is a newer technology that is still under development. The heat leak into the tanks must be removed to maintain the propellant in its cryogenic state over long durations. This heat removal is accomplished through a broad area cooling system consisting of cryocoolers and a coolant loop that surrounds the tanks. The cryocoolers cool a neon gas to the desired operating temperature of the tank. A pump is used to move this coolant through tubes that surround the tank. Outside of this broad area cooling system is a combined multilayer insulation/ micrometeoroid protection system which minimizes the heat leak into the tank.

NRHO Chem/Xe Tanker Element Design





The selection of the NRHO for the SEP-Chem Mars vehicle assembly and fueling necessitates the deployment of tankers designed to accommodate both cryogenic propellants and xenon. While many options exist to provide these propellants, including commercial delivery, a representative tanker was designed to evaluate what the size and mass of such a vehicle. Opting for a superheavy, instead of just a heavy, launcher proves to be a far more suitable choice to meet the requirements of the mission, given that it can efficiently handle the substantial quantity of propellants required.





Fig. 13 NRHO Chem/Xe Tanker Element


One simplified approach involves building upon the planned 200 ton chemical stage for the Mars vehicle but modifying it to carry both xenon and LOx/LCH4 propellants. This involves shortening the chemical stage's design to allow it to fit within an 8-meter long, super-heavy fairing. This approach brings about cost-saving benefits through commonality.


In this proposed configuration, the primary tanks of the tanker can deliver around 25 tons of cryogenic propellants to the chemical stage located in the NRHO. Alternatively, there exists an option to integrate an additional xenon tank that can provide approximately 24 tons of xenon propellant to the SEP Module. Moreover, the flexibility of the design allows for the combination of cryogenic propellants and xenon in varying proportions. This combination can then be used to top off either the chemical stage or the SEP Module, as necessitated by the specific mission requirements.

Krypton Option


While xenon is currently the electrostatic thruster propellant of choice due to its high efficiency and easy supercritical, albeit high pressure, storage its production is currently tied to liquid oxygen production for steel production [12]. Recent world events have impacted production so a look at krypton as an alternative propellant is worthwhile. Krypton is produced at almost 10X the rate making it more available and correspondingly cheaper. A ‘one-off’ design of the SEP-Chem vehicle replacing xenon with krypton as the chosen thruster propellant was executed to see how it impacts the design. The investigation included tankage configurations, propulsion systems, and supplementary hardware.


The chosen size of the EP systems remains unaltered throughout the trade-off evaluation. This includes the utilization of 8+2, 50kW Hall thrusters operational at ~400V. When utilizing krypton as the propellant, these thrusters had a slightly increased specific impulse (Isp) of 2,688 seconds and a slightly reduced efficiency of 56.8 percent [10]. The configuration retains the consistent use of DDUs featuring integrated DCIUs. A single flow control module is designated for each thruster string, with no implementation of cross-strapping among different thruster strings.


When considering gas phase krypton storage, the implementation necessitates extremely high pressure, resulting in a significant increase in tank mass. For the same sized tank as the xenon storage system (which stores almost 52 tons of propellant at a pressure of 1200 psia,) the krypton tank would need have a pressure of over 7000 psia and weigh almost 10 tons to carry the same 52 tons of krypton propellant.

The utilization of liquid krypton storage offers advantages such as a smaller tank with low pressure and weight. The incorporation of liquid krypton as a propellant necessitates the introduction of supplementary hardware. This hardware encompasses slosh baffles to manage propellant motion, a propellant management device for maintaining proper flow, low thermal conductivity struts to reduce heat leaks, and a comprehensive thermal control apparatus. The thermal control hardware includes cryocoolers, broad area cooling, thermal vent systems, and other relevant components. The resulting design using liquid storage required a slightly smaller and lighter tank than the xenon case but required a storage temperature of 120 K along with the cryogenic storage equipment and power (see Table 3.). SEP-Chem vs NEP-Chem


After the Mars Transportation Assessment Study [1] which focused on two year missions the NASA Mars Architecture Team extended the trip time limit to 850 days. This longer trip time allows reducing the NEP power level from 1.9 MW to ~750 kW. A new NEP-Chem design was developed. Fig. 14 shows its main attributes. The architecture for this 750 kW NEP-Chem system was similar to the 1 MW SEP-Chem since it also started in the NRHO. The lower NEP power requirement is due to the fact that nuclear power is continuous while the solar power needs additional solar array for the reduced solar flux at Mars. The power system for NEP is smaller in terms of size but does weigh more.


Fig. 14 compares the main attributes of each concept. While both systems have similar inert masses, the SEP-Chem requires about 33 percent more propellants and thus an additional two tankers. Therefore, the starting mass at trans-Mars-injection is about 18 percent larger for the SEP-Chem system. As with other technology options (such as, nuclear thermal propulsion) whether SEP-Chem is chosen will depend upon the relative technology costs and challenges between the power and propulsion technologies.

 Conclusions 


The proposed engineering approach involves the integration of a 1 MWe SEP system with a LOx/LCH4 chemical stage to enable the realization of an 850-day piloted opposition Mars mission. The strategy aims to keep the LDHEO starting mass below 300 tons, a critical parameter for mission competitiveness with nuclear thermal and nuclear electric vehicle approaches. The launch requirements entail utilizing two Space Launch System (SLS) launches and seven super heavy launchers before the 2039 launch opportunity. The mission's adaptability for various scenarios is achieved by varying chemical propellant quantities. Both the SEP and chemical systems are designed with reusability in mind, targeting up to three mission cycles each. Incorporating an NRHO assembly orbit offers the advantages of removing several year spiral times and solar array degradation. Furthermore, a novel super heavy tanker, leveraging the 200 ton class chemical stage design, has been conceptualized for refueling purposes. It is estimated to accommodate 25 tons of LOx/LCH4 or alternatively 24 tons of xenon propellant. Such a tanker would be used to refuel the SEP-Chem system for repeat missions. This tanker is representative; other options exist to fuel up the vehicle including SEP tugs. Use of krypton in lieu of xenon is possible for similar vehicle sizes and mass performance but would require using a cryogenic krypton storage system on the SEP module.


The 1 MWe SEP-Chem design provides a non-nuclear alternative for transporting crew to Mars and back in 850 days. Compared to the 750 kW NEP-Chem option the SEP-Chem requires two more propellant tankers and weighs about 18 percent more at departure. The 1 MW SEP-Chem key technology will be the very large solar arrays, which will be challenging to develop and test, but could be easier than developing and testing nuclear electric or nuclear thermal technologies. When applying the same SEP-Chem vehicle to different missions, it will be arguably easier to change the chemical stage propellant load rather than changing the power level of the EP system. For conjunction missions, the chemical stage could be much smaller, and for non-time-critical cargo missions, the chemical stage could be eliminated altogether.





Next Steps 


More detailed evaluations are required to mature this concept. First a comprehensive assessment of the standalone 1 MW SEP system's capabilities, focusing on its potential to facilitate the transportation of as many as three landers or alternative architectures to Mars should be performed. Simultaneously, an in-depth analysis of the spacecraft's dynamic response should be undertaken, particularly in relation to the behavior of the large solar arrays during docking and thrusting maneuvers, with the objective of uncovering potential structural stresses and disturbances.


Furthermore, the scope should encompass a thorough investigation into various solar array technologies, including options like Mega-ROSA, to determine their suitability for the mission's unique demands. This exploration would identify and optimize designs that align with the evolving requirements of a Mars architecture. A significant portion of effort must be directed towards options for the development and testing of a 500 kW solar array system. This initiative will include a planned program to validate the system's performance and feasibility under deep space conditions, thereby establishing a critical benchmark for assessing solar-based power generation in contrast to nuclear propulsion alternatives.


Together, these efforts would provide a necessary starting assessment for the use of SEP-Chem for an 850 day Mars mission.
