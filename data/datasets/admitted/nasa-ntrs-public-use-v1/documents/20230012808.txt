Thermocouple Basics



• Bi-metallic joint of specific known materials
• Differential voltage created; Peltier effect 



• Multiple different types (J, K, T, E, etc)
• Each type has an ideal temperature performance range



• NIST standard tables for
differential voltage vs. temp



• “Cold Junction” reference temperature
• Usually, room temperature or ice water



• Order of magnitude: mV
• Measure induced voltage change
• Interpolate b/w table











Thermocouples



• NASA has extensive experience using thermocouples in cryogenic 
systems:



• Best used for systems above 70 – 80 K.
• Can perform end to end system calibration for temperatures below 70 K.
• Best if used with some length of wire along sensor as close to isothermal as 



possible.
• Generally, Type E is most common used – has best sensitivity at 



temperatures below ~ 100 K. 
• Type T and Type K can also work.



• Published uncertainty ~ +/- 1.6 K + electronics. In practice, we get better than 
that all the time.



• Typically potted on using Stycast 2850 FT or attached via taping.



• Commonly available NIST standard curves make T/Cs cheap and 
easy to use.



• Can buy 500 ft spool and spot welder to make yourself as opposed to buying 
premade thermocouples.











What is a Diode?



A diode only allows one-way current flow:
• Silicon type diodes:



• N-type (negative charge) and P-type (positive charge) silicon.



• Created by doping silicon with various elements.



• Joined together so free electrons from N-type flow into P-type to allow current 
flow if battery oriented correctly.



• If battery oriented incorrectly, free electrons attracted the wrong way and no signal.



• The voltage of the diode with constant current is a function of it’s 
temperature.



• Requires excitation current (~ 10 mA).



• Costs several hundreds of dollars per sensor or greater.
• Costs vary based on quantity ordered.











What is an RTD



• RTD = Resistance Temperature Detector:
• Very simple: measure the electrical resistance of a metallic element 



which has well characterized changes as a function of temperature.



• Two types: positive temperature coefficient and negative 
temperature coefficient (slope of temperature response line).



• Positive Temperature Coefficient:
• Typically made using platinum.



• Available in resistances between 1 Ohm and 1000 Ohms (usually not less than 
100 Ohms).



• Can be very cheap (< $10 per sensor).



• Negative Temperature Coefficent:
• Germanium & Cernox



• No standard curve, each sensor requires calibration.



• More accurate, especially below 77 K.



• Several hundred dollars per sensor.











Calibration



• Silicon diodes available with and without calibration 
• Uncalibrated uncertainty larger (+/- 0.25 K to +/- 1 K) depending on 



temperature and quality of sensor (can specify), use standard curve



• Calibrated diode (sensor specific curve) as good as +/- 20 mK



• Cernox®



• Requires sensor specific calibration (Chebechev)



• Platinum RTD
• Generally, two test points: LN2 and room temperature



(+/- 0.25 K)
• Sensitivity of 0.00385 deg/K



• Sensor specific calibration (< +/- 0.1 K)

RTD Calibration



RTDs are often calibrated via 2-point 
temperature measurement (ice bath and LN2) 
and then linear in between.



Can use IEC 60751 platinum curves if desired.
• Make sure to use curve for -200˚C to 0˚C.
• Not analytically reversable with extra C term.
• Solve for Rt/Ro from a measured voltage.
• Then develop equation to reverse solve for 



temperature based on Rt/Ro ratio.
• Used successfully during CryoFILL testing.











Sensors in External Environments



• Imposed magnetic fields can offset the diode response:
• Typically Cernox/RTDs more stable.



• Radiation can alter the output of diodes and RTDs:
• Typically Cernox more stable.



• Duration can also cause a slow drift in output:
• Typically takes years to see.











RTD, Silicon Diodes, and Cernox®



• NASA has extensive experience using silicon diodes from Scientific 
Instruments and Lakeshore as well as Cernox® sensors from 
Lakeshore.



• Used as both temperature sensors and wet/dry sensors in cryogenic fluids.



• Mounted externally to fluid tubes and internally with several different 
configurations



• Multiple different wiring schemes
• 2 wire



• 3 wire



• 4 wire



• Require current excitation and measure:
• Voltage for silicon diodes.



• Resistance for Cernox®











Silicon Diodes and Cernox® as Wet/Dry Sensors



• NASA GRC discovered the ability to use diodes as a wet/dry sensor.
• Dempsey and Fabik, “Using Silicon Diodes for Detecting the Liquid-Vapor Interface in Hydrogen”, NASA TM-



105541.



• Metzger and Zimmerli (NASA GRC) determined that Cernox® had a 6 – 15 x larger response than the silicon 
diodes (lower values for CX-1080 with LN2).



• Normally chip sensors are used for this implementation.



• Normally, a diode (or Cernox ®) receives an excitation current or voltage:
• Silicon Diodes: 10 mA
• Cernox ®: <10 mV



• By increasing the voltage or current across the sensor, it causes the sensor to self-heat more.
• In a liquid that self heating is easily removed through convection, the measured response does not change.



• In a vapor the self heating causes the diode to respond as if the fluid is warmer and the voltage response 
changes.



• Based on the response to self heating, logic can be applied to the DAQ to determine if the sensor is wet or dry.
• This also requires thermal isolation from the temperature rake or any other thermal mass.



• On SHIIVER, cylinder (“can”) style SI diodes were used and this did not work for a few reasons.
• The “can” had more thermal mass than the normally used chip configurations and took longer to “overheat”



• The sensors were more closely coupled to the perforated board (tied down with wire) to prevent damage during 
installation and filling of the tank.

Epoxy/Varnish/Attachments



• SMiRF uses Stycast 2850 FT for epoxying sensors in place.
• The Stycast is thermally conductive, but not electrically conductive.



• Often use “band-aids” to hold sensor in place while epoxy is 
drying.



• ARC and GRC use GE varnish (now IMI 7031TM) if sensors are 
to be “resusable” or relocatable.

Silicon Diodes in Flow



• There are multiple different ways to get a temperature sensor 
into an internal flow:



• Probes available from Lakeshore (or made in house).



• GRC SMiRF internal diode transition.



• GRC SHIIVER internal diode mounting.



• These mounting techniques would work with Cernox® or RTDs 
as well as Silicon Diodes.











Probes



• Probes can be procured or manufactured to get temperature sensors 
into a flow stream.



• Probes typically consist of a tube with one end welded shut.



• The temperature sensor is inserted down the tube with some sort of 
heat sink at the bottom.



• Some sort of cryogenic goop/epoxy/grease to bridge any thermal gaps 
between tube and heat sink.



• Has been known to break thermal contact in right conditions.



• Appropriate design accounts for conduction down the tube and tube 
response to flow.



• Maximize immersion of probe while leaving minimal hot side probe exposure.
• Use external insulation of probe (loosely fit MLI). Ideally hot side of probe 



approaches internal fluid temperature.
• If you need to measure dT, ensure the probe configurations are the same for 



each Temp measurement. Perhaps consider flow entrance condition 
similarity (ex: leave X diameters of straight run prior to measurement), much 
like flow meters.











SMiRF internal transition



• Developed by Helmut Bamberger at SMiRF for various testing 
activities there.



• Uses two wire Ceramtec feedthrough and
VCR gland to attach in a VCR tee.



• Sensor may not be fully immersed in flow
due to length of Ceramtec feedthrough.



Credit Helmut Bamberger, NASA GRC 











SHIIVER Internal Transition



• Developed by Keith Johnson in conjunction with GRC 
instrumentation shop to support SHIIVER testing.



• Similar to probes, but doesn’t weld tip closed.
• Epoxy used to isolate diodes from tube wall.



• Tried just sealing tube off at top and bottom with potting 
compound. Had some leaks over time, eventually filled
whole tube.



• Uses tubing to support diodes against internal 
flow forces



• Use SI can type diodes
• Single sensor in 1/8th inch tube
• Dual sensors in ¼ inch tube
• Goes into piping via swage fitting

Different Wire Measurement Options



• A standard diode/RTD/Cernox® requires excitation.



• The excitation wires can be used to measure response in a two wire 
system:



• Depending on the wire length (i.e. resistance), this can lead to large offsets in 
readings.



• Different types of wire (copper or phosphor bronze) can be used to adjust offsets.



• This is usually corrected for by measuring response with two separate 
wires, hence the four wire system.



• GRC does have experience wiring up to six diodes in series for excitation.
• This is the three wire configuration



• This also minimizes the number of current sources required if current sources are 
used separately from monitoring response.



• If one diode is lost, then all diodes on that string are lost (not appropriate for 
measurement redundancy).



• There may also be some accuracy losses with this method.











Sensor Placement for Conduction Heat Loads



• Based on the uncertainty of sensors and the positioning of the 
sensors, the uncertainty of a measurement can be estimated 
and sensitivities used to drive sensor location.



• Does not take into account radiation between the environment and the 
structure being measured – will push sensors closer together.

Uncertainty Example



Based on the uncertainty in differential 
temperature (UT) measurement (1% 
uncertainty in cross-sectional area and 2% 
uncertainty in thermal conductivity) and 
differential distance (dx) between the sensors, 
the conduction heat load can be minimized.



In this case (SHIIVER), the ideal distance was 
about 0.2 m (8 inches). Placed series of three 
sensors, each 4 inches apart – required 2 of 
the 3 sensors to work to enable calculation.



The uncertainty for heat load with actual 
temperature sensor uncertainty was observed 
to not decrease much below 2.5%, thus the 
standard uncertainty of +/- 0.25 K at 20 K and 
+/- 1 K above 100 K was acceptable.











Pressure Measurements



• For testing, we typically use pressure measurements that are 
thermally isolated from the experiment:



• By long sense tube, even outside the vacuum chamber.



• Need to be wary of Thermo-Acoustic Oscillations.
• Avoid simplest by plumbing redundant sense lines together.



• There have been some developments in cold pressure 
transducers.



• Results still have to be corrected for temperature.



• Typically, more for spaceflight type applications.











Fill Level Measurements (1/2)



• Load Cells
• Direct measurement of system weight preferred method of determining fill 



level.



• Can be tricky with liquid hydrogen because usually system mass much 
greater than differential hydrogen mass.



• Temperature Rakes
• Use in Wet/Dry mode if at all possible (see earlier slides).



• Trying to determine via temperature response is not very accurate, though 
can work in a pinch.



• Capacitance Probes
• Need to recalibrate for different fluids.



• Make sure you know where top of liquid is when you calibrate.



• Make sure that tank wet percentage as a function of height is known in the 
cold, shrunk, pressurized configuration!











Fill Level Measurement (2/2)



• Delta Pressure Transducers:
• Make sure that tank wet percentage as a function of height is known in 



the cold, shrunk, pressurized configuration!



• Use p-traps to prevent liquid ingestion into external lines. This can act 
as an additional heat source on your tank and mess up the 
measurement.



• Avoid thermoacoustic oscillations with a warm sensor.



• Radio Frequency Mass Gauge
• Developed by Dr. Greg Zimmerli at GRC.



• Disperses RF signal in tank and measures response looking for modes 
within tank.



• Compares measured RF response to predicted model response.











Flow Measurements



• Typically for boil-off measurements, use thermal mass flow 
meters (based on thermal anemometry).



• Make sure to calibrate with correct gas or use conversion constant.



• For SHIIVER (and CryoFILL) used Coriolis flow meters
• Not best for low density gas (such as hydrogen).



• Used to minimize pressure drop in the flow meter.



• Requires special care and integration to make work.



• If using where may see temperature below 100 K make sure to inform 
vendor so they purge cavity with helium not nitrogen gas.



• Make sure appropriate line lengths before and after flow 
measurement to enable correct measurement.











Summary



• An array of different temperature sensors can be used 
effectively in liquid hydrogen systems.



• Can measure hardware or fluid temperatures within a system.



• Experience developed at NASA GRC over the past 60+ years of 
liquid hydrogen system testing has been shared to help the 
general audience with their measurement needs.

learned over the past 60 years and not solely of the author or 
presenters.
