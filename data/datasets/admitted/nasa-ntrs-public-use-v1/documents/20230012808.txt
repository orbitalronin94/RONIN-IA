<p>Temperature Sensors Introduction
</p>
<p>• Sensor types for discussion:
• Thermocouples
• Silicone Diodes
• Cernox
• RTDs
</p>
<p>• Some considerations for sensor selection:
• Allowable sensor uncertainty
• Lifecycle of measurement device:
</p>
<p>• Reusability
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

measure
</p>

make the measurement (i.e. radiation, 
vacuum)?
</p>

solid?
</p>

<p>Thermocouple Basics
</p>
<p>• Bi-metallic joint of specific known materials
• Differential voltage created; Peltier effect 
</p>
<p>• Multiple different types (J, K, T, E, etc)
• Each type has an ideal temperature performance range
</p>
<p>• NIST standard tables for
differential voltage vs. temp
</p>
<p>• “Cold Junction” reference temperature
• Usually, room temperature or ice water
</p>
<p>• Order of magnitude: mV
• Measure induced voltage change
• Interpolate b/w table</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Thermocouples
</p>
<p>• NASA has extensive experience using thermocouples in cryogenic 
systems:
</p>
<p>• Best used for systems above 70 – 80 K.
• Can perform end to end system calibration for temperatures below 70 K.
• Best if used with some length of wire along sensor as close to isothermal as 
</p>
<p>possible.
• Generally, Type E is most common used – has best sensitivity at 
</p>
<p>temperatures below ~ 100 K. 
• Type T and Type K can also work.
</p>
<p>• Published uncertainty ~ +/- 1.6 K + electronics.  In practice, we get better than 
that all the time.
</p>
<p>• Typically potted on using Stycast 2850 FT or attached via taping.
</p>
<p>• Commonly available NIST standard curves make T/Cs cheap and 
easy to use.
</p>
<p>• Can buy 500 ft spool and spot welder to make yourself as opposed to buying 
premade thermocouples.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>What is a Diode?
</p>
<p>A diode only allows one-way current flow:
• Silicon type diodes:
</p>
<p>• N-type (negative charge) and P-type (positive charge) silicon.
</p>
<p>• Created by doping silicon with various elements.
</p>
<p>• Joined together so free electrons from N-type flow into P-type to allow current 
flow if battery oriented correctly.
</p>
<p>• If battery oriented incorrectly, free electrons attracted the wrong way and no signal.
</p>
<p>• The voltage of the diode with constant current is a function of it’s 
temperature.
</p>
<p>• Requires excitation current (~ 10 mA).
</p>
<p>• Costs several hundreds of dollars per sensor or greater.
• Costs vary based on quantity ordered.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>What is an RTD
</p>
<p>• RTD = Resistance Temperature Detector:
• Very simple: measure the electrical resistance of a metallic element 
</p>
<p>which has well characterized changes as a function of temperature.
</p>
<p>• Two types: positive temperature coefficient and negative 
temperature coefficient (slope of temperature response line).
</p>
<p>• Positive Temperature Coefficient:
• Typically made using platinum.
</p>
<p>• Available in resistances between 1 Ohm and 1000 Ohms (usually not less than 
100 Ohms).
</p>
<p>• Can be very cheap (&lt; $10 per sensor).
</p>
<p>• Negative Temperature Coefficent:
• Germanium &amp; Cernox
</p>
<p>• No standard curve, each sensor requires calibration.
</p>
<p>• More accurate, especially below 77 K.
</p>
<p>• Several hundred dollars per sensor.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Calibration
</p>
<p>• Silicon diodes available with and without calibration 
• Uncalibrated uncertainty larger (+/- 0.25 K to +/- 1 K) depending on 
</p>
<p>temperature and quality of sensor (can specify), use standard curve
</p>
<p>• Calibrated diode (sensor specific curve) as good as +/- 20 mK
</p>
<p>• Cernox®
</p>
<p>• Requires sensor specific calibration (Chebechev)
</p>
<p>• Platinum RTD
• Generally, two test points: LN2 and room temperature
</p>
<p>(+/- 0.25 K)
• Sensitivity of 0.00385 deg/K
</p>
<p>• Sensor specific calibration (&lt; +/- 0.1 K)
</p>

<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>RTD Calibration
</p>
<p>RTDs are often calibrated via 2-point 
temperature measurement (ice bath and LN2) 
and then linear in between.
</p>
<p>Can use IEC 60751 platinum curves if desired.
• Make sure to use curve for -200˚C to 0˚C.
• Not analytically reversable with extra C term.
• Solve for Rt/Ro from a measured voltage.
• Then develop equation to reverse solve for 
</p>
<p>temperature based on Rt/Ro ratio.
• Used successfully during CryoFILL testing.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Sensors in External Environments
</p>
<p>• Imposed magnetic fields can offset the diode response:
• Typically Cernox/RTDs more stable.
</p>
<p>• Radiation can alter the output of diodes and RTDs:
• Typically Cernox more stable.
</p>
<p>• Duration can also cause a slow drift in output:
• Typically takes years to see.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>RTD, Silicon Diodes, and Cernox®
</p>
<p>• NASA has extensive experience using silicon diodes from Scientific 
Instruments and Lakeshore as well as Cernox® sensors from 
Lakeshore.
</p>
<p>• Used as both temperature sensors and wet/dry sensors in cryogenic fluids.
</p>
<p>• Mounted externally to fluid tubes and internally with several different 
configurations
</p>
<p>• Multiple different wiring schemes
• 2 wire
</p>
<p>• 3 wire
</p>
<p>• 4 wire
</p>
<p>• Require current excitation and measure:
• Voltage for silicon diodes.
</p>
<p>• Resistance for Cernox®</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Silicon Diodes and Cernox® as Wet/Dry Sensors
</p>
<p>• NASA GRC discovered the ability to use diodes as a wet/dry sensor.
• Dempsey and Fabik, “Using Silicon Diodes for Detecting the Liquid-Vapor Interface in Hydrogen”, NASA TM-
</p>
<p>105541.
</p>
<p>• Metzger and Zimmerli (NASA GRC) determined that Cernox® had a 6 – 15 x larger response than the silicon 
diodes (lower values for CX-1080 with LN2).
</p>
<p>• Normally chip sensors are used for this implementation.
</p>
<p>• Normally, a diode (or Cernox ®) receives an excitation current or voltage:
• Silicon Diodes: 10 mA
• Cernox ®: &lt;10 mV
</p>
<p>• By increasing the voltage or current across the sensor, it causes the sensor to self-heat more.
• In a liquid that self heating is easily removed through convection, the measured response does not change.
</p>
<p>• In a vapor the self heating causes the diode to respond as if the fluid is warmer and the voltage response 
changes.
</p>
<p>• Based on the response to self heating, logic can be applied to the DAQ to determine if the sensor is wet or dry.
• This also requires thermal isolation from the temperature rake or any other thermal mass.
</p>
<p>• On SHIIVER, cylinder (“can”) style SI diodes were used and this did not work for a few reasons.
• The “can” had more thermal mass than the normally used chip configurations and took longer to “overheat”
</p>
<p>• The sensors were more closely coupled to the perforated board (tied down with wire) to prevent damage during 
installation and filling of the tank.</p>
<p></p>

<p>Epoxy/Varnish/Attachments
</p>
<p>• SMiRF uses Stycast 2850 FT for epoxying sensors in place.
• The Stycast is thermally conductive, but not electrically conductive.
</p>
<p>• Often use “band-aids” to hold sensor in place while epoxy is 
drying.
</p>
<p>• ARC and GRC use GE varnish (now IMI 7031TM) if sensors are 
to be “resusable” or relocatable.</p>

<p>Temperature Sensors in Tank
</p>
<p>• GRC often uses temperature sensors in the tank, exposed to 
liquid hydrogen.
</p>
<p>• Mounted on epoxy perforated board that allows sensor to “hang out” 
into the liquid that surrounds it.
</p>
<p>• Image below shows Si diode (SD style) mounted on perf-board.
</p>
<p>• Wires run through board and then out through 20 K certified connector 
(CeramTec or equivalent).
</p>
<p>• Sensors used to measure stratification within the liquid of a tank 
and not the metal they are mounted on.</p>

<p>Silicon Diodes in Flow
</p>
<p>• There are multiple different ways to get a temperature sensor 
into an internal flow:
</p>
<p>• Probes available from Lakeshore (or made in house).
</p>
<p>• GRC SMiRF internal diode transition.
</p>
<p>• GRC SHIIVER internal diode mounting.
</p>
<p>• These mounting techniques would work with Cernox® or RTDs 
as well as Silicon Diodes.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Probes
</p>
<p>• Probes can be procured or manufactured to get temperature sensors 
into a flow stream.
</p>
<p>• Probes typically consist of a tube with one end welded shut.
</p>
<p>• The temperature sensor is inserted down the tube with some sort of 
heat sink at the bottom.
</p>
<p>• Some sort of cryogenic goop/epoxy/grease to bridge any thermal gaps 
between tube and heat sink.
</p>
<p>• Has been known to break thermal contact in right conditions.
</p>
<p>• Appropriate design accounts for conduction down the tube and tube 
response to flow.
</p>
<p>• Maximize immersion of probe while leaving minimal hot side probe exposure.
• Use external insulation of probe (loosely fit MLI).  Ideally hot side of probe 
</p>
<p>approaches internal fluid temperature.
• If you need to measure dT, ensure the probe configurations are the same for 
</p>
<p>each Temp measurement.  Perhaps consider flow entrance condition 
similarity (ex: leave X diameters of straight run prior to measurement), much 
like flow meters.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>SMiRF internal transition
</p>
<p>• Developed by Helmut Bamberger at SMiRF for various testing 
activities there.
</p>
<p>• Uses two wire Ceramtec feedthrough and
VCR gland to attach in a VCR tee.
</p>
<p>• Sensor may not be fully immersed in flow
due to length of Ceramtec feedthrough.
</p>
<p>Credit Helmut Bamberger, NASA GRC </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>SHIIVER Internal Transition
</p>
<p>• Developed by Keith Johnson in conjunction with GRC 
instrumentation shop to support SHIIVER testing.
</p>
<p>• Similar to probes, but doesn’t weld tip closed.
• Epoxy used to isolate diodes from tube wall.
</p>
<p>• Tried just sealing tube off at top and bottom with potting 
compound.  Had some leaks over time, eventually filled
whole tube.
</p>
<p>• Uses tubing to support diodes against internal 
flow forces
</p>
<p>• Use SI can type diodes
• Single sensor in 1/8th inch tube
• Dual sensors in ¼ inch tube
• Goes into piping via swage fitting
</p>
<p>Temperature sensors (2x) in 
SHIIVER vent line.SHIIVER TP: NASA TP 20205008233 </p>
<p></p>

<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Different Wire Measurement Options
</p>
<p>• A standard diode/RTD/Cernox® requires excitation.
</p>
<p>• The excitation wires can be used to measure response in a two wire 
system:
</p>
<p>• Depending on the wire length (i.e. resistance), this can lead to large offsets in 
readings.
</p>
<p>• Different types of wire (copper or phosphor bronze) can be used to adjust offsets.
</p>
<p>• This is usually corrected for by measuring response with two separate 
wires, hence the four wire system.
</p>
<p>• GRC does have experience wiring up to six diodes in series for excitation.
• This is the three wire configuration
</p>
<p>• This also minimizes the number of current sources required if current sources are 
used separately from monitoring response.
</p>
<p>• If one diode is lost, then all diodes on that string are lost (not appropriate for 
measurement redundancy).
</p>
<p>• There may also be some accuracy losses with this method.</p>
<p></p>
</div>

sensors, the uncertainty of a measurement can be estimated

</p>
<p>• Does not take into account radiation between the environment and the 
structure being measured – will push sensors closer together.

<p>Uncertainty Example
</p>
<p>Based on the uncertainty in differential 
temperature (UT) measurement (1% 
uncertainty in cross-sectional area and 2% 
uncertainty in thermal conductivity) and 
differential distance (dx) between the sensors, 
the conduction heat load can be minimized.
</p>
<p>In this case (SHIIVER), the ideal distance was 
about 0.2 m (8 inches).  Placed series of three 
sensors, each 4 inches apart – required 2 of 
the 3 sensors to work to enable calculation.
</p>
<p>The uncertainty for heat load with actual 
temperature sensor uncertainty was observed 
to not decrease much below 2.5%, thus the 
standard uncertainty of +/- 0.25 K at 20 K and 
+/- 1 K above 100 K was acceptable.</p>
<p></p>

<p>Pressure Measurements
</p>
<p>• For testing, we typically use pressure measurements that are 
thermally isolated from the experiment:
</p>
<p>• By long sense tube, even outside the vacuum chamber.
</p>
<p>• Need to be wary of Thermo-Acoustic Oscillations.
• Avoid simplest by plumbing redundant sense lines together.
</p>
<p>• There have been some developments in cold pressure 
transducers.
</p>
<p>• Results still have to be corrected for temperature.
</p>
<p>• Typically, more for spaceflight type applications.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Fill Level Measurements (1/2)
</p>
<p>• Load Cells
• Direct measurement of system weight preferred method of determining fill 
</p>
<p>level.
</p>
<p>• Can be tricky with liquid hydrogen because usually system mass much 
greater than differential hydrogen mass.
</p>
<p>• Temperature Rakes
• Use in Wet/Dry mode if at all possible (see earlier slides).
</p>
<p>• Trying to determine via temperature response is not very accurate, though 
can work in a pinch.
</p>
<p>• Capacitance Probes
• Need to recalibrate for different fluids.
</p>
<p>• Make sure you know where top of liquid is when you calibrate.
</p>
<p>• Make sure that tank wet percentage as a function of height is known in the 
cold, shrunk, pressurized configuration!</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Fill Level Measurement (2/2)
</p>
<p>• Delta Pressure Transducers:
• Make sure that tank wet percentage as a function of height is known in 
</p>
<p>the cold, shrunk, pressurized configuration!
</p>
<p>• Use p-traps to prevent liquid ingestion into external lines. This can act 
as an additional heat source on your tank and mess up the 
measurement.
</p>
<p>• Avoid thermoacoustic oscillations with a warm sensor.
</p>
<p>• Radio Frequency Mass Gauge
• Developed by Dr. Greg Zimmerli at GRC.
</p>
<p>• Disperses RF signal in tank and measures response looking for modes 
within tank.
</p>
<p>• Compares measured RF response to predicted model response.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Flow Measurements
</p>
<p>• Typically for boil-off measurements, use thermal mass flow 
meters (based on thermal anemometry).
</p>
<p>• Make sure to calibrate with correct gas or use conversion constant.
</p>
<p>• For SHIIVER (and CryoFILL) used Coriolis flow meters
• Not best for low density gas (such as hydrogen).
</p>
<p>• Used to minimize pressure drop in the flow meter.
</p>
<p>• Requires special care and integration to make work.
</p>
<p>• If using where may see temperature below 100 K make sure to inform 
vendor so they purge cavity with helium not nitrogen gas.
</p>
<p>• Make sure appropriate line lengths before and after flow 
measurement to enable correct measurement.</p>
<p></p>

<p>Summary
</p>
<p>• An array of different temperature sensors can be used 
effectively in liquid hydrogen systems.
</p>
<p>• Can measure hardware or fluid temperatures within a system.
</p>
<p>• Experience developed at NASA GRC over the past 60+ years of 
liquid hydrogen system testing has been shared to help the 
general audience with their measurement needs.
</p>
<p>• This presentation is a cumulation of the developments and lessons 
learned over the past 60 years and not solely of the author or 
presenters.</p>
