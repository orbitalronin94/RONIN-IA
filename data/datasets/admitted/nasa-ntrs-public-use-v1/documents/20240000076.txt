Summary 
A feasibility analysis was performed for the operation of a wind turbine within the Venus surface 



environment. The Venus environment was modeled. This modeling included the gas properties at the 
surface (pressure, temperature, and viscosity) as well as estimates of the wind-speed probability over 
time. Using the environmental data, the wind turbine analysis examined basic operation of a wind turbine 
and determined the amount of torque and power that could be generated. A baseline wind turbine design 
was then made that determined the optimal tip-speed ratio and efficiency for the wind turbine. Also 
specified were the number of blades, airfoil, and sizing. The wind turbine sizing was constrained to fit and 
be deployable from a Long-Lived In-Situ Solar System Explorer (LLISSE) weather station probe. The 
analysis also examined the ability to start the wind turbine rotating on the Venus surface. The analysis 
determined that once rotating, the wind turbine could generate usable amounts of power for a low-power 
Venus lander. However, the ability to start the turbine is difficult, and the proposed vertical-axis design 
would not be capable of starting on its own or even with the augmentation of a Savonius rotor. Therefore, 
alternate means of starting the turbine, such as a starting motor, would need to be employed. In addition, 
alternate turbine designs can be considered, such as a horizontal-axis wind turbine, that have better 
starting characteristics and may be capable of starting under the Venus surface environmental conditions. 



1.0 Introduction 
A surface weather station probe operating on the surface of Venus can collect long-term 



environmental data providing great scientific insight into the surface conditions and characteristics of 
Venus. This is the basis for the development of the Long-Lived In-Situ Solar System Explorer (LLISSE) 
probe. For this type of probe to operate for extended periods of time (months to Earth years), it would 
either require a power source that has very long term operational capability, such as radioisotopes, or have 
the ability to be recharged from energy collected from the environment. For a small, low-cost, lightweight 
probe, the ability to recharge the power system is ideal. However, producing energy on the cloud-covered 
surface of Venus can be difficult. Current state-of-the-art solar cells do not function well under Venus 
surface conditions. The high operational temperature significantly reduces their efficiency, and the thick 
atmosphere and cloud cover obscures most of the sunlight. In addition, the atmosphere filters out most of 
the blue portion of the solar spectrum, which is the main portion of the spectrum that solar-cell types 
typically utilize to convert sunlight into electricity. The other potential source of environmental energy is 
the wind. There is little direct surface wind data, but what wind data does exist, both measured and 
inferred, indicates that the wind speeds are very low near the surface. However, because of Venus’s very 
dense atmosphere, usable amounts of energy can possibly be extracted even at the low wind speeds.

the benefit to be capable of operating regardless of the wind direction. This eliminates the need to have 
the turbine oriented toward the wind to operate. A conceptual LLISSE-type probe with an integrated wind

2.0 Venus Environment 
The environmental conditions on Venus are unique and unlike those on any other known planet or 



moon. Venus is a place of environmental extremes. Near the surface, the atmospheric temperature is very 
hot (~460 °C), and the sunlight is dim due to the extensive cloud cover that shrouds the whole planet. The 
only surface images taken of Venus were from the Venera missions and are shown in Figure 2.1. These 
images show that the Venus surface is fairly flat and rock covered with some regolith. The cloud cover 
extends from ≈45 to ≈64 km above the surface, where the atmospheric temperatures are between 80 and 
–35 °C, respectively. At the top of the cloud layer, the atmospheric pressure is around 0.1 bar,
corresponding to a pressure altitude of 16 km (52,500 ft) on Earth. A diagram of the Venus atmosphere is
shown in Figure 2.2, and the physical and orbital properties of Venus are shown in Table 2.1.



Another unique aspect of Venus is that the day length is longer than the year. Because of this slow 
rotational rate, the subsolar point moves at a rate of ≈13.4 km/h. This slow rate corresponds to a day 
period of 243 Earth days (Ref. 2). Therefore, for a probe on the surface to collect data over one day/night 
cycle, it would need to be capable of operating throughout this period. 



Because of the thick atmosphere, the pressure and density throughout most of the atmosphere is much 
greater than that on Earth. The atmospheric pressure and density experienced near the surface of Earth 
occurs at an altitude of just over 50 km on Venus.

The profile shown in Figure 2.4 provides a good estimation of the wind speeds through the 
atmosphere. From this figure, the wind speed approaches 0 near the surface. Since the wind speed near 
the surface is very low, accurately determining it is a critical aspect to the wind turbine feasibility and 
design. However, there is limited wind-speed data from the surface of Venus. The available wind-speed 
data are mainly from the Venera-surface landers, and for some of these landers the wind speed was not 
directly measured but was inferred through indirect measurement from sensors and instruments. The 
Venera 13 and 14 landers utilized acoustic sensors to determine the wind velocity at the surface (Ref. 18). 
Essentially those missions collected acoustic noise from a microphone and correlated it to a specific wind 
speed. For wind angles relative to the microphone face, the output was not sensitive to the wind direction. 
However, if the wind was originating from the opposite side to the face of the microphone and impinging 
on the rear of the microphone, the signal dropped considerably. 



The Venera 14 data show that upon landing the noise level remained somewhat constant, indicating a 
constant wind speed of 0.35 to 0.39 m/s. For Venera 13, the noise level was much higher and was out of 
the range of the instrument for much of the operational period on the surface. After 79 min of operation, 











NASA/CR-20240000076 9 



the noise level dropped below the limit of the instrument, and three wind speed data points were recorded 
of 0.56, 0.54, and 0.51 m/s. The noise limit of the acoustic sensor on Venera 13 corresponded to a wind 
speed of 0.57 m/s. Therefore, for the period from landing to 79 min, the wind speeds were above the 
maximum recordable wind speed level of 0.57 m/s (Ref. 18). 



Additional information on the winds at the Venera 13 and 14 landing sites is provided by the pictures 
taken from the landers. Subsequent pictures of the landing ring show that the soil that was initially 
deposited onto the ring from the landing was slowly removed over the course of the mission. The 
estimated sizes of the particles removed from the landing ring over time provide an estimate of the force 
of the wind, and therefore, its corresponding speed. From the dust removal, an estimated wind speed of 
0.4 to 0.5 m/s was determined (Ref. 18). 



All the probe data taken through the different methods indicated that the wind speed at the surface is 
low but very constant and independent of solar time or location (Ref. 21).

3.0 Analysis 
A vertical-axis wind turbine was chosen as the baseline geometry for the implementation of a wind 



turbine on Venus to provide power to a small surface probe. The benefits of the vertical-axis turbine are 
that it provides a compact design that can easily integrate into the small probe configuration. It is easily 
deployable and will operate with any wind direction. There are several different types of vertical-axis 
wind turbines that can be considered. These include a fixed vertical blade, a Darrieus rotor, and a 
helicoidal rotor as shown in Figure 3.1. 



Vertical-axis wind turbines are efficient and can produce high tip-speed ratios. However, in general, 
they have poor starting characteristics, which needs to be accounted for in the overall turbine design. 
The Darrieus rotor can reach very high tip-speed ratios but has the poorest starting characteristics. The 
helicoidal rotor can achieve high tip-speed ratios and has better starting characteristics. Its blade design is 
somewhat complicated, and the blade shape may make integration and deployment from the probe

As mentioned, the solidity and TSR are related: the higher the solidity, the lower the TSR for a given wind 
speed. Conceptually this is due to the kinetic energy available in a given volume of gas passing through the 
turbine being split up over a larger number of blades, thereby causing the force generated on each blade to be 
reduced. The solidity affects the TSR through the conservation of momentum. The larger the blade chord or 
the greater the number of blades (the higher the solidity), the greater the local velocity of the air within the 
turbine that slows down relative to the free stream. Therefore, as the solidity increases, the TSR decreases, for 
a given incoming wind speed to conserve the momentum of the complete system.

Both mass and momentum must be conserved as the air flows through the wind turbine. As energy is 
extracted, the wind velocity slows. Therefore, to conserve mass, the stream-tube of air flowing through the 
turbine expands outward to make up for the reduced velocity and thereby conserving mass, as illustrated in 
Figure 3.4. The mass of the air flowing into the turbine is equal to the mass of the air flowing out of the 
turbine. Also, this local slowing of the wind velocity from the free-stream velocity is the root of the 
relationship between TSR and solidity. The greater the number of blades or their chord length (high solidity), 
the more this velocity slows down, reducing the effective TSR.

As the blade rotates around the angle of attack α, the resultant wind velocity vector (Vr in Figure 3.9)

whether the angle of attack is positive or negative. Another factor that influences the angle of attack of the 
turbine blade as it rotates, is the decrease in the free-stream wind velocity. As the air flows through the

4.0 Turbine Startup 
For the turbine to start spinning, sufficient torque must be generated as the wind passes over the 



stationary turbine blades. The forces on the turbine blades and the corresponding torque generated for the 
design point geometry when the blades are stationary (a TSR of 0) with a wind speed of 0.5 m/s is shown 
in Figure 4.1. 



Since Figure 4.1 is for a stationary rotor, the curves represent the forces generated on a blade at any 
position along the full 360° path of rotation. For a three-bladed turbine the turbine blades are located 120° 
apart. So, the forces shown in Figure 4.1 at points 120° apart represent the forces on each of the stationary 
blades of the turbine for a wind speed of 0.5 m/s. For the turbine to begin to rotate the sum of the torque 
produced by all three blades must be positive and greater than the required starting torque for the turbine. 



For a wind speed of 0.5 m/s the maximum torque is generated on one of the blades if the wind 
incident angle to the blade is 7°. This produces a torque of 0.0018 N-m for that blade. However, the 
torque generated by the other two blades at 127° and 247° locations is –0.0025 and –0.0016 N-m, 
respectively. Therefore, the sum of the total torque is –0.0023 N-m. This total torque is negative and 
therefore insufficient to start the turbine rotating in the correct direction. 



To evaluate the wind speed needed to start the turbine, the total torque generated by the three-bladed 
turbine while stationary, a TSR of 0, was determined for a range of wind speeds and blade positions 
relative to the incoming wind speed. These results are shown in Figure 4.2. 



The blade position relative to the wind speed, shown in Figure 4.2, ranges from 0° to 120°. Because 
the blades are spaced 120° apart, the graph repeats for angles between 120° to 240° and 240° to 360°. 
From Figure 4.2 a wind speed of 1.8 m/s or greater is needed to generate a positive torque that can start 
the turbine rotating. The actual value of the torque needed to start the rotor will depend on the rotational 
resistance of the turbine shaft due to the alternator and bearings. Therefore, 1.8 m/s can be looked upon as 
a lower limit that would start the turbine rotating if there were no resistance on the shaft. In practice, a

4.1 Savonius Rotor 



There are two methods, active and passive, that can be considered to start the turbine. The active 
method would utilize a motor on the turbine shaft to initiate the rotation. The downside to this method is 
that it adds complexity, will require power to start the turbine rotating, and will likely need a sensor 
system to indicate when the wind speed is sufficient for the turbine operation prior to initiating its 
rotation. In a conventional operational environment, these requirements would not be considered difficult. 
However, power, sensors, and electronic control are all difficult to achieve within the Venus environment. 
Therefore, the implementation of an active system to start the turbine would be considered a last resort if 
other methods could not be devised. A passive method such as the integration of a drag-based Savonius 
rotor, shown in Figure 4.3, to the main turbine shaft would be preferable. 











NASA/CR-20240000076 40 



 
Figure 4.3.—Savonius rotor operation. 



 
A Savonius rotor is a drag-based wind turbine that uses the blade drag to rotate the turbine. This 



differs from the main vertical-axis turbine that uses aerodynamic forces to rotate the turbine. For a 
Savonius or drag-type turbine, the rotor blades are curved so that they have different drag coefficients 
(Cd) depending on which side the wind impacts. The curved blades have a concave and convex side. The 
concave side has a much higher drag coefficient than the convex side. The wind coming from a specific 
direction will impact a particular side of the rotor blades, depending on their positioning. The wind will 
impact the concave side on one or more of the blades and the convex side on one or both of the other 
blades. Since the drag coefficient of the convex side is much less than the concave side a greater force 
will be generated on the blade(s) where the wind strikes the concave side than the blade(s) where the wind 
strikes the convex side. This force difference produces a torque about the central shaft, causing the turbine 
to rotate.

initial startup, when the turbine is not rotating the resultant velocity is equal to the wind velocity, as given 
by Equation (36)

4.4 Initial Rotation 



To use the drag rotor to start the turbine spinning, it must generate enough torque to overcome the 
total alternator and bearing torques given in Figure 4.12 and Figure 4.14, respectively. The torque 
generated by the Savonius rotor from an initial zero RPM state is shown in Figure 4.6 and will vary 
depending on the wind direction. 



The starting torque generated by the Savonius rotor as a function of wind speed is shown in 
Figure 4.15 for Savonius rotor heights of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 m. This figure shows two curves for each 
Savonius rotor height. These curves represent the wind-direction-dependent maximum and minimum 
torques produced by the Savonius turbine. To ensure that the rotor will start under any wind direction, the 
minimum torque curve—at the desired starting wind speed—must be at or above the required starting 
torque line. From Figure 3.30, little energy can be extracted from the wind at wind speeds below 0.5 m/s. 
Therefore, 0.5 m/s was selected for a design starting wind speed. Figure 4.16 shows that for this wind 
speed to initiate the rotation of the Savonius turbine independent of wind direction, the rotor height would 
need to be 0.3 m. If a starting design wind speed of 0.6 m/s were selected, the rotor height could be 
reduced to 0.2 m. For the 0.3 m rotor height, the Savonius turbine can still start at wind speeds below 
0.5 m/s, down to approximately 0.25 m/s, but this would be dependent on the wind direction and 
significantly reduce the chances of the turbine starting to rotate. 



Once the Savonius rotor is turning, the resultant velocity is based on the rotor speed and the wind 
speed. Since the Savonius rotor is a drag device, the resultant velocity (Vrs) will depend on whether the 
blade is moving into (180° to 360°) or with the wind (0° to 180°), as given by Equations (60) and (61), 
respectively.

The high-drag portion of the blade that generates the rotational force moves with the wind, and therefore 
it has a lower resultant velocity then the lower drag portion of the blade that moves into the wind. 



The force generated on each blade is given by Equation (35), and the resultant velocity is given by 
Equations (60) and (61). The subsequent torque is given by Equation (41). The torque, as a function of 
TSR for several wind speeds, is shown in Figure 4.17. In this figure, the torque generated by the Savonius 
rotor drops off as the TSR increases, as would be expected, and reaches 0 at a TSR of 0.3. At this TSR, 
the drag generated by the portion of the blade with the high-drag coefficient and lower resultant velocity, 
moving with the wind, is equal to the drag on the portion of the blade with the lower drag coefficient and 
higher resultant velocity, moving into the wind. Although the Savonius rotor can start the turbine 
spinning, it is limited as to the TSR that can be achieved as shown in Figure 4.16. 



For the turbine to start rotating, there must be a transition between the Savonius rotor and the main 
turbine rotor. The torque output for the Savonius rotor, shown in Figure 4.17, shows that positive torque 
is generated at TSR values below 0.3. Therefore, the main turbine must be capable of generating 
sufficient torque at TSRs below 0.3 to use the initial rotation started by the Savonius rotor and begin 
rotating independently. However, from Figure 3.26, no positive torque is generated by the main rotor 
below a TSR of 2. To exemplify this, the torque curves for the Savonius rotor and those for the main 
turbine were plotted together in Figure 4.17. This figure shows that there is a gap in which no torque is 
generated by the Savonius rotor and the main rotor. This gap corresponds to a TSR range of ≈0.3 to 2.

Because of this, the Savonius rotor cannot be used to start the main turbine. There are a few 
approaches that can be taken to solve this issue and provide a wind turbine that can operate on the Venus 
surface. For the vertical-axis turbine, a motor can be used to begin the rotation of the turbine. This motor 
drive approach adds complexity and would require stored power to operate the motor. This approach 
would only be applicable if the wind at the surface of Venus was mostly constant. Once the main rotor 
was started, it would continue operating for an extended period. If the winds are intermittent, however, 
then the continual starting of the rotor would be a large drain on the energy storage system, and there 
would be the possibility that the rotor would not operate for enough time to replenish the energy used to 
repeatedly start the rotor. Another approach would be to examine alternate wind turbine designs that have 
better starting qualities than the vertical-axis wind turbine. For example, a conventional horizontal-axis 
wind turbine has much better starting characteristics and might be more applicable to operation on the 
Venus surface. This type of turbine would, however, be more challenging to package and deploy than the 
vertical-axis turbine. 



5.0 Summary of Results 
This feasibility study was performed to assess the ability for a small vertical-axis wind turbine to 



operate within the Venus environment. The analysis showed that the wind turbine can function on the 
surface of Venus and provide usable power. Although the wind speed on the surface of Venus is low with 
a modal value of 0.5 m/s, the wind turbine was able to generate 3.57 W-h of energy per hour. This 
assumed a Weibull distribution for the wind at the surface of Venus with a peak probability at 0.5 m/s. 
The geometry for the wind turbine was a three-bladed turbine with a blade height of 0.5 m, a diameter of 
0.5 m, and a blade chord of 2.5 cm. This geometry was based on both the ability to package the wind 
turbine within the estimated constraints of a small lander and optimizing the performance of the wind 
turbine within the Venus environment. The maximum turbine efficiency was estimated to be 41 percent at 
a TSR of 3.1 for an optimal wind speed of 1.4 m/s. 











NASA/CR-20240000076 54 



Another aspect of the wind turbine operation that was analyzed was the ability to start the turbine 
rotating. Vertical-axis wind turbines are typically difficult to start, and operation in the low-wind-speed 
environment of Venus compounds the problem. Because of the blade positioning and the direction of the 
wind, only a subset of the total number of blades of a vertical-axis wind turbine can generate positive 
force to begin the turbine rotating. This is in contrast with a more common horizontal-axis wind turbine 
where, if pointed into the wind, all the blades will generate force to begin the turbine rotating. An analysis 
was performed to determine the wind speed and direction needed to start the blades rotating. The analysis 
showed that a wind speed of 1.8 m/s or greater was needed to generate positive force to initiate startup. 
Only a small subset of the possible wind directions to the turbine was sufficient to generate this force. 
Based on the wind-speed probability and the directional requirements, it was determined that the 
combination would occur only 0.9 percent of the time. Therefore, the ability for the turbine to start on its 
own is very unlikely. To begin the turbine rotating, a Savonius rotor was sized to overcome the starting 
torque needed to begin rotation. A Savonius rotor is a drag-based turbine that can start rotating at very 
low wind speeds. The Savonius rotor analysis showed that the rotor could start but was limited to a 
maximum TSR of 0.3 within the Venus environment. This did not match up with the minimum TSR of 
approximately 2 to 2.5 needed for the main turbine to begin rotating. Based on this analysis, the Savonius 
rotor would not be able to start the turbine. 



There are some additional options that can be considered to start the turbine. An electric motor, or the 
alternator itself if operated as a motor, can possibly be used to start the turbine rotating. Because this 
startup process would require power, this scenario would only be beneficial if the winds were consistent, 
and startup would only be needed occasionally. There are currently insufficient surface wind data to 
determine the consistency of the surface winds. Additional wind surface data would be needed to evaluate 
the feasibility of this approach and implement it as a means of starting and operating the wind turbine. 
Another approach that can be considered is to utilize a different wind turbine geometry: one that has a 
greater ability to start under low-wind conditions. A typical horizontal-axis wind turbine can be 
considered. A similar design study would need to be performed to evaluate its startup and operation 
within the Venus surface environment to determine its feasibility.
