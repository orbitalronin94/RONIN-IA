<p>We Can Think About EDL in 3 Bins
• Human (Directed and Commercially-Provided):
</p>
<p>✓ Returning humans from Low Earth Orbit
– Returning humans from beyond LEO (Gateway, the Moon)
– Landing humans (precisely) on the Moon
– Landing humans (precisely) on Mars
</p>
<p>• Robotic (Competed and Directed):
✓ Mars: 9 successful landings, increasing in scale and performance
– Rest of the Solar System: Venus, Saturn, Titan, Neptune, Uranus, 
</p>
<p>Europa, (Jupiter)
✓ Earth Return of samples (potentially biological, or not)
</p>
<p>• Emerging (cost is key):
– Small spacecraft: perform EDL or aerocapture at Earth or elsewhere
– Commercial interest in “asset return” – rocket stages, materials, etc. 12</p>

<p>New EDL Paradigm
</p>
<p>• Viking-derived rigid sphere-cone aeroshells with cross sections that fit in a launch vehicle shroud are not large enough 
to decelerate heavy payloads in the thin Mars atmosphere – a larger entry system is needed (“E”)
</p>
<p>• Supersonic parachutes cannot be used; high-speed propulsive descent is enabling (“D”) 
• Precise Lunar landings require and will demonstrate integrated GN&amp;C for the landing and prediction/knowledge of large-
</p>
<p>engine plume surface interaction (PSI) effects. Both feed forward to large Mars missions (“L”)
• Robust guidance and control throughout entry and descent is required for safe, precise landing (“EDL”)
</p>

<p>Plume Surface Interaction (PSI) Advancement Approach
</p>
<p>Advancement Approach (captured in gaps):
• Mature predictive modeling capability, currently unvalidated
</p>
<p>- Complex, multi-physics problem requiring high-end computing resources to achieve required throughput
- Key environment that will drive lander and surface asset design and create dust that requires mitigation
- Obscuration during PSI event may affect precision landing sensor performance/data, in high-thrust cases
</p>
<p>• Conduct vacuum ground tests with regolith/bedrock simulants to generate initial model validation data
- Small-scale, warm-gas tests varying simulants, vacuum levels (Moon and Mars), nozzle heights and mass flows
- Large-scale (1000 lbf+) vacuum tests with simulants, combustion – more relevant to human-scale systems
- Limited vacuum facilities exist, to handle both regolith and combustion, at any scale
</p>
<p>• Develop instrumentation to measure (1), (2), and (3) above
- Implement in ground tests to demonstrate instruments and measure relevant quantities for model validation
- Instrument CLPS landers (100’s of lbf) for single and multiple PSI phenomena
- Instrument larger lunar and robotic Mars landers with low-SWaPc multi-sensor suites to obtain flight data
</p>

<p>What’s Next in EDL (2024-40)?
</p>
<p>• We will land precisely on the Moon, first with commercially-provided landers (small), 
then evolving to human-scale
</p>
<p>• Lightweight, inexpensive sensors for precise landing (feeds to Mars); integration on commercial landers
• Plume/surface/vehicle interactions near touchdown (feeds to Mars)
• Integrated simulations for assessing landers and sensor suites (feeds to Mars)
</p>
<p>• Commercial efforts in EDL are growing: low-cost Earth return
• We want to return samples from Mars by late 2020’s or early 2030’s
</p>
<p>• Landing ~1500 kg precisely, next to samples that Mars 2020 caches
• Landing a rocket on Mars and autonomously launching it
• Returning the samples to Earth in a capsule with 1x10-6 probability of failure
</p>
<p>• Scientists want to go to Venus, Ice Giants, Ocean Worlds, and Outer Planets (&amp; back)
• EDL Challenges: rugged terrain, unknown/thick atmospheres, high entry speeds
</p>
<p>• Scientists want to go to Mars more often
• EDL Challenges: cost, risk tolerance
</p>
<p>• We have the long-term goal of landing humans on Mars
• EDL Challenges: high mass, precise landing, risk posture for humans
