LOFTID 
Background



A New Kind of Heat Shield 



• After more than a decade of 
development of Hypersonic 
Inflatable Aerodynamic 
Decelerator (HIAD) technology, 
including two suborbital flight 
tests, the LOFTID orbital flight test 
is the next step. This return from 
orbit demonstration provides an 
entry environment relevant to 
many potential applications, 
paving the way for its use on 
future missions. The LOFTID re-
entry vehicle, at 19.7 feet (6 
meters) diameter, is the largest 
blunt body aeroshell to ever go 
through atmospheric entry.






Presenter


Presentation Notes


The goal of the LOFTID project is to test a new type of inflatable heat shield that could one day be used to safely enter payloads into Earth’s atmosphere or any destination with an atmosphere, from space. The inflatable decelerator is made up of a flexible heat shield that can be folded up and stored inside a spacecraft during launch. Once the spacecraft reaches the atmosphere, the heat shield is inflated to slow down the vehicle and protect it from the intense heat of re-entry. LOFTID is part of the Technology Demonstration Missions (TDM) under the Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD).On November 10, 2022, the LOFTID reentry vehicle was launched at Vandenberg Space Force Base as a secondary payload with the Joint Polar Satellite System-2 (JPSS-2), which is a weather satellite in polar orbit, using a United Launch Alliance Atlas V. Once JPSS-2 reached orbit, LOFTID was put on a reentry trajectory from low-Earth orbit to demonstrate the inflatable aeroshell capability to decelerate and withstand re-entry temperatures. At a speed of 18,000 mph, the vehicle entered earth’s atmosphere and the aeroshell was able to decelerate it to 80 mph at which point onboard parachutes were deployed, providing a smooth splashdown in the Pacific Ocean.

Presenter


Presentation Notes


Armstrong Flight Research Center (AFRC) was approached to provide FOSS high spatial density temperature measurements in three strategic locations on the vehicle. FOSS provided 1,110 measurands just behind the nose cone, 114 measurands along the side of the vehicle, and 193 measurands on an internal structure. The main measurement of interest for the LOFTID project was just behind the nose cone. At this location, AFRC FOSS technology was able to measure temperature data in a decaying spiral pattern (23ft long) with a spatial density of 0.25”, with measurements taken at 20 samples per second. This data was recorded locally onboard the Armstrong system as well as transmitted to LOFTID’s avionics data recorder via ethernet. FOSS successfully captured data throughout the entire reentry profile, splashdown, and during vehicle recovery.

Conclusion
• The ruggedized FOSS system successfully acquired multiple 



measurements contiguously at 20 samples per second 



• The fiber placed behind the FTPS rigid aeroshell provided 1,110 
temperature data. 



• On the rigid aeroshell, co-located FOSS FBGs and thermocouples 
were in agreement with reasonable tolerance for difference in 
locational protections. 



• Fibers from the mid and aft sections of the payload confirmed 
that the overall temperature is steady, proving the effectiveness 
of the heat shield. 



• The system has seawater residue from the splashdown but 
proved to be fully operational due to its hermetic design.



• PS – FOSS system still functions as of today!
