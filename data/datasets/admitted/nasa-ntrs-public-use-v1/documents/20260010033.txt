<p>What is Orbital Debris?
</p>
<p>• Orbital debris = any human-made object in space that no 
</p>
<p>longer serves a useful purpose
</p>
<p>– Note there are also natural debris – meteoroids
</p>
<p>• Intact objects, &gt; 1 m
</p>
<p>– Old rocket bodies and spacecraft
</p>
<p>– “Operational” debris – shrouds, mounts, lens caps, etc</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>3 Matney
</p>
<p>What is Orbital Debris?
</p>
<p>• Fragmentation debris, 1 mm – 1 m 
</p>
<p>– Deliberate or accidental explosions from on-board energy sources
</p>
<p>• Unvented rocket fuel
</p>
<p>• Active batteries
</p>
<p>• Self-destruct mechanisms
</p>
<p>– Deliberate or accidental collisions
</p>
<p>• Weapons tests
</p>
<p>• Random collisions
</p>
<p>– Solid rocket motor slag
</p>
<p>• Small debris, &lt; ~2 mm
</p>
<p>– Deterioration of satellite surfaces in space environment
</p>
<p>• Small debris impact ejecta
</p>
<p>• Deterioration of paint and other materials</p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>Space Surveillance Network
</p>
<p>• Almost all of our operational knowledge of the space environment is from the U.S. Department of Defense/War 
</p>
<p>(DoD/DoW) Space Surveillance Network (SSN) and its parallels in other countries
</p>
<p>– New launches
</p>
<p>• Payloads
</p>
<p>• Rocket Bodies
</p>
<p>• Operational Debris (brackets, shrouds, etc.)
</p>
<p>– Breakup events
</p>
<p>• Anomalous debris-producing events
</p>
<p>• Explosions – both accidental and deliberate
</p>
<p>• Collisions between tracked objects
</p>
<p>• It is through the SSN Catalog that we know the various orbits where humans have launched their satellites and 
</p>
<p>how they have evolved over time
</p>
<p>• Using database of carefully tracked objects, it is possible to detect potential collisions and do collision avoidance</p>
<p></p>

<p>HUSIR (Haystack) Radar
</p>
<p>• Located in Massachusetts – 42.6° latitude, operated by MIT Lincoln 
</p>
<p>Laboratory
</p>
<p>• Haystack Ultrawide Satellite Imaging Radar (HUSIR – previously 
</p>
<p>known as Haystack)
</p>
<p>– 36 m diameter
</p>
<p>– 3 cm wavelength (X-band)
</p>
<p>– Can detect debris &gt; ~5 mm in LEO
</p>
<p>• Cannot track – can only detect objects as they pass through the 
</p>
<p>narrow beam
</p>
<p>• The radar accurately measures RCS, range, and Doppler velocity 
</p>
<p>along line of sight, but has trouble with other velocity components, so 
</p>
<p>they usually operate in an off-vertical mode (75° East), where Doppler 
</p>
<p>velocity can be used to infer orbit inclination
</p>

</p>
<p>Lincoln Laboratory, Lexington, Massachusetts</p>

<p>Modeling the Future
</p>
<p>• The NASA ODPO uses the LEO-to-GEO Environment 
</p>
<p>Debris (LEGEND) model to project the growth of the 
</p>
<p>environment into the future
</p>
<p>– Traffic cycle – how will space users launch in the future?
</p>
<p>– Explosion rate – how often will spacecraft and rocket bodies 
</p>
<p>explode and create more debris? 
</p>
<p>• There have been more than 250 on-orbit explosions since 1957
</p>
<p>– Collision rate – how often will objects collide and create large 
</p>
<p>clouds of orbital debris?</p>

<p>Modeling Small Debris
</p>
<p>• The problem with the data on small debris is it is incomplete – 
</p>
<p>and these are the debris that are most dangerous for spacecraft
</p>
<p>– We don’t track them, we only have statistical sub-samples of the population
</p>
<p>– We don’t measure everything we want to measure
</p>
<p>• E.g., we measure radar cross section, not size
</p>
<p>• Limited orbital parameters
</p>
<p>• Shape?
</p>
<p>• How do make sense of it all?</p>

<p>21 Matney
</p>
<p>NASA’s Orbital Debris Engineering Model - ORDEM
</p>
<p>• Orbital debris engineering models are mathematical tools to 
</p>
<p>assess orbital debris flux
</p>
<p>– Created primarily for spacecraft designers and users to accurately assess 
</p>
<p>spacecraft risk
</p>
<p>– Also have been used historically to estimate sensor flux (e.g., predicted 
</p>
<p>detection rate in a radar beam)
</p>
<p>• This requires a model that describes how debris is distributed in 
</p>
<p>space and how they might interact with an orbiting spacecraft</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>22 Matney
</p>
<p>NASA’s Orbital Debris Engineering Model - ORDEM
</p>
<p>• Engineering models need to be updated periodically
</p>
<p>– New data
</p>
<p>– New techniques
</p>
<p>– Unanticipated changes in the environment
</p>
<p>– Need for expanded capabilities
</p>
<p>• Need to be able to predict some distance into the future
</p>
<p>– Someone designing a spacecraft today will need to understand the orbital 
</p>
<p>debris environment decades into the future</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>23 Matney
</p>
<p>History of ORDEM
</p>
<p>• Development on a model to compute debris flux on 
</p>
<p>spacecraft began in the 1980s for NASA’s space station 
</p>
<p>program
</p>
<p>– The earliest versions were analytic equations targeting specific 
</p>
<p>orbits
</p>
<p>– ORDEM96 – first computer-based model, 1996
</p>
<p>• Debris populations in inclination, eccentricity, altitude, and size
</p>
<p>– ORDEM2000
</p>
<p>• Used a finite element approach of the debris environment</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>24 Matney
</p>
<p>History of ORDEM (continued)
</p>
<p>• ORDEM 3.0 introduced some substantial upgrades
</p>
<p>– Extended the model to geosynchronous altitudes (up to 40,000 km)
</p>
<p>• Allowed computation of flux for orbits beyond LEO
</p>
<p>– Populations were broken out by material density families
</p>
<p>– Included estimated uncertainties in the flux
</p>
<p>• ORDEM 3.1 extended ORDEM 3.0 with updated 
</p>
<p>measurements
</p>
<p>• ORDEM 3.2 included debris from the Nov, 2021 Russian 
</p>
<p>ASAT test</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>25 Matney
</p>
<p>ORDEM 4.0
</p>
<p>• NASA’s ORDEM 4.0 is being finalized now
</p>
<p>• It will include the latest measurement data and the latest 
</p>
<p>data analysis techniques
</p>
<p>• The most important improvement will be the inclusion of 
</p>
<p>debris shapes</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>26 Matney
</p>
<p>Shapes
</p>
<p>• Most hypervelocity impact experiments and analysis over 
</p>
<p>the last several decades has concentrated on spherical 
</p>
<p>debris
</p>
<p>– Single size parameter
</p>
<p>– Single impact angle parameter
</p>
<p>• How do we parameterize debris shape?
</p>
<p>– Nearly infinite number of parameters
</p>
<p>– Impact geometry has 4 angle parameters
</p>
<p>• Any introduction of shape is a major model improvement</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>27 Matney
</p>
<p>Shapes (continued)
</p>
<p>• For ORDEM 4.0, shapes are simplified as right circular cylinders (RCC)
</p>
<p>• Single shape parameter 
</p>
<p>– Length-to-diameter ratio L:D
</p>
<p>– L:D &gt; 1 = rod-like
</p>
<p>– L:D &lt; 1 = disk-like
</p>
<p>– L:D ~ 1 = cube/sphere-like (nugget) 
</p>
<p>• Symmetry limits to “only” 3 impact angle parameters
</p>
<p>– Velocity angle to surface normal
</p>
<p>– The tilt of the object’s symmetry axis to the velocity vector
</p>
<p>– The yaw of the symmetry axis around the velocity vector 
</p>
<p>• Simple to manufacture for experiments
</p>
<p>•  </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>28 Matney
</p>
<p>Density Families
</p>
<p>• ORDEM 3 divided the population up into 4 major density families (plus intact 
</p>
<p>objects)
</p>
<p> Low density    Medium density
</p>
<p> High density    Sodium-Potassium (NaK)
</p>
<p>• Experience from ORDEM 3 Shuttle analysis indicated a contribution from 
</p>
<p>Titanium particles, with a density (𝝆 = 4.5 g/cm3) straddling the boundary 
</p>
<p>between medium density and high density categories
</p>
<p>• ORDEM 4.0 has two extra material density categories
</p>
<p> Low density (0-2 g/cm3)   CFRP (1.46 g/cm3) 
</p>
<p> Medium-low density (2-4 g/cm3)  Medium-high density (4-6 g/cm3)
</p>
<p> High density (&gt;6 g/cm3)   Sodium-Potassium (0.9 g/cm3)</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>29 Matney
</p>
<p>Debris Size
</p>
<p>• ORDEM 3 binned debris size into half-decade bins from 
</p>
<p>10μm to 1 m
</p>
<p>• However, half-decade binning does not provide the 
</p>
<p>desired resolution in the rapidly-changing population as 
</p>
<p>a function of size around 1 mm and other regions
</p>
<p>• Studies indicated that binning the debris size into 
</p>
<p>quarter-decade bins would provide adequate resolution, 
</p>
<p>so ORDEM 4.0 has debris size divided into quarter-
</p>
<p>decade bins</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>30 Matney
</p>
<p>Igloo
</p>
<p>• ORDEM 3 used a directional “igloo” flux in the spacecraft 
</p>
<p>frame with evenly divided bins in azimuth and elevation
</p>
<p>– Most of the flux for a spacecraft in a circular orbit is in the plane 
</p>
<p>parallel to the surface of the Earth
</p>
<p>– However, the elevation and azimuth binning had the highest 
</p>
<p>resolution near the poles (corresponding to zenith and nadir), where 
</p>
<p>the flux is typically minimal
</p>
<p>– This resulted in inefficiencies in computation and storage</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration
</p>
<p>31 Matney
</p>
<p>Igloo (continued)
</p>
<p>• For ORDEM 4.0, an Arvo mapping is adopted to map 
</p>
<p>squares on a cube face onto the “igloo” sphere surface
</p>
<p>• The mapping creates elements with identical area/solid 
</p>
<p>angle</p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>35 Matney
</p>
<p>Conclusions
</p>
<p>• NASA’s newest orbital debris engineering model ORDEM 4.0 will be released 
</p>
<p>shortly
</p>
<p>– Includes debris shapes, parameterized as right circular cylinders
</p>
<p>– Includes two new debris density categories
</p>
<p>– Based on debris size, divided into quarter decade bins
</p>
<p>– The “igloo” flux direction bins will be based on an Arvo scheme that creates elements with 
</p>
<p>equal solid angle
</p>
<p>– Uses a variety of the most recent debris measurement to update the populations  
</p>
<p>• Ongoing review by the NASA Engineering and Safety Center (NESC)
</p>
<p>–  preliminary recommendation is “go-release”
</p>
<p>• Ongoing comparison study with ESA’s MASTER model </p>
<p></p>

<p>Conclusions
</p>
<p>• ORDEM (along with its sister meteoroid model MEM) 
</p>
<p>provides the basic debris parameters needed for the risk 
</p>
<p>analysis community (i.e., BUMPER) to turn that 
</p>
<p>environment into spacecraft risk
</p>
<p>– And that is where hypervelocity testing and formulation of 
</p>
<p>damage equations come in </p>
