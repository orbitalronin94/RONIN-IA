What is Orbital Debris?



• Orbital debris = any human-made object in space that no 



longer serves a useful purpose



– Note there are also natural debris – meteoroids



• Intact objects, > 1 m



– Old rocket bodies and spacecraft



– “Operational” debris – shrouds, mounts, lens caps, etc

What is Orbital Debris?



• Fragmentation debris, 1 mm – 1 m 



– Deliberate or accidental explosions from on-board energy sources



• Unvented rocket fuel



• Active batteries



• Self-destruct mechanisms



– Deliberate or accidental collisions



• Weapons tests



• Random collisions



– Solid rocket motor slag



• Small debris, < ~2 mm



– Deterioration of satellite surfaces in space environment



• Small debris impact ejecta



• Deterioration of paint and other materials

Space Surveillance Network



• Almost all of our operational knowledge of the space environment is from the U.S. Department of Defense/War 



(DoD/DoW) Space Surveillance Network (SSN) and its parallels in other countries



– New launches



• Payloads



• Rocket Bodies



• Operational Debris (brackets, shrouds, etc.)



– Breakup events



• Anomalous debris-producing events



• Explosions – both accidental and deliberate



• Collisions between tracked objects



• It is through the SSN Catalog that we know the various orbits where humans have launched their satellites and 



how they have evolved over time



• Using database of carefully tracked objects, it is possible to detect potential collisions and do collision avoidance

HUSIR (Haystack) Radar



• Located in Massachusetts – 42.6° latitude, operated by MIT Lincoln 



Laboratory



• Haystack Ultrawide Satellite Imaging Radar (HUSIR – previously 



known as Haystack)



– 36 m diameter



– 3 cm wavelength (X-band)



– Can detect debris > ~5 mm in LEO



• Cannot track – can only detect objects as they pass through the 



narrow beam



• The radar accurately measures RCS, range, and Doppler velocity 



along line of sight, but has trouble with other velocity components, so 



they usually operate in an off-vertical mode (75° East), where Doppler 



velocity can be used to infer orbit inclination

Modeling the Future



• The NASA ODPO uses the LEO-to-GEO Environment 



Debris (LEGEND) model to project the growth of the 



environment into the future



– Traffic cycle – how will space users launch in the future?



– Explosion rate – how often will spacecraft and rocket bodies 



explode and create more debris? 



• There have been more than 250 on-orbit explosions since 1957



– Collision rate – how often will objects collide and create large 



clouds of orbital debris?

and these are the debris that are most dangerous for spacecraft



– We don’t track them, we only have statistical sub-samples of the population



– We don’t measure everything we want to measure

• Limited orbital parameters

21 Matney



NASA’s Orbital Debris Engineering Model - ORDEM



• Orbital debris engineering models are mathematical tools to 



assess orbital debris flux



– Created primarily for spacecraft designers and users to accurately assess 



spacecraft risk



– Also have been used historically to estimate sensor flux (e.g., predicted 



detection rate in a radar beam)



• This requires a model that describes how debris is distributed in 



space and how they might interact with an orbiting spacecraft

• Engineering models need to be updated periodically



– New data



– New techniques



– Unanticipated changes in the environment

– Someone designing a spacecraft today will need to understand the orbital

23 Matney



History of ORDEM



• Development on a model to compute debris flux on 



spacecraft began in the 1980s for NASA’s space station 



program



– The earliest versions were analytic equations targeting specific 



orbits



– ORDEM96 – first computer-based model, 1996



• Debris populations in inclination, eccentricity, altitude, and size



– ORDEM2000



• Used a finite element approach of the debris environment











National Aeronautics and Space Administration



24 Matney



History of ORDEM (continued)



• ORDEM 3.0 introduced some substantial upgrades



– Extended the model to geosynchronous altitudes (up to 40,000 km)



• Allowed computation of flux for orbits beyond LEO



– Populations were broken out by material density families



– Included estimated uncertainties in the flux



• ORDEM 3.1 extended ORDEM 3.0 with updated 



measurements



• ORDEM 3.2 included debris from the Nov, 2021 Russian 



ASAT test

26 Matney



Shapes



• Most hypervelocity impact experiments and analysis over 



the last several decades has concentrated on spherical 



debris



– Single size parameter



– Single impact angle parameter



• How do we parameterize debris shape?



– Nearly infinite number of parameters



– Impact geometry has 4 angle parameters



• Any introduction of shape is a major model improvement











National Aeronautics and Space Administration



27 Matney



Shapes (continued)



• For ORDEM 4.0, shapes are simplified as right circular cylinders (RCC)



• Single shape parameter 



– Length-to-diameter ratio L:D



– L:D > 1 = rod-like



– L:D < 1 = disk-like



– L:D ~ 1 = cube/sphere-like (nugget) 



• Symmetry limits to “only” 3 impact angle parameters



– Velocity angle to surface normal



– The tilt of the object’s symmetry axis to the velocity vector



– The yaw of the symmetry axis around the velocity vector 



• Simple to manufacture for experiments



•

Density Families



• ORDEM 3 divided the population up into 4 major density families (plus intact 



objects)



 Low density Medium density



 High density Sodium-Potassium (NaK)



• Experience from ORDEM 3 Shuttle analysis indicated a contribution from 



Titanium particles, with a density (𝝆 = 4.5 g/cm3) straddling the boundary 



between medium density and high density categories



• ORDEM 4.0 has two extra material density categories



 Low density (0-2 g/cm3) CFRP (1.46 g/cm3) 



 Medium-low density (2-4 g/cm3) Medium-high density (4-6 g/cm3)



 High density (>6 g/cm3) Sodium-Potassium (0.9 g/cm3)

29 Matney



Debris Size



• ORDEM 3 binned debris size into half-decade bins from 



10μm to 1 m



• However, half-decade binning does not provide the 



desired resolution in the rapidly-changing population as 



a function of size around 1 mm and other regions



• Studies indicated that binning the debris size into 



quarter-decade bins would provide adequate resolution, 



so ORDEM 4.0 has debris size divided into quarter-



decade bins











National Aeronautics and Space Administration



30 Matney



Igloo



• ORDEM 3 used a directional “igloo” flux in the spacecraft 



frame with evenly divided bins in azimuth and elevation



– Most of the flux for a spacecraft in a circular orbit is in the plane 



parallel to the surface of the Earth



– However, the elevation and azimuth binning had the highest 



resolution near the poles (corresponding to zenith and nadir), where 



the flux is typically minimal



– This resulted in inefficiencies in computation and storage











National Aeronautics and Space Administration



31 Matney



Igloo (continued)



• For ORDEM 4.0, an Arvo mapping is adopted to map 



squares on a cube face onto the “igloo” sphere surface



• The mapping creates elements with identical area/solid 



angle

35 Matney



Conclusions



• NASA’s newest orbital debris engineering model ORDEM 4.0 will be released 



shortly



– Includes debris shapes, parameterized as right circular cylinders



– Includes two new debris density categories



– Based on debris size, divided into quarter decade bins



– The “igloo” flux direction bins will be based on an Arvo scheme that creates elements with 



equal solid angle



– Uses a variety of the most recent debris measurement to update the populations 



• Ongoing review by the NASA Engineering and Safety Center (NESC)



– preliminary recommendation is “go-release”



• Ongoing comparison study with ESA’s MASTER model 











National Aeronautics and Space Administration

Conclusions



• ORDEM (along with its sister meteoroid model MEM) 



provides the basic debris parameters needed for the risk 



analysis community (i.e., BUMPER) to turn that 



environment into spacecraft risk



– And that is where hypervelocity testing and formulation of 



damage equations come in
