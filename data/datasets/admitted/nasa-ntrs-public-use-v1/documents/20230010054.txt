ABSTRACT 



A finite volume-based network flow modeling tool, Generalized Fluid System Simulation Program 



(GFSSP), was used to simulate the no-vent filling of a cryogenic tank with a thermodynamic vent 



system assisted injector. The tank was discretized into nodes in the radial and axial directions to 



calculate two-dimensional axisymmetric flow with heat transfer between the solid wall and fluid. 



The nodes are connected by branches where momentum equations are solved to calculate the 



flowrates. The mass and energy conservation equations and the equation of state are solved to 



calculate pressure, temperature, and resident mass at the nodes. The energy conservation equations 



at the solid nodes were solved to calculate the temperature of the solid wall. The system of 



equations was solved by a combination of successive substitution and simultaneous Newton-



Raphson method. The numerical model accounts for a) different regimes of pool boiling heat 



transfer, b) condensation of vapor around the spray droplets, c) condensation of vapor at the 



interface of the cooled injector and ullage, and d) condensation at the liquid-vapor interphase. The 



numerical predictions of tank pressure, filling rate, and wall temperatures were compared with the 



test data. The paper also presents the effect of nodal discretization by comparing the results of the 



1D and 2D models and examines the effect of the droplet diameter on tank filling. 



 



1. Introduction 



On orbit transfer of cryogenic liquids is necessary for many future space missions. Filling a tank 



with cryogenic fluid is more challenging than filling a tank with water or any other fluid that is in 



liquid state at atmospheric conditions. Filling a tank with cryogenic fluid is a two-step process. 



First the tank and the transfer line must be chilled. Liquid cryogens start flowing into the tank at a 



significant rate only after the tank and transfer lines are chilled to the fluid saturation temperature. 



In normal gravity, cryogenic tanks are usually filled from the bottom at nearly atmospheric 



pressure. The vapor generated due to heat transfer from the warm tank walls is allowed to vent 



from the top of the tank while the tank is being filled. Filling a cryogenic tank in the absence of 



gravity is more challenging because in a non-stratified environment, liquid propellant may not 



settle at the tank bottom as it does on earth. There is a strong possibility that liquid propellant may 











exit through the vent valve, which is typically located at the top of the tank to vent propellant 



vapor. 



 



To reduce the loss of precious propellant during tank filling, a “charge-hold-vent” (CHV) method 



[1] was developed. CHV is a cyclic process where the tank wall temperature is reduced to the 



saturation temperature of cryogenic propellant by extracting energy from the wall by repeated 



injections of the liquid propellant followed by a hold and vent process. Once the tank wall reaches 



the saturation temperature, the tank is filled with a closed vent valve. A simpler method is that of 



Vented-Chill / No-Vent-Fill (VCNVF) [2]. In this method, the tank’s vent valve is kept open for 



an initial period while the tank walls are chilled with a spray of cryogenic liquid that boils to vapor 



and exits through the vent. When the tank walls have been sufficiently chilled, the vent valve is 



closed, and the tank is filled with liquid. Care must be taken with the timing of the vent closure. 



If the vent is closed too early, residual heat in the walls may drive sufficient boil-off to raise the 



tank pressure high enough to stall the inlet flow. If the vent is closed too late, some liquid 



propellant may be lost out the vent. Compared to the CHV method, VCNVF is less mass efficient; 



the gas being vented during the chill may not be extracting the maximum possible thermal energy 



from the tank wall. There is always the risk that some of the precious liquid propellant will be 



vented. However, VCNVF is much simpler from an operational standpoint. The transfer lines 



need to be chilled down only once and there is less cycling of inlet and vent valves. Since there is 



no hold period, overall loading time is likely to be shorter. 



The limitation of the VCNVF method to find an optimum target temperature was overcome by the 



No Vent Fill (NVF) method [2] where a TVS (Thermodynamic Vent System) augmented injector 



was used with the vent valve closed during the entire filling process. In a TVS-augmented injector, 



the liquid flow splits into two streams: the minor stream is routed to a Joule-Thomson (J-T) orifice 



where the flow immediately flashes from liquid to vapor or two phase mixture, because the 



downstream of the J-T leg is maintained at vacuum level; the primary stream is injected into the 



tank after being cooled by the cold vapor of the J-T leg in a heat exchanger. The flow through the 



J-T leg is also used to cool the outer metal matrix of the injector which in turn cools the vapor in 



the tank ullage. The cooling of vapor in the ullage by the cold injector surface reduces the ullage 



pressure, allowing liquid to enter and fill the tank. 



An accurate simulation of no vent tank chill and fill is necessary to estimate the amount of 



propellant that is needed to chill the tank, determine the maximum sustainable flowrate to fill the 



tank without stalling, and establish a timeline for the filling process. Numerical modeling of filling 



a cryogenic tank is complex because the flow is unsteady two phase and the process involves both 



boiling and condensation with heat transfer between solid and fluid. In recent years, progress has 



been made to develop a numerical modeling technique to simulate CHV [3], VCNVF [4] and NVF 



[5,6] methods of tank filling and verifying the numerical predictions by comparing with the test 



data of Chato et al [1] and Hartwig et al [2]. The numerical technique was incorporated into 



NASA’s Generalized Fluid System Simulation Program (GFSSP) [7]. 



GFSSP was used [3] to model CHV process by discretizing the tank into multiple nodes with 



conjugate heat transfer between tank wall and fluid nodes. The predicted propellant consumption 











and wall temperature compared well with the test data measured at K-Site at NASA/GRC [1]. The 



VCNVF test carried out at NASA/MSFC [2] using CRYOTE tank was also modeled [4] using 



GFSSP’s multi-node modeling approach. The pool boiling curve was introduced in the model 



along with a simplified condensation model at droplet-ullage interphase. The predicted tank 



pressure, filling rate and wall temperatures compared well with the test data. The multi-node model 



of VCNVF test was extended to model NVF test with TVS assisted injector. In this modeling effort 



[5], two additional condensation models were introduced: condensation at injector-wall interface 



and condensation at liquid-vapor interphase. The numerical predictions of pressure and tank filling 



time compare with the measured data within 11% and 5% respectively. 



The purpose of this paper is to investigate the effect of discretization on the performance of No 



Vent Tank Filling model. In reference 4 and 5, the tank was discretized into 10 axial nodes. The 



variation in the radial direction was not considered. In the present model, the tank was also 



discretized in the radial direction to account for a recirculating flow that could develop in the tank 



due to natural convection. The intent was to compare the numerical predictions of 2D 



axisymmetric model with 1D model results and test data. A working progress of 2D model 



development was presented in reference 6. A plausible numerical solution was obtained without 



introducing pressure work and condensation at liquid vapor interface. Model predictions compared 



favorably with test data and 1D solution.

3. Mathematical Modeling 



Numerical modeling of filling a tank at ambient temperature with liquid cryogen is highly 



complex. The physical processes are complex because the flow is unsteady, two-phase, and the 



process involves both boiling and condensation with heat transfer between solid and fluid. The 



objectives of a numerical model of filling a cryogenic tank are to predict the chilldown and 



filling time and to estimate the amount of propellant consumption to chill down a tank. This 



paper uses a nodal approach for numerical simulation using GFSSP. 



 



3.1 Introduction to GFSSP 



GFSSP is a general-purpose computer program for analyzing fluid flow and heat transfer in a 



complex network of fluid and solid systems. It employs a pressure based, finite volume 



algorithm [8] that solves the mass and energy conservation equations at the nodes and the 



momentum conservation equation at branches connecting the nodes. The energy conservation 



equations for solid are solved at solid nodes that are connected with fluid nodes to allow 



conjugate heat transfer. The mass and momentum conservation equations along with the 



equation of state are solved simultaneously to calculate pressure, flowrate and resident mass 



using the Newton-Raphson method. The energy conservation equations are solved by a 



successive substitution method to calculate enthalpies at the nodes within the iteration loop of 



Newton-Raphson scheme. This scheme known as “SAAS” (Simultaneous Adjustment with 











Successive Substitution) scheme [8] has been found to be robust and efficient for a non-linear 



and coupled set of governing equations. 



Thermodynamic properties are calculated for propellants using the computer program GASP [9], 



which is integrated with GFSSP. The fluid resistance library includes pipes, orifices, and 



common fittings and valves. GFSSP has three major parts. The first part is the graphical user 



interface MIG (Modeling Interface for GFSSP). MIG allows users to create a flow circuit by a 



“point and click” paradigm. It creates the GFSSP input file after the completion of the model 



building process. It can also create a customized GFSSP executable by compiling and linking 



User Subroutines with the solver module of the code. The user can run GFSSP from MIG and 



post process the results in the same environment. The second major part of the program is the 



Solver and Property Module. This is the heart of the program, which reads the input data file and 



generates the required conservation equations for fluid and solid nodes and branches with the 



help of thermodynamic property programs. It also interfaces with User Subroutines to receive 



any specific inputs from users. Finally, it creates output files for MIG to read and display results. 



The User Subroutine is the third major part of the program. This consists of several blank 



subroutines that are called by the Solver Module. These subroutines allow the users to 



incorporate any new physical model, resistance option, fluid, etc. in the model. Further details of 



the finite volume procedure and the computer program are described in references 7 and 8. 



 



3.2 Pool Boiling Model 



 



The chilldown of a propellant tank is primarily governed by boiling of liquid propellant at the 



tank wall. Figure 5 shows different pool boiling regimes. During chilldown, the heat flux 



temperature history of the solid surface moves from right to left (E - D - C – B – A). Initially, the 



difference between the solid wall surface temperature and saturation temperature (wall 



superheat) is typically several hundred degrees. Therefore, the boiling starts in the Film Boiling 



regime and continues until it reaches the Leidenfrost (Minimum Film Boiling, MFB) point. After 



reaching the Leidenfrost point, it switches to Transition Boiling and continues to Critical Heat 



Flux. Nucleate Boiling starts after reaching Critical Heat Flux and, finally, heat transfer is 



controlled by natural convection beyond the Onset of Nucleate Boiling (ONB) point. Pool 



boiling correlations used in this paper are described in reference 7.

3.3 Condensation Model 



 



Condensation on a droplet from liquid spray 



 



Some of the vapor in the tank ullage condenses during the process of filling the tank by spraying 



liquid propellant in the tank. Condensation of vapor occurs when vapor comes in contact with 



subcooled liquid spray. Condensation reduces the tank pressure and therefore must be accounted 



for in the numerical model to predict the pressure and the resident liquid mass in the tank. A 



simple condensation model was developed [11] for such analysis. It is assumed that the liquid 



spray in the tank remains in the form of droplets of constant effective diameter. The number of 



droplets in the tank was estimated from the resident mass. The method of estimating the 



condensation rate was described in reference 10. 



Condensation at Injector Ullage Interface 



Figure 6 shows the schematic of the condensation model at Injector-Ullage interface. The 



injector is cooled by the internal flow of cold vapor produced by the J-T valve. Ullage vapor is 



condensed at this cold surface. Condensation occurs on a thin condensate liquid film where the 



liquid film-ullage interface remains at saturation temperature corresponding to the ullage 



pressure near the solid surface. The method of estimating condensation rate described in 



reference 10 was also used in this paper. 











 
 



Figure 6. Schematic of Injector – Ullage Condensation Model 



 



Condensation at the liquid pool-vapor interface 



 The interphase of liquid and vapor remains saturated with respect to the local vapor pressure. At 



the liquid-vapor interphase, there is a possibility of either evaporation or condensation depending 



upon the condition of ullage vapor and liquid. The phase change occurs as a result of imbalance 



between sensible heat transfer rates on the vapor and liquid sides of the saturated interphase. If 



the heat transfer from the ullage to the interphase (QUI) is larger than the heat transfer from the 



interphase to liquid (QIL), evaporation of liquid occurs as shown in Figure 7. On the other hand, 



if the heat transfer from the interphase to liquid (QIL) is larger than the heat transfer from the 



ullage to the interphase (QUI), condensation of vapor occurs at the interphase. Further detail of 



this analysis appears in Reference 10.

5.1 Effect of Droplet Diameter 



 



Calculations have been performed with two different droplet diameters: 1.5 mm and 



0.375 mm. It is observed that the predicted resident mass (Figure 12) with 0.375 mm 



diameter (blue line) matches better with the test data. However, the pressures were under 



predicted (Figure 11) during the 1800 seconds to 2400 seconds period, and they were 



over predicted during the 2400 seconds to 3100 seconds period. It may be noted that three 



modes of condensation influence the tank pressure during tank filling. During the 1700 to 



1900 seconds period, condensation at injector-ullage interface helps to lower the tank 



pressure and thereby avoids the stalling of the tank during no vent filling. The second 



mode of condensation, i.e., condensation around droplets, becomes prevalent after 1900 



seconds. This allows the tank pressure to drop at a faster rate. As the tank fills, 



condensation around droplet reduces since fewer droplets are available for condensation. 



However, the third mode of condensation starts at the liquid-vapor interphase as the tank 



starts filling and thereby causing the tank pressure continue to fall. A detailed description 



of all three condensation models incorporated in the 1D GFSSP model is provided in 



Reference 10. Those condensation models with one additional modification was 



incorporated in the 2D model described in this paper. In the modified condensation model 



around spray droplets, the number of droplets is reduced as the tank fills. 



 



The variation of droplet counts during tank filling is shown in Figure 13. The maximum 



number of droplets for 1.5 mm and 0.375 mm are approximately 88,000 and 14,000 



respectively. The larger number of droplets provides a higher rate of condensation as 











shown in Figure 14. The droplet counts and condensation rate gradually decrease as the 



tank fills.

5.4 Predicted Flow Pattern 



 



One of the reasons of building a 2D model is to account for the effect of recirculating flow on the 



thermodynamics and heat transfer processes that occur during tank filling. The 1D model shown 



in Figure 8, does not have the capability to predict recirculating flow. On the other hand, a Navier-



Stokes based CFD simulation of the entire tank filling process may also not be practicable for 



scoping analysis. Therefore, a network based multi-dimensional flow calculation was attempted in 



this paper. However, viscous effects were not modeled in any detail. In the momentum equation 



only pressure and gravity terms were accounted for. 



 



Figure 16 shows the predicted stream traces and contours of vapor quality at 2000 seconds. The 



X and Y co-ordinates are in inches. Stream traces are computed from radial and axial velocity 



components at each point. It represents the path of “zero mass” particles when released at 



multiple points in the flow domain. The 2D model predicts recirculating flows that are similar to 



those observed in natural convection. An upward flow is observed near the wall which is warmer 



than the fluid. This is due to gravitational effect in a flow with density variation. The vapor 



contour plot shows that the fluid with lower vapor content (denser fluid) is settled at the bottom. 



Overall, the predicted flow field and vapor quality contour plots appear plausible.

6. Conclusions 
Numerical modeling of No Vent Fill of a Cryogenic Tank is complex for several reasons. 



The flow is two-phase with both boiling and condensation taking place during the 



operation. In recent years, a nodal modeling tool, GFSSP [7] has been used [10,111] to 



model tank filling. Due to unavailability of accurate correlations to model condensation, 



“ad-hoc” condensation models were developed. Numerical prediction of system 



parameters such as tank pressure and filling rate compared reasonably well with the test 



data [2]. Typically, nodal modeling tools are used to model flow networks consisting of 



pipes and various flow components where flow is considered one-dimensional. In no vent 



fill models of cryogenic tank, the spherical tank was discretized into cylindrical section 



and was thus represented by a series of pipes with variable diameters. There was no 



attempt to predict recirculating flow in the tank caused due to natural convection. In this 



paper an attempt was made to discretize the tank assuming the tank is two-dimensional 



and axisymmetric. A two-dimensional nodal based model was able to predict 



recirculating flow in the tank and provided some improvement over the 1D model. In the 



2D model it was possible to use a more realistic effective droplet size in comparison to 



the 1D model. However, more validation is necessary to justify the use of the 2D models 



over the 1D model since the 2D model take more time to run. Both 1D and 2D modeling, 



however, need more accurate correlations of condensation and evaporation heat transfer 



to improve the accuracy of numerical predictions. Furthermore, the ability to model 



separated two-phase flow will help to predict the ullage stratification more accurately.
