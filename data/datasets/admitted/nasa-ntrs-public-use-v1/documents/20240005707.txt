<p>Summary 
Low-leakage seals are an enabling technology for future lunar surface systems, and they must be kept 
</p>
<p>clean to ensure that crews have sufficient breathable air for extended lunar surface missions. Previous 
testing on representative state-of-the-art seals showed that while seals can tolerate some level of lunar 
simulant dust contamination, dust can degrade seal performance to a point at which seal leak rates exceed 
maximum allowable values. The ability to clean simulant from seals had not been thoroughly studied, and 
the effectiveness and potential complications of using different cleaning methods for lunar seals were not 
well understood. To address this knowledge gap, researchers at the NASA Glenn Research Center 
executed a multiphase study to better understand and mitigate seal dust-exposure risks. The focus of this 
report is on the second phase of that study, in which tests were conducted to evaluate the viability of seal 
cleaning methods for use in the lunar environment. Multiple methods for cleaning seals contaminated 
with lunar simulant were assessed for cleaning effectiveness and viability for use in the lunar 
environment. Leak tests were performed on seals after they were cleaned to determine whether leak rates 
could be brought back to acceptable levels, and cleaned seals were examined to determine whether their 
surfaces had been damaged by the cleaning processes. This phase of the study also investigated changes 
in the seals’ adhesion forces as a result of contamination and cleaning. 
</p>

<p>Introduction 
NASA’s Artemis Program is working toward a goal of innovative and sustainable lunar exploration 
</p>
<p>by the late 2020s (Ref. 1). Low-leakage seals for future lunar surface systems are an enabling technology 
for a sustained lunar presence. Seals on hatches, docking systems, and critical interfaces on future lunar 
landers, ascent vehicles, habitation modules, and pressurized rovers must be kept clean to achieve the 
extremely low leak rates that will be required to ensure crews have sufficient breathable air for extended 
lunar surface missions. Contamination and debris on these seals can create leak paths for the pressurized 
atmosphere of a surface asset to escape. 
</p>
<p>One of the uncertainties involved with achieving a sustained lunar presence lies with the interactions 
between lunar dust and seals. Lunar dust has unique abrasive, morphological, and electrostatic properties 
that make it pervasive, damaging, and difficult to mitigate for lunar surface assets (Ref. 2). During the 
Apollo missions, lunar dust caused mechanical failures of many systems, including seal failures on 
astronaut suits and sample return containers (Ref. 3). 
</p>
<p>Recent tests on representative state-of-the-art (SOA) seals showed that while seals can tolerate some 
level of lunar simulant dust contamination, dust can degrade seal performance to a point at which seal 
leak rates exceed maximum allowable values (Ref. 4). However, the ability to clean simulant from seals 
had not been thoroughly studied, and the effectiveness and potential complications of using different 
cleaning methods was not well understood.  
</p>
<p>Recognizing the risks for dust exposure and contamination of seals on docking mechanisms and 
extravehicular activity (EVA) hatches, the Human Landing System (HLS) Program recently funded a 
multiphase series of tests at the NASA Glenn Research Center to better understand and mitigate seal dust-
exposure risks. The original plan for this work was organized into four phases: (1) development of a 
performance database for current SOA seals, (2) evaluation of the viability of seal cleaning methods,  
(3) evaluation of the efficacy of alternative seal surface treatments, and (4) evaluation and development of 
new dust-tolerant seal designs and dust mitigation approaches. This paper focuses on the second phase, 
evaluation of the viability of seal cleaning methods. 
</p>
<p>Test Objectives and Requirements 
Degraded seal performance from dust contamination was observed in the first phase of testing  
</p>
<p>(Ref. 4), and the mitigation plan to address the risks associated with lunar dust contamination on seals 
included exploring techniques to remove dust from the surface. Numerous SOA cleaning techniques  
had been identified for seals, but the effectiveness of these techniques on lunar-dust-contaminated seals 
was unknown. To understand and mitigate the risks associated with lunar dust contamination of seals, 
researchers at Glenn were tasked with evaluating the effectiveness of candidate seal cleaning methods for  
 
 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>NASA/TM-20240005707 3 
</p>
<p>representative SOA seal designs exposed to lunar dust simulants. To this end, the test objectives for this 
test campaign were to 
 
1. Evaluate the effectiveness of candidate seal cleaning techniques. 
2. Identify the most promising cleaning technique(s). 
3. Determine if different seal designs respond differently to the cleaning techniques. 
4. Determine if the cleaning techniques are damaging to the seals. 
</p>
<p>Methods and Materials 
At the start of this test campaign, a screening study was performed to identify the most promising of 
</p>
<p>several candidate cleaning techniques for use in further testing. Seals were coated with Braycote 
Micronic 601 EF grease (BP Lubricants USA Inc.) and then contaminated with JSC–1A lunar mare 
simulant at contamination levels that would typically result in unacceptably high leak rates. After 
contamination, a single cleaning pass was used to remove the dust. The specimen was then photographed 
with a digital camera for visual inspection and visually assessed for cleaning effectiveness. Based on the 
ability of each cleaning method to successfully remove simulant from the seals and an assessment of the 
ease of use by the operator, the cleaning techniques were downselected for further testing.  
</p>
<p>Leak tests were performed on each seal test specimen at the start of this study to establish baseline leak 
rates and variability in the seals. For this test campaign, seal specimens were installed in aluminum test 
fixtures before being contaminated with JSC–1A lunar mare simulant. The amount of dust contamination 
was targeted to be greater than or equal to the breakthrough contamination point identified in Phase 1 testing 
(Ref. 4). Shown in Figure 1, dust masks were used to keep dust out of critical areas on the fixture, such as 
pressure ports, vacuum ports, and support seals. The dust contamination level for each test specimen was 
characterized as a percent area coverage of the surface by optical methods, as described in the Phase 1 
testing report (Ref. 4). After the dust was deposited on the seal test specimen, five cleaning passes were 
performed on the seal to clean it as thoroughly as possible. This sequence of dust contamination and 
cleaning was repeated for a total of five cycles to evaluate the effectiveness of each cleaning technique after 
multiple cycles of dusting and cleaning. After five cycles were completed, the cleaned surface and cleaning 
material were photographed. The upper plate of the test fixture was then installed, and the seal test article 
was leak tested in an environmental chamber. The fully assembled test fixture compressed the seals to the 
same nominal compression loads they would experience during a mission. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 1.—NASA Docking System (NDS) seal in leak test fixture 
with dust masks installed. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>

</p>
<p>After leak testing, some of the specimens were subjected to an adhesion test in which the adhesion 
force between the seal and the opposing sealing surface in the test fixture was measured at –50 °C in an 
axial load frame. The specimens were kept in the test fixtures and connected to the load frame. The load 
frame was used to apply nominal compression loads on the seal, and then the force required to separate 
the seal from the opposing sealing surface was measured. 
</p>
<p>A subset of specimens of each seal design was examined under an optical white light profilometer to 
evaluate any damage caused by the various cleaning methods. These specimens were removed from their 
test fixtures and cleaned with isopropyl alcohol (IPA) and task wipes before being scanned. 
</p>
<p>Additional details about the test methods and materials used in this study are provided in the 
following subsections. 
</p>
<p>Cleaning Methods 
</p>
<p>Based in part on lessons learned from a previous test campaign that explored cleaning methods on 
silicone O-rings (Ref. 5), several methods were selected for the initial screening study. These were 
broadly grouped as wipes, brushes, wiper blade tools, and compressed air nozzles. Three types of wipes 
(Kimtech® task wipes (Kimberly-Clark Worldwide, Inc.), terry cloth wipes, and looped microfiber cloth 
wipes), two types of brushes (a custom brush with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) bristles and a foam 
brush), four custom wiper blade tools per seal design, and six different nozzles were evaluated. Examples 
of each method are shown in Figure 2. The wipes, brushes, and wiper blade tools all required contact with 
the seal during the cleaning procedure. For these methods, a constant contact force of approximately 11 N 
(2.5 lbf) was applied manually by the operator. All these cleaning methods were evaluated in the 
screening study with a subset selected for further evaluation in the main cleaning study.  
</p>
<p>The three types of wipes tested were commercially available products. The Kimtech® wipes (Kimtech® 
Science Precision Wipes, product number 7552) were 11.2- by 20.8-cm (4.4- by 8.2-in.) light-duty, low-
linting, single-ply sheets suitable for use in International Organization for Standardization (ISO) Class 4 
environments. The terry cloth wipes were looped fiber hand towels sourced from McMaster-Carr® Supply 
Company (part number (PN) 2576K35) that were cut into 7.6- by 12.7-cm (3- by 5-in.) pieces with a razor 
blade for testing. The microfiber cloth wipes were 40.6- by 40.6-cm (16- by 16-in.) looped fiber polyester–
nylon blend cloths (Texas Feathers, PN CMC–1616B) that were cut into 7.6- by 12.7-cm (3- by 5-in.) pieces 
for testing. The microfiber cloths were cut with a laser cutter, which had the added effect of fusing the edges 
of the pieces together to prevent fraying and fiber loss. The pieces of the terry cloth and microfiber cloth 
wipes were washed with IPA and tumbled to remove loose fibers and lint.  
</p>
<p>During the cleaning process, the wipes were held in the hand of the test operator such that the 
operator’s index and middle fingers maintained positive contact with the seal crest. The wipe was dragged 
circumferentially along the entire seal bulb in a continuous, fluid motion. As the wipe was moved, some 
of the dust collected on the wipe and some was pushed forward along the seal surface, accumulating 
ahead of the wipe. At the end of the cleaning procedure, as the wipe approached the starting position, the 
operator used a sweeping technique to help capture the dust on the wipe and prevent reapplication. 
Rotating the wipe as it was removed from the surface, as opposed to continuing in the circumferential 
direction, mitigated the accumulation of dust on the seal surface at the starting/stopping position. 
</p>
<p> </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>NASA/TM-20240005707 5 
</p>
<p>

</p>
<p>(c) Microfiber cloth wipe. (d) PTFE wiper blade. (e) Compressed air nozzle. (f) Silicone wiper blade. (g) PTFE-

<p> 
Two types of brushes were evaluated: a brush with bristles and a foam brush. The bristled brush  
</p>
<p>was a prototype brush tool from the NASA Johnson Space Center (JSC) that had been used in an EVA 
tools dust mitigation testing study. It looked like a large toothbrush and featured PTFE bristles that were 
0.089 cm (0.035 in.) in diameter by 2.5 cm (1 in.) long. The same bristled brush was used throughout the 
study, so the brush was cleaned with IPA and task wipes after each test to prepare it for the next test. The 
foam brush consisted of a tapered foam block that was 6.4 cm (2.5 in.) long, 2.5 cm (1 in.) wide, and 
1.3 cm (0.5 in.) thick and was attached to a wooden handle. It was similar to common foam brushes used 
to apply stain in woodworking. A new foam brush was used for every test. Following the same procedure 
used for the wipes, the brush was dragged circumferentially in a continuous motion along the entire seal 
bulb, and at the end of the cleaning procedure, the brush was rotated tangentially while being lifted from 
the surface.  
</p>
<p>The wiper blade tools were custom designed and fabricated at Glenn. Wiper blades of two designs (a 
flat edge and a notched edge) and two materials (S0383–70 silicone and PTFE) were evaluated for each 
seal design in the study. The flat-edge wiper blade had a straight tapered edge that contacted the seal 
surface. The notched-edge wiper blade had a notch that conformed to the seal bulb profile. Each wiper 
blade was attached to an angled steel backing plate that was then attached to a wooden handle for ease of 
use. The tool was held approximately 30° from the vertical axis, and the notch was aligned with the seal 
bulb. The tool was then dragged circumferentially along the seal surface while maintaining the 30° 
orientation. At the end of the cleaning procedure, the tool was rotated in the tangential direction and lifted </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>NASA/TM-20240005707 6 
</p>
<p>from the seal surface. Because a single wiper blade tool was used with multiple test samples, the wiper 
blade was cleaned with IPA and task wipes prior to each test and at the end of each test.  
</p>
<p>The compressed air nozzle cleaning method was the only noncontact cleaning method evaluated. Six 
commercially available nozzles were sourced from McMaster-Carr®: 
</p>
<p> 
1. Diffuser (PN 5369K55) 
2. 1/16-in.-diameter tube (PN 5446K73) 
3. 1/8-in.-diameter tube (PN 5446K71) 
4. 3/16-in.-diameter tube (PN 5450K22) 
5. Blow gun (PN 31875K31) 
6. Air knife (PN 6069K11) 
</p>
<p> 
The air nozzle tools operated using a constant stream of 103 kPa (15 psig) dry air at a flow rate of 
</p>
<p>0.991 std-m3/h (35 std-ft3/h). The tools were held approximately 45° from the vertical axis and tangential 
to the seal bulb with the air outlet of the nozzle 1 cm (0.4 in.) above the seal bulb. The cleaning procedure 
used the same circumferential motion as the other cleaning procedures. 
</p>
<p>Test Specimens 
</p>
<p>Because the exact seal designs that would be used on future lunar surface systems were not known, 
seals of two representative SOA designs were evaluated in this study: the seals for the main docking 
interface of the NASA Docking System (NDS) and the environmental seals for the Orion docking hatch. 
To aid in the throughput and feasibility of these tests, subscale versions of these seal designs were used, 
with the same cross-sectional dimensions as the full-scale flight seal designs but a smaller overall 
diameter. Further details on the test articles can be found in NASA/TM-20230015308 (Ref. 4).  
</p>
<p>Both seal designs are made of space-rated silicone materials that enable low leak rates over a wide 
operating temperature range representative of potential lunar operating conditions while also exhibiting 
low outgassing properties. However, as-fabricated silicone seals can be tacky and can generate relatively 
high adhesion forces during hatch opening or undocking if left untreated. High adhesion forces increase 
the release force needed to separate the seal interface, and they introduce the risk of the sealing material 
separating from its installation location. For silicone seals, vacuum-rated grease is commonly applied to 
the seal to reduce these adhesion loads. A thin layer of Braycote Micronic 601 EF (BP Lubricants USA 
Inc.) vacuum-rated grease was applied to the top surfaces of the seals in this test campaign. 
</p>
<p>To assess the effects of lunar dust contamination on seal leak performance, leak rates measured after 
dust was applied to and then cleaned off of the seals were compared with scaled-down leak rate 
requirements values for each seal design. The two seal designs have different geometries and material 
characteristics and may respond to cleaning differently. Therefore, the effects of seal design on the 
effectiveness of each cleaning method were also assessed. 
</p>
<p>Simulant 
</p>
<p>The lunar dust simulant used in this study was JSC–1A, a manufactured simulant designed to reflect 
the physical, chemical, and compositional properties of regolith in the lunar mare region visited by  
Apollo 14 and 15 (Ref. 6). This simulant was selected for its high glass and lithic content, which provides 
highly abrasive qualities (Ref. 7). The simulant was sieved, dried, rolled, and stored per NASA–STD–
1008 (Ref. 8). It was then sieved to include only particles smaller than 250 μm (0.0098 in.), dried for at 
least 12 h at 110 °C (230 °F) per ASTM D2216 (Ref. 9), and stored in the dry environment of the test </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>NASA/TM-20240005707 7 
</p>
<p>chamber under a rough vacuum. It was assumed that larger particles were less likely to be introduced and 
adhere to the seal surface, and previous testing has shown that small particles adhere strongly to materials 
(Ref. 10). The simulant was stored in 25-g (0.055-lbm) batches in the antechamber and rolled by hand 
immediately before testing. The simulant preparation process and storage are described in greater detail in 
NASA/TM–20210024128 (Ref. 11). 
</p>
<p>Dust Contamination and Cleaning 
</p>
<p>The seal test articles were contaminated with lunar simulant using the Uniform Dust Deposition 
System (UDDS) developed at Glenn (Ref. 11). This system, shown in Figure 3, was developed to provide 
repeatable, uniform, and automated deposition of simulants on surfaces of interest for dust mitigation 
testing. Installed inside a dry-air glovebox, the UDDS operates at ambient pressure and temperature in a 
dry environment with a relative humidity inside the chamber of less than 0.5 percent. The glovebox 
provides access to the samples with two gloves on the front face, allowing test operators to clean the test 
articles in the dry environment after dust is deposited on them. 
</p>
<p>At the start of a test, the seal test specimen (installed in its test fixture) was inserted into the test 
chamber and placed on the rotation stage of the UDDS transfer subsystem. The transfer subsystem then 
repositioned the test specimen to the center of the glovebox, under the dust deposition plate. The test article 
was rotated slowly as dust was deposited on the surface from the deposition subsystem. When the dust 
deposition process was completed, the transfer subsystem moved the test article to the imaging subsystem. 
The imaging subsystem was used to verify that the level of contamination was at or above the level at which 
the seal design did not sufficiently hold pressure, as identified in the Phase 1 testing (Ref. 4). After imaging, 
the test article was returned to the home position on the right side of the glovebox.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
Figure 3.—Uniform Dust Deposition System. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>NASA/TM-20240005707 8 
</p>
<p>For the cleaning portion of the procedure, the test operator used the glovebox gloves to position the 
desired cleaning tool on the dust-covered surface of the seal test article. The operator held the cleaning 
tool in position and applied a constant force on it while the test article rotated beneath the tool at 4 rpm. 
Five cleaning passes were completed before additional dust was deposited on the surface. This process of 
contamination and cleaning was repeated for a total of five cycles. After the final cleaning cycle, the seal 
test article was again moved to the imaging station for posttest imaging. 
</p>
<p>After imaging, the test article was removed from the chamber and prepared for leak testing. 
Additional details about the UDDS can be found in NASA/TM–20210024128 (Ref. 11). Dust 
contamination on the seal articles was characterized by percent coverage and uniformity using methods 
described in the Phase 1 test report (Ref. 4). 
</p>
<p>Leak Rate Testing  
</p>
<p>The leak rate testing of the test articles was performed in a Tenney® (TPS, LLC) environmental test 
chamber (model BTRC) where the test pressure was regulated using the Automated Pressure Equalization 
System (APES) developed at Glenn (Ref. 12). The seal article was installed into the environmental 
chamber shown in Figure 4 and allowed to reach steady-state temperature. The volume between the inner 
and outer seal was evacuated and the inside of the test fixture was pressurized to establish a 14.7-psid

<p>Adhesion Testing 
</p>
<p>As discussed previously, a thin layer of grease was applied to the top surface of each seal test 
specimen at the beginning of testing, an approach typically used to reduce adhesion forces at the sealing 
interface during hatch opening or undocking. However, cleaning the seal bulbs with the contact methods 
utilized in this campaign introduced the possibility of removing this grease layer. Without the grease 
layer, adhesion forces were expected to increase toward as-manufactured levels. To evaluate the effect 
that cleaning the seals had on adhesion force, adhesion tests were performed on the test articles. These 
tests were performed at –50 °C (–58 °F) because prior testing has shown that higher adhesion forces are 
typically measured for seals at colder temperatures (Refs. 13 and 14). 
</p>
<p>The test articles remained installed in the same test fixtures, which were connected to the Instron 
(Illinois Tool Works Inc.) load frame (model 5584) and environmental chamber (model 3119–408), shown 
in Figure 5. The load frame was outfitted with an Interface Force Measurement Solutions (Interface, Inc.) 
12,500-lbf capacity load cell with an accuracy of ±6.4 lbf. The test articles were compressed to fully 
compressed nominal loads at room temperature and were held in that configuration as the temperature was 
reduced to –50 °C. The system remained at –50 °C for 60 min before separation began. The force was then 
recorded as the test fixture plates were separated at a rate of 0.254 mm/s (0.10 in/s). 
</p>

<p>Surface Damage Evaluation 
</p>
<p>To evaluate if the test specimens were damaged during cleaning, the seal surfaces were scanned with a 
Zygo® Nexview™ NX2 optical profilometer (Zygo Corp.), shown in Figure 6. The surfaces were scanned 
for average surface roughness (Sa), root-mean-square (RMS) roughness (Sq), and maximum height (Sz). To 
prevent remaining dust contamination from skewing results, the test specimens were washed with IPA and 
dried before scanning. The scans of cleaned test specimens were compared to those for as-manufactured 
seals. To investigate the effect of severe surface damage, a 5-cm- (2-in.-) long section of an as-manufactured 
NDS seal was abraded 50 times using a custom-made JSC–1A sandpaper strip. The JSC–1A sandpaper strip 
was a cardboard strip with bulk JSC–1A adhered to it with 3M Super 77 multipurpose adhesive (3M 
Company). This test specimen was also scanned, and its surface characteristics were compared to those for 
the seal test specimens that had been contaminated with dust and then cleaned. 
</p>
<p>Results and Discussion 
Screening Study Results 
</p>
<p>When the initial set of candidate cleaning methods was evaluated in the screening study, a 
downselection was performed to the most promising methods based on two parameters: 
</p>
<p> 
1. The qualitative ability to remove simulant from the surface  
2. A subjective operator evaluation for ease of use 
</p>
<p> 
As expected, some cleaning techniques were more effective at removing lunar dust simulant from the 
</p>
<p>surfaces of the seals than others. The task wipes were effective at removing dust from the seals, but they 
were difficult to handle and tore easily, especially with a gloved hand. The terry cloth wipes were effective 
at removing dust from the seals, but they were prone to leave lint and fibers on the surface. The PTFE bristle 
brush and the foam brush were not effective at removing dust from the seals. Of the compressed air nozzles, 
the 1/16-in.-diameter compressed air tube was the most effective at removing dust from the seals. The most 
promising seal cleaning techniques were the notched PTFE wiper blade, the looped microfiber cloth wipes, 
and the 1/16-in.-diameter compressed air tube. These techniques, shown in Figure 7, were selected for 
further testing. </p>

<p>Cleaning Results 
</p>
<p>The downselected techniques and materials were used to complete the previously outlined 
contamination and cleaning study. Several observations were made during the study for each cleaning 
method. For all methods, the first cleaning cycle in a given test was much more effective at removing dust 
than the last cleaning cycle. This could be for a number of reasons, including a buildup of static charge on 
the seal surface and cleaning tool as the test progressed and the gradual loss of the grease layer that was 
initially applied to the seal. The grease on the seal served as a medium for capturing dust, trapping the 
dust on the seal surface; the cleaning tools would then remove the dust-saturated grease from the surface.  
</p>
<p>The notched PTFE wiper blade was sensitive to the angle orientation of the blade with respect to the 
surface, and this affected its cleaning effectiveness. As shown in Figure 8, when positive contact was 
maintained between the wiper blade and the seal surface, larger particles and most of the smaller particles 
were removed from the surface. However, the level of contact maintained was sensitive to user error, and 
little to no cleaning occurred where contact was not maintained, as shown in Figure 9. Another 
observation during wiper blade cleaning tests was that some simulant could remain on the seal surface at 
the end location of the cleaning pass where the tool was lifted from the surface. As discussed previously, 
this effect was mitigated by adopting a sweeping technique at the end of the cleaning pass in which the 
tool was rotated in the tangential direction before it was lifted from the surface. The effects of static 
charge induced by the dry environment were apparent on the wiper blade; dust that was collected on the 
wiper blade was observed to jump back onto the seal in front of the wiper during cleaning passes. As 
shown in Figure 10, dust also adhered to the blade itself as the tool was introduced to the dry, dusty 
environment. 
</p>

<p>Recognizing the benefits of both the notched PTFE wiper blade and microfiber cloth wipes, tests

microfiber cloth wipe was used for the remaining three cleaning passes in the cleaning cycle. Because the 
notched PTFE wiper blade removed most of the larger particles during the initial cleaning passes, the 
microfiber cloth wipe was then able to remove the remaining dust more effectively. The microfiber cloth 
wipe was visibly less saturated with dust in test trials using the combined cleaning method compared to 
test trials using only a microfiber cloth wipe, which could potentially result in less cloth required for 
cleaning for future lunar missions. The combined cleaning method was very effective at cleaning the 
seals, as shown in Figure 16.

<p>Leak Test Results 
</p>
<p>After the dust deposition and cleaning cycles were completed, leak tests were performed on the seals 
to assess their performance after cleaning. Measured leak rates were compared with the baseline values 
for clean seals and with the requirement equivalent thresholds for each seal design (Ref. 4). Each seal test 
specimen was then assigned a Pass/Fail grade based on whether its measured leak rate was below or 
above the requirement equivalent thresholds for each seal design. The equivalent leak rate requirement 
thresholds were defined as 73,280 nanograms per second (ng/s) for the Orion docking hatch seals,  
2,688 ng/s for NDS, and 451 ng/s for NDS Block 2 (NDSB2) requirements. The results are summarized 
in Table I. In some of the tests that failed, the seals leaked so much that they were unable to hold pressure 
for a long enough period to measure an accurate leak rate. The results show that the specimens that were 
cleaned with microfiber cloth wipes consistently passed leak tests. Specimens cleaned with the notched 
PTFE wiper blade passed when contact was maintained consistently during cleaning but failed in 
instances where contact was not maintained. NDS seals that were cleaned with the 1/16-in.-diameter 
compressed air tube had unacceptably high leak rates, but Orion docking hatch seals that were cleaned 
this way passed their leak tests. Seals that were cleaned using the combined cleaning method (notched 
PTFE wiper blade followed by microfiber cloth wipe) consistently passed leak tests. 
</p>

Braycote® grease applied, (3) with Braycote® grease and dust contamination, (4) after cleaning with the

comparison, the same seal test specimen was used for conditions 4 and 5.

<p>Surface Damage Evaluation 
</p>
<p>Macroscopic visual evaluation of the seals revealed no evidence of surface damage or material 
removal after they were subjected to multiple cycles of dust deposition and cleaning. However, previous 
NDS seal development tests showed that sanding the seal surface to remove flaws resulted in higher leak 
rates after exposure to ultraviolet (UV) radiation when compared to unsanded seals (Ref. 15). Based on 
that finding, there was a potential concern that seals in the lunar environment could be damaged by 
cleaning methods and would then exhibit the same effects when exposed to solar radiation. Therefore, 
further examinations of the dusted and cleaned seal surfaces were performed using the optical 
profilometer. The main metrics used to distinguish whether or not a seal had been damaged were 
increases in the average surface roughness (Sa) and changes to any surface features as compared with the 
as-manufactured seals. 
</p>
<p>The seal surface scans for the Orion docking hatch seal specimens are displayed in Figure 20. The Sa 
values for the Orion docking hatch seals were similar—approximately 0.800 µm for the as-manufactured 
seals and the seals for all tested cleaning methods—and the scans are similar in appearance to each other. 
As such, it was concluded that the sealing surfaces of the Orion docking hatch seals were not significantly 
affected by the cleaning methods used.  
</p>
<p>The seal surface scans for the NDS seal specimens are displayed in Figure 21. The as-manufactured 
NDS seal had a Sa value of 0.346 µm and exhibited circumferential mold line texture features from the 
machined mold in which the seals were molded and cured. The seal that was cleaned with the 1/16-in.-
diameter compressed air tube had a similar Sa value and exhibited the same mold line features that were </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>NASA/TM-20240005707 21 
</p>
<p>seen on the as-manufactured seals. These similarities were attributed to the compressed air being a 
noncontact cleaning method that did not alter the surface of the seal. The cleaning processes that 
contacted the seal surface, such as the microfiber cloth wipe, notched PTFE wiper blade, and combined 
cleaning method, did cause changes in the sealing surface. The average roughness values for these 
cleaning methods increased significantly, up to 0.869 µm, and the mold line features were removed. 
Similar results were seen for the seal that was sanded with the custom-made JSC–1A sandpaper strip. 
</p>
<p>The differences in surface damage that were observed for the two seal designs may be attributed to 
several factors, including differences in seal compliance and seal groove design. The NDS seals were 
made of a higher durometer (i.e., stiffer) silicone material and had a solid cross section. Therefore, when 
they were cleaned using any of the processes that contacted the seal surface, they were less compliant and 
more likely to be damaged by the cleaning process. In contrast, the Orion docking hatch seals were made 
of a lower durometer (i.e., softer) silicone material and had a hollow cross section. These seals were more 
compliant and could more readily deflect under load during cleaning, thereby minimizing surface 
damage. Additionally, the Orion docking hatch seals were encapsulated in a dovetail groove, which 
further prevented force being imparted on the seal during cleaning. 
</p>

<p>Conclusions 
Seals on future lunar surface systems must be kept clean to achieve the extremely low leak rates that 
</p>
<p>will be required to ensure crews have sufficient breathable air for extended lunar surface missions. 
Previous testing has shown that contamination and debris on current state-of-the-art seals for docking 
systems and hatches can cause the seals to exhibit leak rates that exceed their respective leak rate 
requirement thresholds. In light of this, the effectiveness and feasibility of cleaning these seals was an 
open area of interest in the community. To address this knowledge gap, researchers at the NASA Glenn 
Research Center performed tests to evaluate the ability of various cleaning methods to remove lunar 
simulant contamination from the seals and improve contaminated seal leak rates. Based on the tests and 
evaluations described in this report, the following conclusions were made: 
</p>
<p> 
• Cleaning tools can be used to effectively remove lunar dust simulant from seals and reduce their 
</p>
<p>leak rates to acceptable levels. 
</p>
<p>(f) 
</p>
<p>(b) 
</p>
<p>(d) </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>NASA/TM-20240005707 23 
</p>
<p>• Both the Orion docking hatch and the NASA Docking System seal designs can be cleaned 
effectively of lunar simulant. 
</p>
<p>• The most promising single cleaning method tested was the looped microfiber cloth, followed by 
the notched polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) wiper blade. 
</p>
<p>• A combined cleaning method approach using these two methods in sequence was the most 
effective technique for cleaning the seals. First, the notched PTFE wiper blade was used to 
remove any heavy deposits and large particles of dust. The microfiber cloth was then used to 
remove the remaining dust from the surface. With this approach, the notched PTFE wiper blade 
reduces the amount of dust the microfiber cloth needs to remove, thereby delaying contamination 
saturation of the cloth, extending the cloth’s usability, and potentially reducing mass requirements 
given that smaller quantities of cloth would be needed for use over multiple missions. 
</p>
<p>• Adhesion forces for greased seals increased after multiple cycles of dust contamination and 
cleaning, but they were still lower than those for seals in the as-manufactured condition. 
Reapplication of Braycote® grease successfully reduced adhesion forces to precontaminated 
levels. Applying grease with the cleaning wipes may improve cleaning effectiveness and reduce 
overall cleaning time needed. 
</p>
<p>• Mechanical cleaning techniques can cause some surface damage, which may accelerate seal 
degradation, especially if the seals are subsequently exposed to solar radiation. 
</p>
<p> 
Given that the cleaning methods and processes were performed in a laboratory environment, process 
development for in situ seal cleaning methods should give consideration to the additional challenges 
present in the lunar environment, including limited and harsh lighting, limited mobility and dexterity, 
increased electrostatic charging and adhesion, and inaccessible areas. 
</p>
