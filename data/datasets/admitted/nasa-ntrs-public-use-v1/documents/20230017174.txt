Ascent vehicles in the developmental stages of a launch vehicle program are instrumented with
Developmental Flight Instrumentation (DFI) sensors. These sensors establish a link between
a vehicle and engineers on the ground to communicate conditions experienced during the
ascent and these data are then compared to preflight predictions used in the design process.
The aerodynamic, acoustic, thermal, and structural data are either telemetered to ground
stations during the ascent or stored on the vehicle for post-flight recovery and archived at the
Huntsville Operations Support Center (HOSC). Following NASA’s Artemis I Space Launch
System (SLS) launch on November 16, 2022, data from three separate programs are available at
the HOSC: Space Shuttle Program (Space Transport System (STS)), Constellation Program
(Ares I-X), and Artemis Program (SLS). Availability of these data presents a unique opportunity
to examine DFI data from three distinct vehicles and analyze the broad impact of the DFI data
on the understanding of transonic aerodynamics. Classical spectrogram and Empirical Mode
Decomposition techniques were used to present data in aerodynamically analogous regions on
each vehicle. On the SLS and Ares I-X, a region downstream of the Launch Abort System
motor was chosen. Comparing SLS and the STS, a region downstream of booster forward
attach hardware was selected as an analogous flow region in addition to other regions of interest.
Although similarities in flow features on three vehicles were identified, some challenges in the
comparison were also encountered, especially due to poor temporal and spatial resolution of
Shuttle measurements.

Introduction
On November 16th, 2022, NASA launched Artemis I, an inaugural flight of the Space Launch System (SLS) with



an uncrewed Orion capsule. The vehicle was densely instrumented with Developmental Flight Instrumentation (DFI)
sensors. These sensors established a link between the vehicle and engineers on the ground to communicate conditions
experienced during the ascent for comparison to and validation of preflight predictions used in the design process.
The aerodynamic, acoustic, thermal, and structural data were telemetered to ground stations during the ascent and
were transferred to the Huntsville Operations Support Center (HOSC) for dissemination and long-term storage. With
the Artemis I mission successfully completed, data collected from the flight were added to a vast HOSC archive,
which contains DFI measurement from other NASA vehicles. These include Ares I-X, an experimental flight from the
Constellation program, and multiple flights of Space Transport System (STS) – better known as the "Shuttle" for short.
The availability of these data provides an attractive opportunity to compare measurements from all flights and assess
how well the instrumentation captured the ascent aerodynamics. Also, these three vehicles were tested at the NASA
Langley Research Center Transonic Dynamics Tunnel, a primary test facility for the author’s experimental work, adding
to the natural interest in this comparison [1–4].



Some preliminary comparison work had started even before launch of Artemis I. During the two SLS Green Run hot
fire tests, wet dress rehearsals, and a tanking test, ice formation covering pressure sensors on the core was observed. The
ice formation was attributed to the cryogenic temperature of the liquid oxygen and hydrogen propellants, which cooled
the sensors mounted on the core to freezing temperatures. Although the external pressure sensors were embedded
in the Thermal Protection System (TPS) foam, their orifices were exposed to the humidity in the ambient air, which
once cooled, allowed for ice formation. During prelaunch tests, the sensors blocked by ice exhibited an unusual trend,
deviating from the expected ambient pressure value. The Space Shuttle external tank also contained liquid oxygen and
hydrogen propellants and was sprayed with TPS foam for thermal insulation. It was natural to analyze the Shuttle
external tank pressure data to see any similar pressure measurement trends in an effort to better predict which sensors
are unreliable. This interest in the comparative analysis of DFI data from various vehicles remained and was revisited
while working on the Artemis I flight data. For example, comparison between Space Shuttle and the SLS aerothermal
environments was performed by Mehta et al.[5].



The author’s original intent of the article was to conduct a comprehensive review of the unsteady aerodynamics for
three vehicles: SLS, Ares I-X, and Shuttle based on the DFI data. However, after reviewing the Shuttle data, the task
proved impossible due to lack of unsteady aerodynamic sensors on the Shuttle external tank. Instead, an attempt was
made to compare only a few similar flow features between vehicles. To do this comparison, regions of interest on each
vehicle were identified that exhibited analogous flow behavior. The first one is a region on the nose of the SLS and Ares
I-X where the pressure sensors captured the vortex shedding from motor nozzles of the Launch Abort System (LAS).
The second is a region downstream of the booster Forward Attach Hardware (FAH) on both the SLS and the Shuttle
where strong vortex shedding is expected. Other regions are also presented, which were of aerodynamic interest.



Flight data for all three vehicles are presented using two approaches. A classical spectrogram is used to visualize
the frequency content of the signal as a function of time. An empirical mode decomposition (EMD) approach was
used to visualize the waveform of the same measurement. A spectrogram is a well established approach for presenting
frequency and magnitude content of a transient signal, including pressures from an ascent vehicle. EMD, however, was
predominantly used in analysis of cyclic signals, such as ones present in pulsatile flows [6], wind-wave interactions, and
tidal flows [7]. However, the external pressure of an ascent vehicle cannot be classified typically as cyclic. Moreover, in
the case of absolute pressure sensors, a signal contains a strong trend as the vehicle ascends from atmospheric ambient
pressure to vacuum. Therefore, some challenges were encountered in using this approach. The success and deficiencies
of this method are discussed.



Data analysis methodology
The EMD method is briefly introduced as described by Huang et al. [7]. The method is usually used to decompose



a cyclic signal with a wide frequency content into mode shapes called Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs). This process
is demonstrated using a noisy signal, 𝑌 (𝑡), shown with a black line in Figure 1a. The upper bound of the original
signal, shown as a red dashed line in the figure, is calculated by fitting a spline curve connecting local maxima points of
the original signal. Similarly, the lower bound is found using local minima. The mean curve of the upper and lower
bounds, 𝑚11, is then calculated and is shown in the figure with a blue line. The subscript 11 indicates that this is the
first mean curve used in calculating the first IMF. Ideally, 𝑚11 would be used to calculate the IMF, ℎ11, by subtracting
𝑚11 from 𝑌 (𝑡). However, 𝑚11 may not be the true mean of the signal since the process described thus far does not

The calculated IMF is subtracted from the original signal to reveal the measurement devoid of highest frequency
fluctuations. The new signal can be used as an input into the process described above to find all other IMFs, each with
its own characteristic frequency. A sample of this calculation is shown in Figures 1c and 1e, resulting in the second and
third IMFs shown in Figures 1d and 1f. The process can be repeated until the signal lacks all fluctuations and no local
extrema are present in the waveform. The resultant waveform is a trend of the original signal. Combining the trend and
all IMFs will reconstruct the original signal. The ability to isolate and add high-frequency signal components alone
presents a benefit of thr EMD method for flight data analysis. By adding only the high-frequency IMFs and excluding
the trend and some of the low-frequency IMFs, a signal is essentially detrended without a need for any windowing.
Eliminating the need to optimize the window size by using the EMD approach is a benefit over moving an average
detrending approach, which relies on the window size selection.



In the discussion of flight data below, each individual IMF is not shown. Instead, five frequency bins were defined.
All IMFs whose characteristic frequency falls into a particular bin are added together. As a result, for all flight analyses,
five waveform plots will be presented with each containing all IMFs that fall into a consistent characteristic frequency
range. Unless otherwise stated, the IMF plot y-axis scale may vary.



The EMD is often used for analysis of the cyclic signals. In contrast, the external pressure data captured during
the ascent portion of the flight may not be described as cyclic and may cause problems in applying this method. One
issue may arise from the strong mean trend seen in an absolute pressure sensor. The mean of such sensor data would
monotonically decreased from the atmospheric pressure to the vacuum value as the vehicle ascends from seal-level to
space, creating a potential problem of finding extremas. To ensure that at least one set of extreme values is found, a linear



3











trend is subtracted from flight data. This will not affect any of the high-frequency waveforms and will only affect the
trend and IMFs of lowest frequency, which are unimportant in the unsteady aerodynamics discussion presented below.
Another issue may arise if a certain frequency content is present only in a portion of the time series. In the context of
providing binned IMFs, this means that the presence of a fluctuating phenomena with a short burst of frequency content
that is significantly different from the rest of the waveform may skew the calculation of the characteristic frequency and
artificially bump the waveform to a different frequency bin.

Space Launch System
Artemis I, a test flight of the Space Launch System and Orion vehicle, was launched on November 16, 2022 on a



highly successful test flight of the integrated vehicle and spacecraft. The entire stack was instrumented with thousands of
DFI sensors. External pressure was measured using over three hundred (388) transducers and microphones distributed
between the core, Launch Abort System (LAS), and two boosters. Figure 3 shows the Artemis I geometry and the
locations of the pressure sensors color-coded by their sampling frequency. Static pressure sensors whose sampling rate
was set to below 100Hz is represented by black spheres. The unsteady pressure sensors with sampling rate between 100
and 5kHz are represented by blue spheres. The acoustic sensors, whose sampling rate is above 5kHz are shown with red
spheres. Sensor locations were chosen based on numerous wind-tunnel tests [4] to capture the regions of aerodynamic
interest. Rings of sensors placed at various longitudinal stations along the vehicle allow azimuthal integration of
pressure to yield orthogonal sectional forces for assessment of buffet forcing functions [8].



The first region of interest is associated with vortex shedding from the LAS motor nozzles. The LAS is intended to
detach the crew module from the rest of the stack in case of emergency by firing three solid rocket motors. The motor
nozzles protrude from the spire on top of the vehicle and shed vortices resulting in pressure fluctuations, which can be
sensed by downstream pressure sensors. Figure 4a shows the spectrogram and the binned IMFs for a sensor positioned
downstream of the LAS motors whose the approximate location is indicated in Figure 3 as SLS P1. The spectrogram
shows a presence of the signal increasing in frequency with time, consistent with expectations of the Strouhal shedding
frequency to increase with flow speed. The frequency of shedding appears to start in Bin 4 and increase to Bin 5 in
the spectrogram. The wave forms in the IMF representation of the signal show a similar trend, with the amplitude of
fluctuations increasing in both Bin 4 and Bin 5. A terminal shock is established near the region of LAS motor nozzles,

Comparing the analysis using spectrogram and the IFM waveforms, a consistent story appears. However, the two can
give different details about the aerodynamic flow phenomena present. To illustrate how the two approaches supplement
each other, another interesting transonic phenomenon was analyzed that was captured by an external pressure sensor on
the SLS left booster, whose approximate location is indicated as SLS P2 in Figure 3. In Figure 4b, both the spectrogram
and IMFs show the broadband fluctuation amplitude increasing leading up to sonic condition. Just prior 𝑡𝑀=1, the
spectrogram exhibits an increased intensity, which spans Bin 2 through 3 frequency ranges. The associated IMF
waveforms also appear to contain a spike near 𝑡𝑀=1. Two insert plots in Figure 4b IMF plots show a zoomed-in view of
the spikes and reveal a clear oscillatory waveform. The frequency associated with these fluctuations suggests the flow
oscillates between two modes possibly due to the location of the shock fluctuating between upstream of the sensor and
downstream of the sensor. Following the bimodal oscillations, the content for all frequencies seems to be attenuated,
consistent with a terminal shock traveling further downstream. This is an unusual bimodal transition behavior in an
accelerating flight, which is sometimes seen in wind-tunnel testing loitering at a Mach number close to a sonic condition
[9]. Surprisingly this behavior was observed on the Artemis I flight at some DFI pressure sensors in this forward booster
region. The examples shown in Figure 4b illustrate that the EMD approach is convenient in quickly identifying the
signal waveform, which may not be deduced from the spectrogram alone. The mismatch in the frequency of oscillations
is observed. Spectrogram shows the dominant fluctuations occurring at frequency corresponding to Bin 3. However,
both Bin 3 and Bin 4 IMFs contain this oscillatory fluctuation indicating the the current method to calculate IMF
characteristic frequency needs to be improved.



The next region of interest is located in the vicinity of the booster Forward Attach Hardware (FAH). Figures 4c and
4d show the response of two sensors both downstream of the longitudinal location of the booster tip. The IMFs in the
two figures are plotted on using the same scale to clearly show the difference in the signal of two sensors. The sensor
shown in Figure 4c is located upstream of the FAH and labeled in Figure 3 as SLS P3. Although a small decrease in
fluctuations can be seen just prior to time 𝑡𝑀=1, no signature of the terminal shock followed by a decrease in fluctuations
is evident. Instead, the high-frequency fluctuations, although relatively small in amplitude, appear to grow well into
supersonic flight of the vehicle. The lack of a terminal shock can be supported by the shadowgraph image from a recent
wind tunnel test [10] shown in Figure 5a, which captured the formation of a bow shock around the booster nose. The
shock impinges upstream of the sensor SLS P3 adding intensity to the turbulent boundary layer, which is evident by
increased high-frequency fluctuations in seen in the associated IMFs. This shock is pinned to the booster tip and is
prevented from traveling downstream.



The sensor whose measurements are shown in Figure 4d is located downstream of the FAH at a location labeled SLS
P4 in Figure 3. The sensor captured a dominant aerodynamic effect on the SLS vehicle, which was caused by the vortex
shedding originating from the booster FAH. This strong disturbance was sensed by the instrumentation downstream of
the FAH along the entire booster-core gap length. Figure 4d shows several lines of high-intensity fluctuations in the
subsonic region of the spectrogram plot associated with the harmonics of this shedding. Around the time associated
with sonic transition, an increase in fluctuations is observed in Bins 2 and 3 in both the spectrogram and the IMF
waveforms. The frequency and amplitude of the fluctuations appear to increase following the transition to the supersonic
flight. The increase of the fluctuation amplitude may be attributed to the shock, which forms abound the booster nose
and interacts with and amplifies the FAH vortex shedding. The highest amplitude fluctuations seem to reside in Bin 4
according to the spectrogram. However, looking at the IMF waveforms, highest fluctuations appear in Bin 5.



Ares I-X

on later Ares flights as a part of Constellation program. The vehicle was instrumented with high- and low-frequency
external pressure transducers whose approximate locations are shown in Figure 6. The sensors with sampling rates of
about 10Hz were designated as static pressures and are indicated by black spheres in the figure. The low-frequency

Space Transport System
The Space Transport System, better known as the Space Shuttle, is a multibody vehicle designed to ferry crew and



cargo in a reusable orbiter. The ascent of the vehicle was powered using three main engines on the orbiter and assisted
by two solid rocket boosters. During the ascent, the fuel for the orbiter main engines was supplied by an external tank.
Although each component of the system was instrumented with external pressure transducers, the present discussion
will focus on the external tank instrumentation. Figure 8 shows the approximate geometry of the STS external tank
and the location of DFI sensors. The sensor density was sparse compared to the instrumentation on the SLS and Ares
I-X vehicles, with half of all the pressure transducers and microphones installed on the base of the external tank. The
pressure sensors were sampled at a low rate of 1Hz and 10Hz, which is insufficient to perform any meaningful unsteady
aerodynamics analysis. Moreover, no ring of sensors is available to perform azimuthal integration. Data from a few
microphones sampled at 50kHz are available. The STS datasets do offer a benefit of multiple launches. In the present
discussion, data from five STS missions listed in Table 1 were analyzed.



Both the SLS and the Shuttle use solid rocket boosters to provide the majority of the thrust during liftoff and ascent.
In fact, the solid rocket boosters are nearly identical, with SLS boosters having one more segment than the boosters used
on the Shuttle. As was seen in Figure 4d, the FAH on the SLS produced a strong vortex shedding that was sensed by
downstream pressure transducers. The general trend appears to show increased high-frequency fluctuation once the
vehicle is supersonic, as previously described. To observe the presence of this behavior on the Shuttle, a set of sensors,
one downstream and one upstream of the FAH, was used. Both sensors were sampled at a relatively low sampling

Discussion and Conclusion
From the discussion above in the context of flight instrumentation, it is apparent that the SLS and ARES I-X flight



data measurement rates are sufficient to resolve the unsteady aerodynamics. The sensor density and their distribution
in rings allows for integration of pressures to calculate sectional loads and perform cross-channel analysis. On the
STS, however, only a few microphones were available to capture limited unsteady aerodynamics. The rest of the
pressure sensors were sampled at a low rate and were sparsely positioned on the external tank. This limitation of the
contemporary state-of-the-art is summarized in Figure 11a, which shows the number of sensors on a vehicle plotted
versus the associated sample rate. Both the SLS and Ares I-X were instrumented with sensors, whose sample rate
ranged from static pressure measurements to acoustic measurements. The STS was missing sensors capable of sampling
in what today is considered appropriate unsteady pressure measurement sample rate range. Figure 11b shows the sensor
count and their associated engineering unit resolution. The resolution was computed by finding minimum, nonzero
value of the signal time series difference in each channel. It is assumed that the value would represent a bit-toggle,
and therefore, is the smallest increment that the signal could be resolved in. From this figure, it is apparent that the
resolution for all three vehicles appears to fall in the same range.



Figure 11c shows a number of measurements for each vehicle per unit time and surface area. This quantity was
computed by adding the sample rates of all sensors and dividing by the surface area of the instrumented vehicle or
component. In the case of the SLS, the surface area was taken of the entire vehicle excluding boosters. On the Ares I-X,
the surface area of the entire rocket was taken. On the STS, the area of the external tank was taken. Also on the plot, is
shown the same quantity for a typical wind-tunnel test used for buffet analysis. This quantity is a good way to illustrate
the combined information about the sensor distribution on the vehicle and their sample rate. Again, it can be seen the
STS is inferior to the SLS and Ares I-X in terms of the DFI pressure instrumentation.
