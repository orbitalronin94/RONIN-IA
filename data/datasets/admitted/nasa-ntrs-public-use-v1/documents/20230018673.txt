Preliminary Analysis of the Effective Vertical Resolution of Rawinsondes at US Space Force Ranges
Jake Williams, Frank Leahy, Nathan Curtis


NASA/Marshall Space Flight Center


Huntsville, AL


Introduction:


Wind data are used by space launch vehicles in generating ascent trajectory profile and evaluating vehicle performance during ascent through Earth’s atmosphere.


It is important to characterize the capability of the wind measurement system to ensure mission success and crew safety.


One important aspect is the effective vertical resolution (EVR), which represents the smallest signal dominated wind feature (wavelength) the system can detect.


Wind features with wavelengths above the EVR will be included in vehicle load assessments, while those below the EVR are removed and accounted for through other methods (e.g., discrete gust loads).


The US Space Force Ranges at Vandenberg Space Force Base in California (Western Range) and Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida (Eastern Range) are replacing rawinsonde systems in 2024.


Preliminary EVR estimates of new rawinsonde system (Vaisala MW51) were conducted to assess performance against current system (Lockheed Martin/Sippican LMS6).


Results: 


The two figures on the left show mean PSDs and CSD for the U (westerly) and V (southerly) wind components.


If the wind measurement system was perfect (i.e, no system noise), the PSD would be linear, with higher power at the longer wavelengths and lower power at the shorter wavelengths.


The PSDs show a departure from the linear trend around 400 m. This is a result of filters in the rawinsonde system processing, which remove noise due to balloon pendulum motion.


The figure on the right shows the coherence squared of both the U and V wind components. Note that longer wavelengths are highly coherent (values near 1), while smaller wavelengths are incoherent (values near 0).


The plot shows that coherence begins to drop around 1000 m, and crosses 0.25 (horizontal dashed red line) around 300 to 325 m. This indicates wind features with wavelengths greater than 300 to 325 m are dominated by signal, while wavelengths less than that are dominated by noise. 


Data and Methodology:


To perform EVR assessments, it is ideal to have two simultaneous measurements of the same wind environment.


For this assessment, test data of the new rawinsonde system was obtained from 24 near simultaneously released rawinsonde pairs provided in 1-second intervals.


To perform an EVR analysis, the data must be converted to constant altitude intervals. 30 meters was chosen since it closely matches the altitude interval NASA uses for launch operations.


EVR is determined by performing a coherence analysis of wind component data between the first and second profile in the balloon pairs. Coherence is similar to correlation coefficient (values between 0 and 1), but in the frequency/wavenumber domain.


The first step in the coherence analysis to calculate the power spectral density (PSD) of wind component data in each profile of the rawinsonde pairs. The first and second PSDs are noted as PSD1 and PSD2.

Conclusions:


Coherence analysis of 24 near simultaneous releases shows EVR estimates for the new rawinsonde system is approximately 300 to 325 m.


This exceeds the EVR of the previous rawinsonde system, and meets NASA’s requirement for low resolution wind EVR (350 m) for NASA’s Artemis mission launch operations


The data in this analysis is from preliminary testing, and the results are not considered official.


Formal testing of the new system will be done at the US Space Force Eastern Range in summer of 2024. NASA/MSFC will then conduct an official assessment of the system before operational acceptance to support launch operations.
