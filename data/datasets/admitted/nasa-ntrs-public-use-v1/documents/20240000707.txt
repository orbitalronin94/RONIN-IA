<p>LUCY MISSION DESIGN STRATEGY IN A DYNAMIC 
OPERATIONS ENVIRONMENT FROM LAUNCH THROUGH 
</p>
<p>FIRST ASTEROID ENCOUNTER 
</p>
<p>James V. McAdams,* Dale R. Stanbridge,* Daniel R. 
</p>
<p>Wibben,* Andrew H. Levine,* Jeroen L. Geeraert,* Joel 
T. Fischetti,* Coralie D. Adam,* and Kevin Berry† 
</p>
<p>Lucy, a 12-year long NASA Discovery-class mission which launched on October 
16, 2021, will fly by several Jupiter-Trojan asteroids at the L4 and L5 Lagrange 
points. Prior to the L4 Trojan encounters in 2027 and 2028, Lucy will utilize two 
Earth gravity-assist flybys and fly by two main-belt asteroids. After the L4 en-
</p>
<p>counters a third Earth flyby will set up the L5 Trojan encounter in 2033. This 
paper summarizes the baseline mission plan with focus on the spacecraft trajec-
</p>
<p>tory and the corresponding essential trajectory correction maneuvers, as well as 
how the Lucy team adapted to many unforeseen changes to the spacecraft config-
uration and added an asteroid target of opportunity that required changes to the 
</p>
<p>baseline mission plan. Refinement of asteroid flyby science requirements after 
</p>
<p>launch also required updates to the baseline Lucy trajectory. 
</p>
<p>INTRODUCTION 
</p>
<p>The NASA Discovery Program’s Lucy mission, which launched on the first day of its October 
2021 twenty-day launch period has completed the first 20% of its 11.4-year trajectory1 including 
</p>
<p>the first of three Earth gravity-assist flybys and the first of seven planned asteroid flybys. The mis-
</p>
<p>sion-enabling Earth flybys not only lower launch energy and delta-V (ΔV, also known as velocity 
</p>
<p>change) requirements, but also enable the first-ever flybys of Trojan asteroids2 near Jupiter’s dis-
</p>
<p>tance from the Sun in both the L4 and L5 spatial regions about 60º ahead of and 60º behind Jupiter’s 
</p>
<p>orbital location. 
</p>
<p>After providing the overall mission design plan as of launch, this paper will chronicle an unu-
</p>
<p>sually high number of spacecraft operational and encounter science changes that the flight opera-
tions teams had to understand and then adapt ground or onboard processes and software. Each 
</p>
<p>change either in spacecraft performance or a science requirement resulted in changes to operational 
</p>
<p>constraints such as trajectory correction maneuver (TCM) implementation or adding TCMs to tar-
</p>
<p>get a nearby asteroid early in cruise phase or changing Earth or asteroid flyby periapsis distances. 
</p>
<p>Note that the Navigation and Mission Design scope here of various post-launch changes is only a 
</p>
<p>fraction of total mission impact due to issues that were unknown before launch.  
</p>
<p>Multiple post-launch updates significantly affected one or more aspects of the Lucy mission’s 
</p>
<p>scope including mission design and navigation. Shortly after launch flight operators discovered that 
</p>

<p>one of the two large solar arrays did not fully deploy, which led to in-depth investigation, maxim-
</p>
<p>izing the array’s deployment, culminating in the development and implementation of modified 
</p>
<p>flight operations. Less than a year after launch mission leadership authorized analysis to determine 
</p>
<p>costs and potential benefits of flying close enough to observe a small inner main belt asteroid that 
</p>
<p>came within 64,000 km of the nominal mission trajectory before re-targeting the baseline trajectory. 
Some months later risk reduction trades considered the benefits of splitting the first bipropellant 
</p>
<p>main engine deep-space maneuver (DSM) into two parts separated by three days. Finally, scientific 
</p>
<p>analysis yielded adjustments to periapsis range or encounter timing for most of the asteroid flybys. 
</p>
<p>The solar array anomaly3 (SAA) resulted in more conservative implementation of the earliest 
</p>
<p>TCMs, a slightly higher perigee altitude constraint for two of the three Earth flybys to reduce drag-
induced torques on Lucy and updated onboard thruster control software. The trajectory optimiza-
</p>
<p>tion, orbit determination, optical navigation, and multiple science and other engineering analyses 
</p>
<p>favored the low-cost addition of a targeted flyby of asteroid 1999 VD57 which was later named 
</p>
<p>(152830) Dinkinesh. This 1 November 2023 asteroid flyby served as a highly useful practice for 
</p>
<p>later asteroid flybys during the mission. Science planning also revealed more optimal flyby periap-
sis altitudes and encounter times for some of the Trojan asteroid encounters. The mission’s largest 
</p>
<p>delta-V expenditure by a significant factor coincided with the first use of the bipropellant main 
</p>
<p>engine in early February 2024. Lessons learned from the Juno Jupiter orbiter mission and other 
</p>
<p>analysis led to a decision to split this maneuver in two parts of 100 m/s delta-V followed by 816 
</p>
<p>m/s delta-V about three days later. 
</p>
<p>While it is normal for many of the post-launch changes noted above to occur during a mission, 
</p>
<p>all these changes occurring within the first 20% of the nominal mission duration provides evidence 
</p>
<p>for cohesive and highly experienced engineering, science, and management teams. The paper ends 
with an actual versus planned performance of maneuvers and solar system body flybys completed 
</p>
<p>from launch through the end of 2023. 
</p>
<p>PRE-LAUNCH MISSION PLAN 
</p>
<p>The Lucy mission’s four operational phases provide a chronological means of segmenting the 
</p>
<p>mission’s many solar system body encounters as well as the DSMs and deterministic and statistical 
</p>
<p>TCMs. Launch phase started at Cape Canaveral in Florida with a characteristic energy (C3) of 
</p>
<p>28.635 km2/s2 (maximum allowable of 29.2 km2/s2) and extended until 30 days after launch at the 
</p>
<p>nominal TCM 1 epoch. Initial Cruise phase continues through TCM 3 (also called DSM 1) and the 
first Earth gravity-assist (EGA 1) flyby one year after launch, then a February 2024 DSM 2 will 
</p>
<p>target the second EGA flyby 2.1 years after EGA 1 which will target the flyby of main-belt asteroid 
</p>
<p>Donaldjohanson in April 2025. After completing this rehearsal for the later Trojan asteroid flybys, 
</p>
<p>DSM 3 will set up the L4 Trojan Flyby phase by targeting the Jupiter Trojan Eurybates encounter 
</p>
<p>in August 2027 and the Trojan Polymele encounter just 34 days later. The L4 Trojan Flyby phase 
</p>
<p>will end with DSMs 4 and 5 targeting Trojans Leucus and Orus, respectively, in April 2028 and 
November 2028. The final mission phase, Late Cruise and L5 Trojan Flyby, will utilize DSM 5 and 
</p>
<p>a later third EGA flyby in December 2030 to target a flyby of Jupiter Trojan binary Patroclus and 
</p>
<p>Menoetius in March 2033. 
</p>
<p>The TCM schedule at launch minimized risk of not meeting flyby target accuracy requirements 
</p>
<p>by using 33 primary maneuver opportunities, one pre-flyby near-Earth object collision avoidance 
</p>
<p>contingency maneuver per EGA flyby, a primary and secondary cleanup TCM after each EGA, and 
</p>
<p>one contingency TCM five days before each asteroid flyby. Table 1 lists TCM epochs and their 
</p>
<p>corresponding target events for the 16 October 2021 launch period open. Statistical TCMs will 
strategically occur 30 and 10 days before each EGA and 30 and 7 days before each asteroid en-
</p>
<p>counter. With the L4 Trojan Eurybates and Polymele encounters just 34 days apart, the second-to-</p>

</p>

<p>Overview of Key Trajectory Events 
</p>
<p>The first planned course correction would be TCM 1 30 days after launch. About six months 
</p>
<p>after launch DSM 1 targeted EGA 1 to occur one year after launch. The ΔV for DSM 1 was small 
</p>
<p>for the first half of the 21-day launch period. With the launch at the first launch opportunity in this 
</p>
<p>launch period, DSM 1 therefore used lower thrust hydrazine TCM thrusters. To prevent the space-
</p>
<p>craft having a probability impacting the Earth greater than 1% after each trajectory correction, the 
EGA target is biased from the optimal target on approach, and this target offset decreases as the b-
</p>
<p>plane radial errors shrink. This “walk-in strategy” lowers the likelihood of Earth impact if the abil-
</p>
<p>ity to conduct TCMs ceased before an EGA. An initial EGA target offset and one or more perigee 
</p>
<p>target walk-in (from biased off optimal to optimal) maneuvers were planned between DSM 1 and 
</p>
<p>EGA 1. This strategy was planned for each of the three EGAs such that, with all predicted errors 
and trajectory perturbations accounted for, the probability of coming within 125 km of Earth’s 
</p>
<p>surface (atmospheric entry approximation) has less than a 1% probability of Earth impact without 
</p>
<p>any subsequent TCM. The minimum target altitude for EGA 1 ranged from 300 km to 2390 km 
</p>
<p>across the launch period. Some perigee altitude and asteroid periapsis range values changed after 
</p>
<p>launch. </p>

<p>HOW THE SOLAR ARRAY ANOMALY ALTERED OPERATIONS 
</p>
<p>The first of two primary post-launch mission plan changes arose when, shortly after launch, one 
</p>
<p>of two large solar arrays did not fully deploy3. Although the +Y-axis solar array failed to latch at 
</p>
<p>the 100% deployed position, careful investigation led to planning, testing, and implementation of 
</p>
<p>multiple solar array re-deployment attempts (RDAs) within the first 14 months after launch. Anal-
</p>
<p>ysis revealed that deployment of close to 98% was achieved after completing the last planned RDA. 
With a firm projection of an acceptable power margin throughout the nominal mission and analysis 
</p>
<p>indicating only slightly compromised spacecraft stability throughout structural stressing events 
</p>
<p>such as main engine maneuvers, the maneuver design and trajectory optimization had to adjust to 
</p>
<p>this off-nominal new operational mode.  
</p>
<p>Course-Correction Maneuver Updates with an Unlatched Solar Array 
</p>
<p>The SAA investigation led to several changes to the course-correction plan and Earth flyby 
</p>
<p>targeting. Uncertainties in solar array deployment resulted in a more cautious operations approach 
</p>
<p>that included performing smaller test maneuvers rather than implementing the pre-launch course-
</p>
<p>correction maneuver plan. The combination of highly accurate launch vehicle performance and 
</p>
<p>caution in minimizing torques and thruster accelerations on a spacecraft with a floppy unlatched 
</p>
<p>solar panel, the first two planned TCMs at one and two months after launch were cancelled. The 
first completed TCM, TCM 2a at 4.5 months after launch, was added after launch to the schedule 
</p>
<p>for Deep Space Network (DSN) tracking and flight operations design and implementation. Instead 
</p>
<p>of having a single TCM 3 with delta-V near 6 m/s several months after launch, the Project took a 
</p>
<p>more conservative approach to study spacecraft response to a 1.25 m/s delta-V 4.5 months after 
</p>
<p>launch co-designed with a 4.2 m/s delta-V 3.2 months later (launch + 7.7 months). Not only did 
this approach provide helpful information about spacecraft response to smaller thruster firings a 
</p>
<p>few months before the next TCM, but it provided more time for the SAA review board to under-
</p>
<p>stand the status of the partially deployed solar panel and to formulate plans to deploy the solar array 
</p>
<p>closer to the latched condition. 
</p>
<p>Monte Carlo analysis of deterministic and statistical maneuver delta-Vs4 resulted in cancellation 
</p>
<p>of multiple maneuvers with re-optimizing the remainder of the mission’s trajectory5,6 to minimize 
</p>
<p>the small propellant cost of the cancelled maneuvers and the associated offset from the optimal 
</p>
<p>perigee target achieved at the launch plus one year first Earth flyby. So instead of achieving the 
lowest possible deviation from the optimal EGA 1 perigee target, statistical TCMs 5, 6, 7, and 8 
</p>
<p>were cancelled at 30 and 10 days before and 17 and 37 days after perigee, respectively. The TCM 
</p>
<p>3 cleanup maneuver, TCM 4, also used an off-optimal EGA 1 perigee target to satisfy a Project 
</p>
<p>requirement to keep the probability of Earth impact below 1% if no more thruster activity were </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 7 
</p>
<p>possible before EGA 1 for any maneuver that directly targets Earth perigee. This was assessed both 
</p>
<p>by analytical methods that confirmed 10 or fewer trajectory samples out of 1000 intersecting a B-
</p>
<p>plane representation of Earth plus 125 km altitude to simulate atmospheric entry and spacecraft 
</p>
<p>breakup during re-entry. And to keep the EGA 1 minus 30 days TCM-5 delta-V from becoming 
</p>
<p>large, yet another TCM not requested before launch, a new TCM 4a was added to the mission plan 
at about 2.5 months before EGA 1 to return the biased EGA 1 perigee target to the optimal EGA 1 
</p>
<p>perigee target since the ellipse of potential dispersed trajectories at EGA 1 perigee yielded less than 
</p>
<p>a 1% Earth impact probability. A highly accurate TCM 4a execution and careful trajectory re-
</p>
<p>optimization through the remainder of the mission contributed to the cancellation of the next four 
</p>
<p>statistical TCMs as mentioned above. Table 3, which depicts the cancellation of six TCMs (1, 2, 5, 
6, 7, and 8) and the addition of two TCMs (2a and 4a) within about 13 months after launch, also 
</p>
<p>provides the timing and target events for each maneuver. 
</p>

<p>HOW THE ASTEROID DINKINESH FLYBY ENHANCED EARLY OPERATIONS 
</p>
<p>The second of two significant post-launch mission plan changes occurred when, about a year 
</p>
<p>after launch, an opportunity arose to fly by a small (~0.8 km diameter) S-type inner main-belt 
</p>
<p>asteroid about two years after launch. In late August 2022 a scientist identified an asteroid with a </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 8 
</p>
<p>precisely known orbit that would pass close to Lucy’s planned trajectory at least one year before 
</p>
<p>the April 2025 “operations rehearsal” flyby of the ~4-km diameter main-belt asteroid Donaldjohan-
</p>
<p>son. Careful study of this asteroid (identified as 1999 VD57 and later named Dinkinesh) flyby 
</p>
<p>opportunity revealed in January 2023 that it would serve as a valuable test of science and operations 
</p>
<p>plans for later Jupiter Trojan asteroid flybys. NASA approved this 4.4 km/s spacecraft-asteroid 
relative encounter velocity encounter in February 2023. The geometry of the Dinkinesh encounter, 
</p>
<p>with a high approach phase angle of 114°, and corresponding departure phase angle of 62°, is close 
</p>
<p>to those of the Leucus and Orus encounters with approach phase angles of 100° and 121° respec-
</p>
<p>tively. This provided the Science and Optical Navigation teams with a more realistic scenario to 
</p>
<p>prepare for the Trojan encounters with the primary rationale being that this new flyby would better 
test the Terminal Tracking system that optimizes image quality of the observed asteroid.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Costing only about 4% of the full mission’s delta-V margin, the Dinkinesh flyby needed only 
</p>
<p>one new deterministic 3.4 m/s delta-V TCM (-8a) on May 9, 2023, with three follow-up statistical 
</p>
<p>cleanup TCM opportunities. Only TCM 8c, on September 29, 2023, was needed, and its delta-V 
was small. At only 6.2 cm/s delta-V, TCM 8c tested the lower limit of the spacecraft’s maneuver 
</p>
<p>execution capability. There was a concern that maneuver execution errors would be too large a 
</p>
<p>percentage of the total delta-V due to startup transients, attitude disturbances, and the initial imple-
</p>
<p>mentation of a new attitude controller updated to account for the changed spacecraft dynamics due 
</p>
<p>to the unlatched solar array. Post-TCM-8c maneuver performance evaluation revealed that these 
</p>
<p>concerns were unfounded, as TCM 8c was executed 33 days prior to Dinkinesh closest approach 
on September 29 with very small maneuver execution errors. Science and engineering analysis 
</p>
<p>revealed that a closest approach distance of 425 km would best accommodate science objectives 
</p>
<p>for the November 1, 2023, flyby. How close the actual maneuver delta-Vs and the reconstructed 
</p>
<p>spacecraft-asteroid periapsis location were to planned targets will appear in the “Trajectory and 
</p>
<p>Maneuver Performance Since Launch” section. 
 
</p>
<p>Processes Practiced with the Addition of the Dinkinesh Flyby  
</p>
<p>Adding the Dinkinesh flyby provided multiple useful opportunities to refine and test the limits 
</p>
<p>of spacecraft performance and selected flight operations team processes. While beyond the scope 
</p>
<p>of this paper, numerous asteroid encounter science operations including imaging and instrument 
pointing and sequence planning were tested for the first time after launch. Optical navigation tech-
</p>
<p>niques demonstrated during the MESSENGER Mercury orbiter, New Horizons (Pluto and Arro-
</p>
<p>koth flybys), and OSIRIS-REx asteroid sample return missions were refined and implemented by 
</p>
<p>the Lucy Navigation team to improve spacecraft and Dinkinesh orbit knowledge and Dinkinesh 
</p>
<p>physical properties7,8. Flight team members at KinetX and Lockheed Martin coordinated with 
</p>
<p>NASA/Goddard Space Flight Center (GSFC) and Southwest Research Institute leadership to plan 
and implement (or cancel if unnecessary) a series of asteroid approach TCMs at times relative to 
</p>
<p>asteroid periapsis that match the schedule for the primary science Jupiter Trojan asteroid encoun-
</p>
<p>ters. The nominal schedule for the final two asteroid approach maneuvers includes TCMs at en-
</p>
<p>counter periapsis (E) minus 30 days, E-7 days, and a E-7 days backup at E-5 days. However, a solar 
</p>
<p>conjunction (known well before launch) with Sun-Earth-spacecraft angle less than 3° forced the E-
30 days TCM to E-33 days to facilitate the availability for TCM planning of more reliable space-
</p>
<p>craft orbit data less tainted by solar plasma effects. Shortly after a post-SAA adapted new thruster 
</p>
<p>controller software update was placed on the spacecraft, TCM-8c was performed to impart a delta-
</p>
<p>V at just twice the minimum allowable 0.03 m/s. Due to the flyby periapsis distance, relative flyby 
</p>
<p>velocity, and small binary system mass, the Radio Science team was unable to accurately compute 
the mass of Dinkinesh and its unexpected binary system companion that was later named Selam. </p>

<p>HOW SPLITTING THE LARGEST DSM INTO TWO PARTS ALTERED OPERATIONS 
</p>
<p>The final of three primary post-launch mission plan changes arose early in 2023 when lead 
engineers referenced the recent Juno Jupiter orbiter mission experience of splitting a major cruise-
</p>
<p>phase DSM into two maneuvers9 separated far enough in time to allow a delay of the second part 
</p>
<p>if any anomaly were detected with the first maneuver. Firing the main engine thruster for the first 
</p>
<p>time with the largest maneuver that will consume over half the onboard propellant was deemed to 
</p>
<p>be higher risk than desired. After analysis by Lead Engineers at Lockheed Martin and KinetX a 
plan was approved to split DSM-2 into a 100 m/s delta-V DSM-2A on January 31, 2024, followed 
</p>
<p>by the 816 m/s delta-V DSM-2B on February 3, 2024. Note the maneuver nomenclature uses up-
</p>
<p>percase letters after maneuver number for multiple burn or follow-up contingency maneuvers for 
</p>
<p>DSMs and lowercase letters after the lower delta-V TCM maneuver number when that TCM was 
</p>
<p>added to the TCM schedule after launch. 
</p>
<p> 
There are primary reasons why splitting DSM-2 into two parts is a prudent risk reduction. First, 
</p>
<p>there is a term known as the “pyro ladder” which allows up to five main engine firings for maneu-
</p>
<p>vers with delta-V exceeding 50 m/s but preferably over 100 m/s to utilize the higher bi-propellant 
</p>
<p>specific impulse. With a two-part DSM-2 within three days, four of the five rungs of the pyro ladder 
</p>
<p>would be committed during the nominal mission with one rung reserved for a contingency situation. 
Furthermore, consecutive main engine firings must occur no closer than three days apart.  With 
</p>
<p>DSM-2 occurring near the spacecraft orbit’s aphelion when heliocentric velocity is lowest, and 
</p>
<p>considering that the DSM-2 target is over ten months later at the second Earth flyby perigee, the 
</p>
<p>delta-V cost of splitting DSM-2 into two parts was much less than 1 m/s. 
</p>
<p> 
Other reasons for starting DSM-2 with a 100 m/s delta-V trial use of the main engine involve 
</p>
<p>the opportunity to monitor how the spacecraft performs and have time to make changes before the 
</p>
<p>remainder of the maneuver completes three or more days later. If a minor anomaly occurs during 
</p>
<p>DSM-2A execution, there is potential to make a small adjustment to the DSM-2B command se-
</p>
<p>quence – especially if that anomaly was either quickly understood with a clear and testable solution, 
</p>
<p>or if that anomaly was foreseen and already had a resolution tested on a simulator before DSM-2A 
execution. Examples of such changes between DSM-2A and DSM-2B might involve changes to 
</p>
<p>onboard autonomy settings or limits that define when the spacecraft would enter safe mode during 
</p>
<p>DSM-2B. If a major anomaly occurs during DSM-2A, defined as an anomaly that requires DSM-
</p>
<p>2B to be delayed, then sufficient propellant margin exists to accommodate a three-week delay to 
</p>
<p>conduct a larger DSM-2C to complete DSM-2 on a re-optimized trajectory with updated target 
parameters for the December 13, 2024 perigee and all subsequent asteroid encounters and the third 
</p>
<p>Earth flyby. The three-week delayed DSM-2C will be designed, delivered, and tested before DSM-
</p>
<p>2A as a maximum delta-V contingency case. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Low Delta-V Options to Keep Earth Impact Probability Less Than 1% at EGA 2  
</p>
<p>The final area where splitting DSM-2 into two maneuvers added operational complexity in-
</p>
<p>volved conducting the Monte Carlo analysis required to ensure that any maneuver that directly 
</p>
<p>targets an Earth flyby perigee would result in less than a 1% probability of Earth atmospheric en-
</p>
<p>try/impact. Since the pre-launch 905 m/s DSM-2 and post-launch 816 m/s DSM-2B both have error 
</p>
<p>ellipses many times larger than Earth at the December 13, 2024, Earth arrival such that the proba-
</p>
<p>bility of Earth impact is less than 0.6% if no TCMs were possible after DSM-2B. The cleanup 
maneuver after DSM-2 or post-launch DSM-2B will be TCM-10 three weeks later. Prior to launch 
</p>
<p>the TCM-10 target required a 5690 km bias (offset from the optimal perigee target) to keep Earth 
</p>
<p>impact probability under 1% with no post-TCM-10 maneuvers.  </p>

<p>CONCLUSION 
</p>
<p>While many planetary missions make numerous flight plan and objective adjustments during 
</p>
<p>their nominal missions, the Lucy mission successfully made multiple adaptations to enhance space-
</p>
<p>craft operational safety and increase returned science all within the first 20% of the nominal mis-
</p>
<p>sion. With none of these adaptations planned before launch, the flight team elements worked dili-
</p>
<p>gently in a professional unified manner guided by an experienced and capable management team. 
</p>
<p>The primary post-launch mission updates that altered the trajectory and course-correction ma-
</p>
<p>neuvers planned originated with the solar array anomaly and resolution, the opportunity to fly by 
an inner main-belt asteroid at low relative velocity well before the earliest planned asteroid flyby, 
</p>
<p>and lessons learned from the two-part DSM strategy used by the Juno mission. The SAA recovery 
</p>
<p>to a stable spacecraft configuration with one of two large solar panels at least 98% (353° out of 
360°) deployed resulted in shifting the minimum perigee altitude constraint from 300 to 350 km for 
</p>
<p>the first two of three Earth flybys. The SAA outcome also combined with Juno Jupiter orbiter prec-
edent to split the mission’s largest course-correction maneuver (DSM-2) into two parts three days 
</p>
<p>apart with the first maneuver imparting only 100 m/s delta-V of the total 916 m/s required. For less 
</p>
<p>than 4 m/s extra deterministic delta-V and two new TCMs, mission designers added a November 
</p>
<p>1, 2023, flyby of what turned out to be a binary Dinkinesh-Selam asteroid system. The addition of 
</p>
<p>a previously unplanned statistical maneuver (TCM-10a) will lower the cost associated with shifting 
</p>
<p>the second Earth flyby perigee target to keep Earth impact probability &lt; 1%.  
</p>
<p>Early post-launch changes based on science analysis of Trojan encounters required changes in 
</p>
<p>characteristics of several of those asteroid flybys. Two asteroid periapsis ranges were updated from 
1000 km to 960 km and 1000 km to 750 km. Most recently, a new requirement arose to adjust 
</p>
<p>Trojan encounter periapsis times such that from acquisition of signal through closest approach 
</p>
<p>through loss of signal (periapsis ± one-way light time to Earth) lies within a single Deep Space 
</p>
<p>Network Earth-based tracking station pass. Three Trojan encounter periapsis epochs were shifted 
</p>
<p>the minimum time, about 2 to 2.6 hours, off the minimum-delta-V times to meet this requirement 
</p>
<p>at the time of planning a full-mission reference trajectory update just before DSM-2. Shifting these 
encounter times three to four years before they occur adds stability to advance planning phases of 
</p>
<p>encounter science and spacecraft operation command sequences, as well as reduced the total mis-
</p>
<p>sion delta-V cost of these changes to under 0.5 m/s. The Lucy team adapted to multiple unexpected 
</p>
<p>changes by redesigning the mission to address each change while preserving delta-V margin. </p>
