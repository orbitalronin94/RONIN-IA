Applying Machine Learning Algorithms to Label and Track 



Vibrational Mode-Shapes of a Generic Lunar Ascent Vehicle 



Jamshid Samareh*, Alexander Chin†, Hilmi Alkamhawi‡, and Sasan Armand§ 



NASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, VA 23681 



Finite-element analysis of dynamic structures results in a finite number of mode shapes 



with corresponding frequencies and mode-shape scale factors. Most structural designs for 



spacecraft require the lowest frequencies to stay above the launch vehicle design constraints. 



As the design changes, the output sequence for mode shapes can switch, e.g., a second mode of 



vibration which is torsional may switch its place with that which is a bending mode. The task 



of labeling and tracking the mode sequence entails careful visual inspection of mode shapes, 



typically through a human-in-the-loop process. Some design applications involve labeling and 



tracking thousands of mode shapes, requiring significant wall-clock time and human 



resources, which increases opportunities to introduce errors. The machine learning field is an 



emerging area of interest, especially for engineering applications. This paper presents the use 



of machine learning algorithms to automate the mode shape labeling and tracking tasks. The 



algorithms were tested on a tube and a generic lunar ascent vehicle. The mode shape tracking 



tests had a minimum of 96% accuracy for identifying the first mode shapes. The accuracy for 



tracking four subsequent mode shapes decreased but stayed above 90%. For labeling mode 



shapes, the algorithms captured mode diversity with 50 clusters. The mode shape labeling and 



tracking automation reduced the processing time from days and weeks to minutes.

II. Introduction 



 NASA has made a significant investment in developing advanced materials and structural concepts. The advanced 



materials include advanced carbon nanotubes (CNT), intermediate modulus fibers (IM7), polymers, and matrix 



composites. The traditional advanced materials assessment relies on the subject matter experts to provide qualitative 



assessments used to prioritize technology investments. However, quantitative assessments of advanced materials 



require a careful examination of materials and their applications using high-fidelity tools such as finite-element 



analysis (FEA). 



A typical structural modal analysis provides buckling and vibrational responses, including the natural frequencies, 



mode shapes, and mode factors. The structural responses are equivalent to a mathematical model for the dynamical 



behavior of the structure. A structural natural frequency has an associated mode shape, which provides a visual 



representation of the structural deformation in response to the given frequency. A mode shape is a coalescent of

possible deformed shapes at which a structure exchanges kinetic and strain energies. Each mode shape has a 



corresponding frequency at which the energy exchange occurs. The combined mode shapes and the corresponding 



frequencies capture an important portion of dynamic characteristics of a structure. 



A continuous structural analysis results in an infinite number of mode shapes and frequencies. However, a FEA 



results in a finite number of mode shapes with corresponding frequencies and mode-shape scale factors. Most 



structural designs require the lowest frequencies to stay above a design constraint to avoid interference with the control 



system of a vehicle. In the case of launch vehicles and their payloads, the minimum flexible mode should be higher 



than the controls frequency of the launch vehicle to eliminate the coupling among these frequencies. In addition, the 



flexible frequencies and their mode shapes should be accurate because they are used to decouple the equations of 



motion for integrated loads analysis of launch vehicles and spacecraft. 



The material properties such as stiffness (Young’s modulus) and strength (yield, tensile, compressive, and 



bending) play a key role in the design of structural concepts. Improving these properties may have benefits for 



structural designs used for NASA missions. To assess and quantify the impact of these parameters, we selected a 



generic lunar ascent vehicle and created a parametric FEA model (Fig. 1) with the goal of minimizing the structural 



mass subject to realistic constraints and loads from an earlier set of analyses performed by NASA. The load cases 



included Earth launch, lunar landing, and the ascent from the lunar surface. Two test cases were created for this paper. 



The first test case was an isolated load-bearing tube extracted from the generic lunar ascent vehicle. A parametric FEA 



model of the tube was created with constraints and loads. The input variables for the parametric model were scaled 



stiffness, scaled strength, number of fiber layers, and tube diameter. The second test case was the lunar ascent vehicle 



shown in Fig. 1. A parametric FEA model of the ascent vehicle was created with constraints and loads. The input 



variables for the parametric model were scaled modules and tube diameters for the supporting structures. 



For these two test cases, the task of labeling and tracking the mode shapes within the output sequence entails 



careful visual inspection of all modes, requiring human-in-the-loop inspection. The focus of this paper is the 



applications of machine learning algorithms to automate the mode shape labeling and tracking. 



III. Mode Shape Labeling and Tracking Algorithms 



Machine Learning (ML) is a set of statistical learning algorithms with a wide range of applications. Readers are 



referred to standard textbooks on machine learning (e.g., Ref. [1, 2]). Most machine learning algorithms are designed 



to handle large data sets, which are either labeled or unlabeled. In some applications, the set may include both labeled 



and unlabeled data. The data types play a key role in selecting suitable ML algorithms. The data types for ML 



algorithms are divided into the following categories: 



• Numerical Data: the data are quantitative and made of numbers. 



o Continuous: the data have infinite options (e.g., mass). 



o Discrete: the data have finite options (e.g., number of composite fiber layers). 



• Categorical Data: the data are qualitative and made of characters and words. 



o Ordinal: the data are structured and have a hierarchical order (e.g., low risk, medium risk, and 



high risk). 



o Nominal: the data are unstructured and unordered with no hierarchy (e.g., material type). 



The data sets used for this paper were either continuous (e.g., eigenvectors) or nominal (e.g., mode shape 



designations). 



The ML algorithms are divided into the following categories: 



• Supervised Learning: it is used to train a model on labeled datasets. 



o Classification: it is used to predict which cluster (grouping) the data belong to. 



o Regression: it is used to predict continuous values. 



• Unsupervised Learning: it is used to find clusters or reduce an unlabeled dataset. 



o Dimensionality Reduction: it is used to compress the dataset into a smaller dataset. 



o Clustering: it is used to identify clusters within a dataset. 



• Reinforced Learning: it uses trial and error to train a predictive model. 



For this paper we used classification and clustering algorithms from the ML module scikit-learn in python** to 



label and track mode shapes. The labeling process used the K-Means (KM) and Gaussian Mixture (GM) algorithms, 



and the tracking process used the K-Nearest (KN) neighbor algorithm. The labeling process was a relatively simple 



implementation of the KM algorithm. The mode shape tracking is more involved. Figure 2 shows the flowchart

tracking algorithm shown in Fig. 2: 



1. The full dataset in a matrix form with M rows (total number of modes) and N columns (number of nodes 



multiplied by three). 



2. Use KM and GM algorithms to select m mode shapes and n nodes that captured the diversity of available 



mode shapes. 



3. The reduced matrix (mk x n) is updated for each iteration. The index k is the iteration number. 



4. Label the reduced set of mode shapes (mk x n). An engineer labeled mk mode shapes. This step is similar 



to labeling commercial images using crowdsourcing web services. 



5. The reduced labeled matrix includes labels as an additional column with nominal data type. 



6. Synthesize a large dataset using mk labels from step five. Each synthesized mode shape was a random 



perturbation of a labeled mode shape. 



7. The synthesized matrix has 𝑀𝑘
𝑠 rows and n columns. The index s is equal to the number of synthesized 



mode shapes plus the number of labeled modes from step four. 



8. Use the synthesized matrix and train a KN model. This model was used to label the dataset matrix in step 



one. 



9. The new labeled matrix includes the label uncertainty for each mode shape. 



10. Select a small number of mode shapes with the largest uncertainty to label. We selected a maximum of 



10 modes shapes for the isolated tube and a maximum of 14 for the generic lunar ascent test cases. This 



step completed one iteration. The next iteration started at step three with the combined labeled mode 



shapes from this step and step 3. The iterative process will stop when the least uncertain label is below a 



user-defined threshold. 



At the conclusion of the algorithm, the entire data set was labeled using the model developed in step eight. Each 



label included a label uncertainty. 



IV. Results 



For this paper we used an isolated tube and a lunar ascent vehicle to demonstrate the applications of ML algorithms 



to label and track mode shapes. The results for both cases are discussed in the next sections. 



A. An Isolated Tube 



Figure 3 shows a tube extracted from the generic lunar ascent model shown in Fig. 1. The FEA model for the tube 



included loads and constraints from the lunar ascent model. Because the tube was isolated, it was modeled with a set 



of cantilevered boundary conditions. The top of Fig. 3 shows the tube fiber layout. The bottom of Fig. 3 shows a 



sample deflected contour for a given mode shape at a deformed position for a specific natural frequency. 



A parametric model for the normal mode FEA was created with 14,136 nodes. The large number of nodes may 



appear to be excessive, but it is required to capture the large variation in diameter (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 inches) and 



number of fiber layers (3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 layers). The model was executed with seven different diameters, six 



different fiber layers, and two material types (IM7 and CNT), for a total of 84 runs. The first 10 mode shapes were 



extracted from each FEA solution, resulting in 840 mode shapes. The full dataset matrix was 840 rows and 34,359 



(11,453x3) columns. 



To reduce the size of the dataset matrix and the required computational cost, step 2 of the algorithm in Fig. 2 was 



used to select a representative set of FEA nodes (n=75) and their surrounding regions (clusters). Figure 4 shows the 



selected nodes and their associated clusters, forming a quilt like pattern. The inputs to the clustering algorithm were 



the full matrix and number of desired clusters. The algorithm selected nodes for each cluster with no additional user 



input. The resulting reduced matrix had 840 rows and 225 (75x3) columns. Figure 5 shows the first output mode shape 



from the 84 FEA solutions. The baseline mode shape is shown in row 1, column 1. The first output mode shapes varied 



across different runs (e.g., see row 2, column 6). 



Mode Shape Labeling 



The mode shapes depend on the input parameters such as diameter and number of fiber layers. There are clusters 



of mode shapes that may look similar, but there are slight differences in their shapes. The K-Means algorithm was 



used to identify the major mode shape clusters. The inputs to the algorithm were the reduced matrix and the desired 



number of clusters, which has significant impact on the clustering results. Figure 6 shows the impact of the number

of clusters on the maximum number of modes among clusters. The curve for the maximum number bent around 45 to 



50 clusters, which was an optimal balance between the number of clusters and the maximum number of modes among 



clusters. Figure 7 shows the number of mode shapes in each cluster for a 50-cluster case. Figure 8 shows the labeled 



mode shapes for cluster 14 of Fig. 7. The figure legends show the FEA run number (R) and mode sequence number 



(M). Figures A1-A3 in the appendix show additional labeling results for the 50-cluster case. Two out 50 clusters 



contained dissimilar mode shapes that need to be labeled by the user, resulting in 96% accuracy. Increasing the number 



of clusters to 70 resulted in one dissimilar cluster (98.6% accuracy). 



Mode Shape Tracking 



The tracking algorithm described in section III was used to track mode shapes. Figure 9 shows the intermediate 



results for 12 iterations. The iteration sequence starts at the top row of Fig. 9, and each row represents a single FEA 



run. For this implementation, 10 modes shapes were extracted from the FEA output for each iteration. To simplify the 



labeling process, the potential mode clusters are identified with a single character (e.g., A). In Fig. 9, the red labels 



are mode labels identified by the user, and the black labels are identified by the algorithm. The black labels in 



parenthesis are mode shapes that were incorrectly identified by the algorithm and corrected by the user. After each 



iteration, the user examined the prediction for the next FEA run and corrected the misidentified mode shapes. After 



12 iterations, the KN model was applied to the entire 840 mode shapes. Figure 10 shows 348 mode shapes identified 



as mode A, with 14 misidentified modes (e.g., row 1, column 16). The tracking accuracy for mode shape A was 96%. 



The tracking accuracy for modes further into the sequence decreased. For example, the accuracy for modes B and C 



were 94% and 92%, respectively. For structural dynamic analysis and design, the first mode is the critical mode shape. 



Figures A4-A5 in the appendix show additional tracking results. 



B. Generic Lunar Ascent 



A parametric FEA model for normal mode was created for an aluminum lunar ascent vehicle with 11,474 nodes. 



The model was executed with five different scaled modules and five different tube diameters for the crew-cabin 



support structure, for a total of 25 runs. The first six rigid body modes were ignored, and the next fourteen mode 



shapes were extracted from each FEA solution, resulting in 350 mode shapes. Figure 11 shows the seventh mode of 



the output sequence extracted from the 25 FEA solutions. The baseline mode shape is shown in row 1, column 1. 



There were three distinct mode shapes: seven mode shapes similar to R1/M7, nine similar to R5/M7, and 10 similar 



to R16/M7. Similar to the isolated tube, the mode shape sequence changed as design parameters changed. 



 The full matrix for the dataset was 350 rows and 34,422 (11,474x3) columns. To reduce the size of the matrix and 



the required computational cost, the K-Means algorithm was used to select a representative set of FEA nodes (n=150) 



and their surrounding regions (clusters). Figure 12 shows the selected nodes and their associated clusters. The inputs 



to the clustering algorithm were the full matrix and number of desired clusters. The algorithm selected nodes for each 



cluster with no additional user input. The resulting reduced matrix has 350 rows and 450 (150x3) columns. 



 



Mode Shape Labeling 



The K-Means algorithm was used to identify mode shape clusters. The inputs to the algorithm were the reduced 



matrix and the desired number of clusters, which had significant impact on clustering results. Figure 13 shows the 



impact of the number of clusters on the maximum number of mode shapes among cluster. The curve for the maximum 



number bent around 45 to 50 clusters. Figures 14 and 15 show sample results for a 50-cluster case. The K-Means 



algorithm was effective at clustering unique mode shapes. One out 50 clusters contained dissimilar mode shapes that 



need to be labeled by the user, resulting in 98% accuracy. Reducing the number of clusters to 30 resulted in three 



clusters with dissimilar mode shapes (90% accuracy). 



Mode Shape Tracking 



 The tracking algorithm described in section III was used to track mode shapes for a generic lunar ascent vehicle. 



Figure 16 shows the intermediate results for eight iterations. Each column represents one iteration, and the iteration 



sequence starts at the left column with the mode sequence going from bottom to top. During each iteration, 14 mode 



shapes were either identified by ML algorithms or corrected by the user. To simplify the labeling process, the modes 



are identified with a single character (e.g., A). In Fig. 16, the red text are mode shapes identified by the user, and the 



black text are mode shapes identified by the algorithm. The black text in parenthesis are mode shapes that were 



incorrectly identified by the algorithm and corrected by the user. After each iteration, the user examined the prediction 



for the next FEA run and corrected the misidentified mode shapes. Some modes shapes were symmetric about each 



other, and the tracking algorithm could not resolve the differences. For example, rotating the first mode shape of the

fifth run (bottom row of Fig. 16) by 90° around the primary axis produced a similar mode shape to the first mode of 



the first run. The user used the same labels for these symmetric mode shapes. 



 After the eighth iteration, the KN model was applied to the entire 350 mode shapes. Figure 17 shows 25 mode 



shapes identified as mode shape A. In Fig. 17, thirteen mode shapes are similar to R1/M7 and 12 similar to R11/M7. 



Figures 18, A6, and A7 show similar results for mode shapes B, C, and D, respectively. The tracking accuracy for 



mode shapes A-E, G, and K were 100%. The tracking accuracy for modes further into the sequence decreased. For 



example, the accuracy for modes F and H were 55% and 79%, respectively. For structural dynamic analysis and 



design, the first mode is the critical mode shape. 



V. Summary 



We used machine learning algorithms to automate the labeling and tracking of structural mode shapes for two sets 



of finite-element analysis models. The algorithms had a minimum of 96% accuracy for labeling and tracking the first 



mode shapes. The accuracy for second through fifth mode shapes decreased but stayed above 90%. The automation 



reduced the time for labeling and tracking mode shapes from days and weeks to minutes. Three areas of improvements 



are recommended. First, the algorithm implementations should be validated with additional finite-element analysis 



models. Second, combining K-Means and the K-Nearest algorithms to track mode shapes could reduce the burden for 



the user to label mode shapes. Third, other machine learning classification and clustering algorithms should be 



evaluated (e.g., spectral clustering for labeling and gradient boosting for tracking).
