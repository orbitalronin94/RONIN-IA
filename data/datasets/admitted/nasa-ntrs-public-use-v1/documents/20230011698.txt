<p>Applying Machine Learning Algorithms to Label and Track 
</p>
<p>Vibrational Mode-Shapes of a Generic Lunar Ascent Vehicle  
</p>
<p>Jamshid Samareh*, Alexander Chin†, Hilmi Alkamhawi‡, and Sasan Armand§ 
</p>
<p>NASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, VA 23681 
</p>
<p>Finite-element analysis of dynamic structures results in a finite number of mode shapes 
</p>
<p>with corresponding frequencies and mode-shape scale factors. Most structural designs for 
</p>
<p>spacecraft require the lowest frequencies to stay above the launch vehicle design constraints. 
</p>
<p>As the design changes, the output sequence for mode shapes can switch, e.g., a second mode of 
</p>
<p>vibration which is torsional may switch its place with that which is a bending mode. The task 
</p>
<p>of labeling and tracking the mode sequence entails careful visual inspection of mode shapes, 
</p>
<p>typically through a human-in-the-loop process. Some design applications involve labeling and 
</p>
<p>tracking thousands of mode shapes, requiring significant wall-clock time and human 
</p>
<p>resources, which increases opportunities to introduce errors. The machine learning field is an 
</p>
<p>emerging area of interest, especially for engineering applications. This paper presents the use 
</p>
<p>of machine learning algorithms to automate the mode shape labeling and tracking tasks. The 
</p>
<p>algorithms were tested on a tube and a generic lunar ascent vehicle. The mode shape tracking 
</p>
<p>tests had a minimum of 96% accuracy for identifying the first mode shapes. The accuracy for 
</p>
<p>tracking four subsequent mode shapes decreased but stayed above 90%. For labeling mode 
</p>
<p>shapes, the algorithms captured mode diversity with 50 clusters. The mode shape labeling and 
</p>
<p>tracking automation reduced the processing time from days and weeks to minutes. 
</p>
<p>I. Nomenclature 
</p>
<p>k = iteration number 
</p>
<p>M = number of rows (number of available mode shapes) 
</p>
<p>𝑀𝑠 = number of rows (number of synthesized mode shapes) 
</p>
<p>𝑚𝑘 = number of rows (number of labeled mode shapes for iteration k) 
</p>
<p>N = number of columns (number of nodes multiplied by 3) 
</p>
<p>n = number of columns (number of selected nodes multiplied by 3) 
</p>
<p>s = number of synthesized mode shapes 
</p>
<p> = fiber angle (degree) 
</p>
<p>II. Introduction 
</p>
<p> NASA has made a significant investment in developing advanced materials and structural concepts. The advanced 
</p>
<p>materials include advanced carbon nanotubes (CNT), intermediate modulus fibers (IM7), polymers, and matrix 
</p>
<p>composites. The traditional advanced materials assessment relies on the subject matter experts to provide qualitative 
</p>
<p>assessments used to prioritize technology investments. However, quantitative assessments of advanced materials 
</p>
<p>require a careful examination of materials and their applications using high-fidelity tools such as finite-element 
</p>
<p>analysis (FEA). 
</p>
<p>A typical structural modal analysis provides buckling and vibrational responses, including the natural frequencies, 
</p>
<p>mode shapes, and mode factors. The structural responses are equivalent to a mathematical model for the dynamical 
</p>
<p>behavior of the structure. A structural natural frequency has an associated mode shape, which provides a visual 
</p>
<p>representation of the structural deformation in response to the given frequency. A mode shape is a coalescent of 
</p>

</p>
<p>possible deformed shapes at which a structure exchanges kinetic and strain energies. Each mode shape has a 
</p>
<p>corresponding frequency at which the energy exchange occurs. The combined mode shapes and the corresponding 
</p>
<p>frequencies capture an important portion of dynamic characteristics of a structure. 
</p>
<p>A continuous structural analysis results in an infinite number of mode shapes and frequencies. However, a FEA 
</p>
<p>results in a finite number of mode shapes with corresponding frequencies and mode-shape scale factors. Most 
</p>
<p>structural designs require the lowest frequencies to stay above a design constraint to avoid interference with the control 
</p>
<p>system of a vehicle. In the case of launch vehicles and their payloads, the minimum flexible mode should be higher 
</p>
<p>than the controls frequency of the launch vehicle to eliminate the coupling among these frequencies. In addition, the 
</p>
<p>flexible frequencies and their mode shapes should be accurate because they are used to decouple the equations of 
</p>
<p>motion for integrated loads analysis of launch vehicles and spacecraft. 
</p>
<p>The material properties such as stiffness (Young’s modulus) and strength (yield, tensile, compressive, and 
</p>
<p>bending) play a key role in the design of structural concepts. Improving these properties may have benefits for 
</p>
<p>structural designs used for NASA missions. To assess and quantify the impact of these parameters, we selected a 
</p>
<p>generic lunar ascent vehicle and created a parametric FEA model (Fig. 1) with the goal of minimizing the structural 
</p>
<p>mass subject to realistic constraints and loads from an earlier set of analyses performed by NASA. The load cases 
</p>
<p>included Earth launch, lunar landing, and the ascent from the lunar surface. Two test cases were created for this paper. 
</p>
<p>The first test case was an isolated load-bearing tube extracted from the generic lunar ascent vehicle. A parametric FEA 
</p>
<p>model of the tube was created with constraints and loads. The input variables for the parametric model were scaled 
</p>
<p>stiffness, scaled strength, number of fiber layers, and tube diameter. The second test case was the lunar ascent vehicle 
</p>
<p>shown in Fig. 1. A parametric FEA model of the ascent vehicle was created with constraints and loads. The input 
</p>
<p>variables for the parametric model were scaled modules and tube diameters for the supporting structures.  
</p>
<p>For these two test cases, the task of labeling and tracking the mode shapes within the output sequence entails 
</p>
<p>careful visual inspection of all modes, requiring human-in-the-loop inspection. The focus of this paper is the 
</p>
<p>applications of machine learning algorithms to automate the mode shape labeling and tracking. 
</p>
<p>III. Mode Shape Labeling and Tracking Algorithms 
</p>
<p>Machine Learning (ML) is a set of statistical learning algorithms with a wide range of applications. Readers are 
</p>
<p>referred to standard textbooks on machine learning (e.g., Ref. [1, 2]). Most machine learning algorithms are designed 
</p>
<p>to handle large data sets, which are either labeled or unlabeled. In some applications, the set may include both labeled 
</p>
<p>and unlabeled data. The data types play a key role in selecting suitable ML algorithms. The data types for ML 
</p>
<p>algorithms are divided into the following categories: 
</p>
<p>• Numerical Data: the data are quantitative and made of numbers. 
</p>
<p>o Continuous: the data have infinite options (e.g., mass). 
</p>
<p>o Discrete: the data have finite options (e.g., number of composite fiber layers). 
</p>
<p>• Categorical Data: the data are qualitative and made of characters and words. 
</p>
<p>o Ordinal: the data are structured and have a hierarchical order (e.g., low risk, medium risk, and 
</p>
<p>high risk). 
</p>
<p>o Nominal: the data are unstructured and unordered with no hierarchy (e.g., material type). 
</p>
<p>The data sets used for this paper were either continuous (e.g., eigenvectors) or nominal (e.g., mode shape 
</p>
<p>designations). 
</p>
<p>The ML algorithms are divided into the following categories: 
</p>
<p>• Supervised Learning: it is used to train a model on labeled datasets. 
</p>
<p>o Classification: it is used to predict which cluster (grouping) the data belong to. 
</p>
<p>o Regression: it is used to predict continuous values. 
</p>
<p>• Unsupervised Learning: it is used to find clusters or reduce an unlabeled dataset. 
</p>
<p>o Dimensionality Reduction: it is used to compress the dataset into a smaller dataset. 
</p>
<p>o Clustering: it is used to identify clusters within a dataset. 
</p>
<p>• Reinforced Learning: it uses trial and error to train a predictive model. 
</p>
<p>For this paper we used classification and clustering algorithms from the ML module scikit-learn in python** to 
</p>
<p>label and track mode shapes. The labeling process used the K-Means (KM) and Gaussian Mixture (GM) algorithms, 
</p>
<p>and the tracking process used the K-Nearest (KN) neighbor algorithm. The labeling process was a relatively simple 
</p>
<p>implementation of the KM algorithm. The mode shape tracking is more involved. Figure 2 shows the flowchart 
</p>
<p>

</p>
<p>implementation for mode shape tracking. The algorithm started from the upper left of Fig. 2 with the entire dataset in 
</p>
<p>a matrix form as an input. Matrix rows represented individual mode shapes, and columns represented mode shape 
</p>
<p>vectors at FEA nodes. Below is a list of steps (even numbers) and data descriptions (odd numbers) for the mode shape 
</p>
<p>tracking algorithm shown in Fig. 2: 
</p>
<p>1. The full dataset in a matrix form with M rows (total number of modes) and N columns (number of nodes 
</p>
<p>multiplied by three). 
</p>
<p>2. Use KM and GM algorithms to select m mode shapes and n nodes that captured the diversity of available 
</p>
<p>mode shapes. 
</p>
<p>3. The reduced matrix (mk x n) is updated for each iteration. The index k is the iteration number. 
</p>
<p>4. Label the reduced set of mode shapes (mk x n). An engineer labeled mk mode shapes. This step is similar 
</p>
<p>to labeling commercial images using crowdsourcing web services. 
</p>
<p>5. The reduced labeled matrix includes labels as an additional column with nominal data type. 
</p>
<p>6. Synthesize a large dataset using mk labels from step five. Each synthesized mode shape was a random 
</p>
<p>perturbation of a labeled mode shape. 
</p>
<p>7. The synthesized matrix has 𝑀𝑘
𝑠 rows and n columns. The index s is equal to the number of synthesized 
</p>
<p>mode shapes plus the number of labeled modes from step four. 
</p>
<p>8. Use the synthesized matrix and train a KN model. This model was used to label the dataset matrix in step 
</p>
<p>one. 
</p>
<p>9. The new labeled matrix includes the label uncertainty for each mode shape. 
</p>
<p>10. Select a small number of mode shapes with the largest uncertainty to label. We selected a maximum of 
</p>
<p>10 modes shapes for the isolated tube and a maximum of 14 for the generic lunar ascent test cases. This 
</p>
<p>step completed one iteration. The next iteration started at step three with the combined labeled mode 
</p>
<p>shapes from this step and step 3. The iterative process will stop when the least uncertain label is below a 
</p>
<p>user-defined threshold. 
</p>
<p>At the conclusion of the algorithm, the entire data set was labeled using the model developed in step eight. Each 
</p>
<p>label included a label uncertainty. 
</p>
<p>IV.  Results 
</p>
<p>For this paper we used an isolated tube and a lunar ascent vehicle to demonstrate the applications of ML algorithms 
</p>
<p>to label and track mode shapes. The results for both cases are discussed in the next sections.  
</p>
<p>A. An Isolated Tube 
</p>
<p>Figure 3 shows a tube extracted from the generic lunar ascent model shown in Fig. 1. The FEA model for the tube 
</p>
<p>included loads and constraints from the lunar ascent model. Because the tube was isolated, it was modeled with a set 
</p>
<p>of cantilevered boundary conditions. The top of Fig. 3 shows the tube fiber layout. The bottom of Fig. 3 shows a 
</p>
<p>sample deflected contour for a given mode shape at a deformed position for a specific natural frequency. 
</p>
<p>A parametric model for the normal mode FEA was created with 14,136 nodes. The large number of nodes may 
</p>
<p>appear to be excessive, but it is required to capture the large variation in diameter (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 inches) and 
</p>
<p>number of fiber layers (3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 layers). The model was executed with seven different diameters, six 
</p>
<p>different fiber layers, and two material types (IM7 and CNT), for a total of 84 runs. The first 10 mode shapes were 
</p>
<p>extracted from each FEA solution, resulting in 840 mode shapes. The full dataset matrix was 840 rows and 34,359 
</p>
<p>(11,453x3) columns. 
</p>
<p>To reduce the size of the dataset matrix and the required computational cost, step 2 of the algorithm in Fig. 2 was 
</p>
<p>used to select a representative set of FEA nodes (n=75) and their surrounding regions (clusters). Figure 4 shows the 
</p>
<p>selected nodes and their associated clusters, forming a quilt like pattern. The inputs to the clustering algorithm were 
</p>
<p>the full matrix and number of desired clusters. The algorithm selected nodes for each cluster with no additional user 
</p>
<p>input. The resulting reduced matrix had 840 rows and 225 (75x3) columns. Figure 5 shows the first output mode shape 
</p>
<p>from the 84 FEA solutions. The baseline mode shape is shown in row 1, column 1. The first output mode shapes varied 
</p>
<p>across different runs (e.g., see row 2, column 6). 
</p>
<p>Mode Shape Labeling 
</p>
<p>The mode shapes depend on the input parameters such as diameter and number of fiber layers. There are clusters 
</p>
<p>of mode shapes that may look similar, but there are slight differences in their shapes. The K-Means algorithm was 
</p>
<p>used to identify the major mode shape clusters. The inputs to the algorithm were the reduced matrix and the desired 
</p>
<p>number of clusters, which has significant impact on the clustering results. Figure 6 shows the impact of the number </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>4 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>of clusters on the maximum number of modes among clusters. The curve for the maximum number bent around 45 to 
</p>
<p>50 clusters, which was an optimal balance between the number of clusters and the maximum number of modes among 
</p>
<p>clusters.  Figure 7 shows the number of mode shapes in each cluster for a 50-cluster case. Figure 8 shows the labeled 
</p>
<p>mode shapes for cluster 14 of Fig. 7. The figure legends show the FEA run number (R) and mode sequence number 
</p>
<p>(M). Figures A1-A3 in the appendix show additional labeling results for the 50-cluster case. Two out 50 clusters 
</p>
<p>contained dissimilar mode shapes that need to be labeled by the user, resulting in 96% accuracy. Increasing the number 
</p>
<p>of clusters to 70 resulted in one dissimilar cluster (98.6% accuracy). 
</p>
<p>Mode Shape Tracking 
</p>
<p>The tracking algorithm described in section III was used to track mode shapes. Figure 9 shows the intermediate 
</p>
<p>results for 12 iterations. The iteration sequence starts at the top row of Fig. 9, and each row represents a single FEA 
</p>
<p>run. For this implementation, 10 modes shapes were extracted from the FEA output for each iteration. To simplify the 
</p>
<p>labeling process, the potential mode clusters are identified with a single character (e.g., A). In Fig. 9, the red labels 
</p>
<p>are mode labels identified by the user, and the black labels are identified by the algorithm. The black labels in 
</p>
<p>parenthesis are mode shapes that were incorrectly identified by the algorithm and corrected by the user. After each 
</p>
<p>iteration, the user examined the prediction for the next FEA run and corrected the misidentified mode shapes. After 
</p>
<p>12 iterations, the KN model was applied to the entire 840 mode shapes. Figure 10 shows 348 mode shapes identified 
</p>
<p>as mode A, with 14 misidentified modes (e.g., row 1, column 16). The tracking accuracy for mode shape A was 96%. 
</p>
<p>The tracking accuracy for modes further into the sequence decreased. For example, the accuracy for modes B and C 
</p>
<p>were 94% and 92%, respectively. For structural dynamic analysis and design, the first mode is the critical mode shape. 
</p>
<p>Figures A4-A5 in the appendix show additional tracking results. 
</p>
<p>B. Generic Lunar Ascent 
</p>
<p>A parametric FEA model for normal mode was created for an aluminum lunar ascent vehicle with 11,474 nodes. 
</p>
<p>The model was executed with five different scaled modules and five different tube diameters for the crew-cabin 
</p>
<p>support structure, for a total of 25 runs. The first six rigid body modes were ignored, and the next fourteen mode 
</p>
<p>shapes were extracted from each FEA solution, resulting in 350 mode shapes. Figure 11 shows the seventh mode of 
</p>
<p>the output sequence extracted from the 25 FEA solutions. The baseline mode shape is shown in row 1, column 1. 
</p>
<p>There were three distinct mode shapes: seven mode shapes similar to R1/M7, nine similar to R5/M7, and 10 similar 
</p>
<p>to R16/M7. Similar to the isolated tube, the mode shape sequence changed as design parameters changed. 
</p>
<p> The full matrix for the dataset was 350 rows and 34,422 (11,474x3) columns. To reduce the size of the matrix and 
</p>
<p>the required computational cost, the K-Means algorithm was used to select a representative set of FEA nodes (n=150) 
</p>
<p>and their surrounding regions (clusters). Figure 12 shows the selected nodes and their associated clusters. The inputs 
</p>
<p>to the clustering algorithm were the full matrix and number of desired clusters. The algorithm selected nodes for each 
</p>
<p>cluster with no additional user input. The resulting reduced matrix has 350 rows and 450 (150x3) columns. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Mode Shape Labeling 
</p>
<p>The K-Means algorithm was used to identify mode shape clusters. The inputs to the algorithm were the reduced 
</p>
<p>matrix and the desired number of clusters, which had significant impact on clustering results. Figure 13 shows the 
</p>
<p>impact of the number of clusters on the maximum number of mode shapes among cluster. The curve for the maximum 
</p>
<p>number bent around 45 to 50 clusters. Figures 14 and 15 show sample results for a 50-cluster case. The K-Means 
</p>
<p>algorithm was effective at clustering unique mode shapes. One out 50 clusters contained dissimilar mode shapes that 
</p>
<p>need to be labeled by the user, resulting in 98% accuracy. Reducing the number of clusters to 30 resulted in three 
</p>
<p>clusters with dissimilar mode shapes (90% accuracy). 
</p>
<p>Mode Shape Tracking 
</p>
<p> The tracking algorithm described in section III was used to track mode shapes for a generic lunar ascent vehicle. 
</p>
<p>Figure 16 shows the intermediate results for eight iterations. Each column represents one iteration, and the iteration 
</p>
<p>sequence starts at the left column with the mode sequence going from bottom to top. During each iteration, 14 mode 
</p>
<p>shapes were either identified by ML algorithms or corrected by the user. To simplify the labeling process, the modes 
</p>
<p>are identified with a single character (e.g., A). In Fig. 16, the red text are mode shapes identified by the user, and the 
</p>
<p>black text are mode shapes identified by the algorithm. The black text in parenthesis are mode shapes that were 
</p>
<p>incorrectly identified by the algorithm and corrected by the user. After each iteration, the user examined the prediction 
</p>
<p>for the next FEA run and corrected the misidentified mode shapes. Some modes shapes were symmetric about each 
</p>
<p>other, and the tracking algorithm could not resolve the differences. For example, rotating the first mode shape of the </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>5 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>fifth run (bottom row of Fig. 16) by 90° around the primary axis produced a similar mode shape to the first mode of 
</p>
<p>the first run. The user used the same labels for these symmetric mode shapes. 
</p>
<p> After the eighth iteration, the KN model was applied to the entire 350 mode shapes. Figure 17 shows 25 mode 
</p>
<p>shapes identified as mode shape A. In Fig. 17, thirteen mode shapes are similar to R1/M7 and 12 similar to R11/M7. 
</p>
<p>Figures 18, A6, and A7 show similar results for mode shapes B, C, and D, respectively. The tracking accuracy for 
</p>
<p>mode shapes A-E, G, and K were 100%. The tracking accuracy for modes further into the sequence decreased. For 
</p>
<p>example, the accuracy for modes F and H were 55% and 79%, respectively. For structural dynamic analysis and 
</p>
<p>design, the first mode is the critical mode shape. 
</p>
<p>V. Summary 
</p>
<p>We used machine learning algorithms to automate the labeling and tracking of structural mode shapes for two sets 
</p>
<p>of finite-element analysis models. The algorithms had a minimum of 96% accuracy for labeling and tracking the first 
</p>
<p>mode shapes. The accuracy for second through fifth mode shapes decreased but stayed above 90%. The automation 
</p>
<p>reduced the time for labeling and tracking mode shapes from days and weeks to minutes. Three areas of improvements 
</p>
<p>are recommended. First, the algorithm implementations should be validated with additional finite-element analysis 
</p>
<p>models. Second, combining K-Means and the K-Nearest algorithms to track mode shapes could reduce the burden for 
</p>
<p>the user to label mode shapes. Third, other machine learning classification and clustering algorithms should be 
</p>
<p>evaluated (e.g., spectral clustering for labeling and gradient boosting for tracking). </p>
