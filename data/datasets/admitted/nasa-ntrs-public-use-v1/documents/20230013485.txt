<p>LUNAR WATER EXTRACTION via 
LUNAR AUGER DRYER ISRU (LADI)  
</p>
<p>Jacob A. Collins1, Jeff Michel2, and Koorosh Araghi3 
NASA Johnson Space Center, Houston, TX 77058, USA 
</p>
<p>In 2009, the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) and Lunar Crater Observation and 
Sensing Satellite (LCROSS) provided definitive proof of water in the Lunar’s southern 
permanently shadowed region (PSR)1. Both the 2020 NASA Technology Taxonomy2 and the 
Lunar Surface Innovation Initiative (LSII) team identified capability gaps in icy regolith 
transfer and reactor processing in Permanently Shadowed Region (PSR) environmental 
conditions. A water processing plant operating inside the PSR can continuously process water 
(and volatiles) for both breathable air and propellant. NASA’s Johnson Space Center (JSC) 
fabricated a unique breadboard test stand for validating the feasibility of this concept, 
developed a lunar auger dryer sizing tool, and identified the physical flow and thermal models 
required to design an Engineering Development Unit (EDU) for environmental testing. Over 
the two years this project was active, models were developed including: a) a sizing tool that 
scales the auger parameters based on production targets, b) accurate thermal models in both 
lab and lunar conditions, c) a Discrete Element Model (DEM) for observing particle effects in 
low gravity, and d) incorporated these subsystem models into a MATLAB parametric 
Modular Open System Approach (MOSA) system model. These models were validated by 
testing the auger in both a mechanical and thermal system configuration. The results of this 
testing increased the Technology Readiness Level (TRL) of the Lunar Auger Dryer ISRU 
(LADI) system to TRL 4. 
</p>

<p>II. Introduction 
NASA’s Artemis plan outlines a return to the moon by 2024 and a sustainable surface presence in the 2028 
</p>
<p>timeframe4. A key part of sustainability is In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU), which is the practice of using local 
resources to provide mission consumables. NASA’s mission plans target the lunar South Pole to leverage extended 
periods of solar illumination and water-ice observed in the Permanently Shadowed Regions (PSR) around the poles. 
While water can be used for life support and power, the initial focus for mission studies is as a propellant. Water can 
produce both fuel and/or oxidizer consumables to fully refuel a lander or ascent vehicle. To address ISRU infusion 
into mission planning, studies of end-to-end water based ISRU systems for propellant production have been initiated 
to assess several ISRU architectures, Concepts of Operations (CONOPS), and obtain mass and power estimates5. The 
details of the mission architectures, how ISRU will be infused, technology options, site selection, availability of 
supporting systems, and operational concepts are still in work. The Lunar Auger Dryer ISRU (LADI) project 
developed a concept of producing water continuously in steady-state mode without the use of isolation valves. 
</p>
<p>While terrestrial augers are a mature technology, the current State-of-Art (SOA) for lunar augers is low. 
Continuous icy regolith processing in a lunar environment is complex with many variables including but not limited 
to low gravity (low inertia environment so dynamic forces are less pronounced), thermal effects from extreme 
temperatures, solid and vapor flow with a higher system pressure than the hard lunar vacuum, and balancing the 
regolith transient time (physical flow rate) versus residence time (thermal flow rate) suitable for water condensation 
production rates. The project goal’s as defined in the Lunar Oxygen/Water Extraction (LOWE) project plan is as 
follows: 
</p>
<p>1 Update the previously developed Mars sizing tool and thermal model with lunar environmental conditions 
and mission architectural assumptions. The breadboard test stand was initially designed and sized for Mars 
operations and canceled in 2017 due to NASA’s redirection from Mars to Moon. A JSC led trade study3 in 
FY20 formulated a plan to leverage this hardware to reduce funding and prove Lunar concept feasibility. 
</p>
<p>2 Validate the sizing tool mechanical models using a lunar simulant to increase TRL to 3. 
3 Test the breadboard auger dryer subsystem in laboratory conditions using Commercial Off-The-Shelf 
</p>
<p>(COTS) available subsystems and validate the thermal and physics-based numerical models to increase the 
TRL to 4. 
</p>
<p>4 Test an Engineering Development Unit (EDU) auger dryer with COTS upstream and downstream 
subsystems in a thermal vacuum chamber to verify continuous water processing in a relevant lunar 
environment using an icy regolith simulant to increase the TRL to 5. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>3 
 
</p>
<p>5 Integrate the EDU auger dryer subsystem with a flight-like upstream hopper/size sorter and downstream 
cold trap and test the system in a relevant environment to increase the TRL to 6.  
</p>
<p> Due to flat NASA technology development budgets, the Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) 
strategically decided to focus resources primarily on ISRU oxygen production starting in FY22 and LADI was placed 
on hold preventing completion of goals #4 and #5. The justification is that oxygen can be produced independent of 
landing site, accounts for the highest mass fraction in propellant, and does not require prospecting missions to 
determine the quantity and concentration of feedstock. When missions such as the Polar Resources Ice Mining 
Experiment (PRIME) or the Volatiles Investigating Polar Exploration Rover (VIPER) return with data, STMD will 
revisit its ISRU water processing commercialization strategy. System Engineering and Integration (SE&amp;I) meetings 
started early between JSC, Glenn Research Center (GRC), and Kennedy Space Center (KSC) to identify the key 
interfaces and common design parameters required for sizing the upstream and downstream subsystems. LADI also 
communicated mission architecture assumptions and subsystem requirements with Paragon Space Development 
Corporation, the University of Texas at El Paso (UTEP), and Grainflow. Early communication and planning enabled 
progress on subsystems compatible with a continuous lunar water processing system. Despite the abrupt project stop, 
the tools and technology developed under LADI increased the SOA. An executive summary of the accomplishments 
completed is located below: 
</p>
<p>1 Defined the Lunar environmental and mission architectural requirements for a pilot plant and full-scale 
mission as well as the necessary ground testing for validation. 
</p>
<p>2 Updated and validated the Mars Screw Conveyor Dryer (SCD) sizing tool with lunar constraints. This 
model represents a significant NASA capability for the design of a continuous Lunar water processing plant 
and can be later modified for a Mars auger design. 
</p>
<p>3 Generated thermal models to predict ground test requirements for the upstream hopper and downstream 
condenser. Also produced thermal models for the auger dryer subsystem for both ground testing (water in 
3-phases) and lunar environment (sublimation only). This environmental model represents a significant 
NASA capability for the design of low-pressure lunar systems dealing with sublimation. 
</p>
<p>4 A Discrete Element Method (DEM) simulation of the breadboard test stand was created. This tool has the 
potential to decrease test costs of any future auger dryer water processing system. 
</p>
<p>5 The auger dryer thermal model and SCD tool was integrated to work together and imported into STMD’s 
Mission Analysis and Integration Tool (MAIT). This tool represents a critical advancement in ISRU system 
modelling and is required to define mission architectural requirements. 
</p>
<p>6 Completed mechanical testing of the auger subsystem and increased the TRL to 4 (mechanically). This 
significantly increases the state-of-art by eliminating the need for upstream and downstream isolation 
valves and enables an innovative valveless technique for moving solids on the lunar surface without losing 
volatiles. 
</p>
<p>7 Completed thermal testing to achieve TRL 4. The LADI breadboard setup for thermal testing was ~90% 
complete when the project was placed on hold. Limited JSC Division Operating Expenses (DOE) funds 
were used to complete setup and perform a reduced scope thermal test matrix. Additional testing is required 
to complete the thermal model validation. 
</p>
<p>8 A design of an EDU full-scale lunar auger dryer sized as a payload for a Commercial Lunar Payload 
Services (CLPS) lander was in-work using the MAIT tool prior to cancelation.  
</p>
<p> The accomplishments completed above provide an excellent starting point for a future industry or international 
partner wanting to invest in Lunar ISRU water processing 
</p>
<p>III. Technical Approach 
</p>
<p>A. System Concept of Operations 
The mission architecture assumed in this document was defined in the PSR ISRU water-ice pilot plant conceptual 
</p>
<p>design developed by the COllaborative Modelling for Parametric Assessment of Space Systems (COMPASS) team 
released 04/08/2020. The CONOPS of an ISRU system that extracts water from the icy lunar regolith in a PSR and 
processes it into liquid oxygen (LO2) and liquid hydrogen (LH2) is shown in Fig. 1 and includes the following top-
level operations: 
</p>
<p>1 Excavator, such as KSC’s ISRU Pilot Excavator (IPEx) digs up icy regolith and delivers to a stationary ISRU 
water processing plant inside the PSR (travels ~100 m at 1 mph). 
</p>
<p>2 Icy regolith is fed into an inlet hopper and size sorted to remove large rocks, discharged into the auger, and 
then transported thru the auger casing while heated to the sublimation temperature. Heating can be </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>4 
 
</p>
<p>accomplished with electrical resistance band heaters or potentially heating a fluid surrounding the casing 
with a laser located on the ridge. Dry regolith is discharged, and water vapor is processed in a cold trap to 
form water ice and stored on a tanker. 
</p>
<p>3 An excavator collects the dried regolith and transports it away from the ISRU plant, travels ~100 m to the 
excavation site, and repeats the process. Alternately, the processed regolith can be discharged into a ‘dump 
truck’ rover to remove the dried regolith from the area and potentially use the waste heat. 
</p>
<p>4 The water tanker travels out of the PSR and delivers the water ice to a stationary processing plant located on 
the ridge. 
</p>
<p>5 The water is cleaned, used directly, or electrolyzed into oxygen (O2) &amp; hydrogen (H2), liquefied, and stored. 
6 The ISRU plant recharges the excavator and mobile platform batteries as needed. Due to the location in PSR, 
</p>
<p>the power source for processing the soil and recharging was originally assumed non-solar. However, beaming 
power into the PSR to heat a transfer fluid pumped around the auger casing may enable water processing on 
mobile platforms or Vertical Takeoff and Landing (VTOL) hoppers deep inside a PSR. While power is 
calculated in project, distribution of power is beyond the scope of the LADI project. 
</p>
<p> 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
B. Key Auger Dryer Subsystems 
</p>
<p>While the auger dryer is the heart of the system, it does not function alone and requires multiple subsystems to 
produce water. A secondary objective of the LADI project was to determine subsystem interface requirements and 
design constraints. 
</p>
<p>1. Hopper / Size Sorter 
 The purpose of the hopper subsystem is to store icy regolith prior to processing in the auger dryer. Design of 
this subsystem requires knowledge of the lunar environment and physical characteristics of feedstock (such as 
particle size distribution and density), but also the mass flow range of the soil entering the auger dryer required to 
hit production targets. The primary design constraints are as follows: 
</p>
<p>• Maximum Particle Size: 
After the icy regolith is excavated, it must be size sorted to remove large rocks to avoid damaging the 
system by jamming a rock between the auger and casing. Screw conveyor dryers must be optimized with 
a designated maximum particle size based on ground testing and particle size distribution of either the 
Moon or Mars. In general, the larger the gap, the more diverse icy regolith mixture can be feed to the 
auger dryer but also results in a reduced volumetric flow capability and decreased thermal efficiency due 
to a larger static layer of soil on bottom of auger casing that is not moved by the screw. Therefore, auger 
robustness is inversely proportional to volumetric flow and thermal efficiency.  
</p>
<p>• Hopper Volume: 
The volume of the hopper must be large enough to allow for continuous processing based on the auger 
flow rate and excavator processing time. The excavators must be able to keep the hopper full while the 
auger dryer processes the soil. If the upstream excavation process is delayed and the auger continues to 
run until the hopper is empty, the auger motor must stop to avoid losing the inlet and discharge soil plug 
and subsequently, losing the water vapor through the hopper. Optimization of the hopper volume 
involves knowing the maximum speed of the excavator, assuming a maximum travel distance from the 
auger, maximum payload of excavator &amp; assumed time to excavate, and the auger dryer flow rates based 
</p>

<p>on production targets. The larger the hopper, the more system mass and potential sublimation water

</p>
<p>• Hopper Geometry: 
The shape of the auger affects more than the volumetric capacity available. The hopper walls must be 
steeper than the angle of repose for the lunar highland soil. The angle of repose of a granular material is 
defined as the steepest angle relative to the horizontal plane which a material can be piled without the 
surface material sliding. In ground testing, simulant cohesion (ability to stick to hopper wall) varied 
drastically depending on if the simulant was dry or wet. Future hopper development must include 
measuring the flow properties and behavior of multiple highlands simulants over the entire predicted 
range of water. 
</p>

subsystem should be completed in parallel to optimize the

sort the feedstock. The same motor powering the hopper auger could potentially turn a vertical auger interfaced 
with a bevel gear to directly feed the auger dryer. This would prevent feed issues caused by reduced lunar gravity 
and increased soil cohesion in vacuum.

</p>
<p>maintain a minimum system “head pressure” by keeping a full soil column (the inlet plug and inlet tube prevent 
water vapor from escaping upstream through hopper). Two inlet tube lengths and materials were tested: a 12-inch 
clear inlet tube for mechanical testing, and a 12-inch and 3-inch stainless steel inlet tube for thermal testing. The 
setup prevented vapor from exiting upstream and it successfully created a thermal standoff for ground testing, 
However, the inlet tube would clog demonstrating that gravity flow through a standard tube cannot be used in a 
lunar environment. A vertical auger running through the hopper and inlet tubing will ensure uninterrupted flow to 
auger dryer. During the thermal test phase of ground testing, this hopper was wrapped with copper tubing and 
thermal epoxy, and then connected to a COTS chiller to maintain frozen regolith feedstock prior to entering the 
auger. Unfortunately, a size-sorter was not available during testing; the lunar simulant maximum particle size was 
defined and sorted prior to delivery at JSC.  
</p>
<p>2. Inlet / Outlet Isolation Valves 
As regolith is heated and water ice sublimates within the auger dryer, the system pressure increases. This 
</p>
<p>pressure can potentially be used to push water vapor through a filter and into the downstream cold trap. However, 
wet soil and water vapor will be lost through the soil discharge or the hopper inlet unless isolated. The LADI 
project initially researched adding an inlet and outlet isolation valve after the hopper and discharge box. However, 
a single upstream and downstream valve would create a semi-batch operational scenario where plugs of soil are 
processed through the auger preventing the valve from opening until all water was processed through the cold trap. 
To achieve continuous operation, two inlet and two outlet isolation valves are required as shown in Fig. 4. The

<p>inlet and exit tube volume were sized to match the 
volumetric flow rate required to meet production

<p>Despite some benefits of operating in higher pressure, adding isolation valves increases system mass, power 
requirements, stowage volume, and decreases reliability (four valves must be cycled thousands of times with sand 
flowing through them over the entire mission duration). In addition, operating the auger dryer above the triple 
point of water (273.16 K and 611.657 Pa) will form liquid. This liquid water may react with the impurities in the 
lunar soil and create equilibrium reactions that increase the difficulty of downstream purification. Moreover, the 
liquid water will increase the regolith’s viscosity by forming mud in the auger and increase torque requirements 
on the auger dryer motor. A significant technology developmental effort is also required to increase the TRL of 
solid-flow lunar valves. For these reasons, a variable geometry auger design that creates soil plugs at the inlet and 
outlet was baselined for mechanical ground testing. Isolation valves are not planned to be used and not shown in 
Fig. 1 above. 
</p>
<p>3. Auger Dryer 
 The auger dryer, also known as a Screw Conveyer Dryer (SCD) in the mining industry is a common technology 
with a well-developed history of terrestrial uses. The auger dryer is the heart of the PSR water processing plant 
and enables sustainability by offering efficient continuous water production independent of lunar regolith ice type. 
The lunar regolith is fed into the inlet box from the upstream hopper, transported through the casing and heated to 
the desired temperature, and water vapor is then sent to a downstream cold trap for processing as the dry regolith 
is discharged. The system must balance the transient time of the soil from the inlet to the discharge by adjusting 
the motor’s RPM with the residence time required to heat the soil to water’s sublimation temperature by adjusting 
power applied to heaters. 
 The breadboard test stand used 
for ground testing was originally 
designed in 2017 using Martian 
mission architectural assumptions. 
Due to similarities in production 
targets, the full-scale size and flow 
requirements are similar for both the 
Moon and Mars. Moreover, despite 
the Martian soil requiring high temperatures to break the hydrated bonds and having a lower % water by weight, 
the polar Lunar soil enters the auger at cryogenic temperatures resulting in each design also sharing similar power 
requirements. Some early successes were observed testing the Mars simulant JSC-Mars-1A in the breadboard test 
stand by manipulating the geometry of the shaft and auger flights to intentionally create a plug of regolith at the 
inlet and exit as shown in Fig. 5. This plug created a seal without the need for isolation valves and allowed the 
system pressure to increase. 
 The key design features of LADI include a.) using a variable geometry auger to manipulate the soil fullness in 
the inlet, heated, and discharge sections to eliminate the need for isolation valves and b.) operating below the triple 
point of water. By creating a 100% full soil plug at the inlet and discharge section, trapped sublimated water vapor 
travels thru the path of least resistance from the heated section to the cold trap. The heated section pitch is designed 
at the optimal flow rates to create a 15% fullness (i.e. 85% ullage) with cut and fold flights to promote mixing and 
increase available surface area for heating. This design requires the use of a discharge gate to promote plug 
formation at startup and open at steady state. Operation of the gate was manual during ground testing; future 
designs would automate the gates opening and closing based on torque calculated from motor current. Operating 
below the triple point of water ensures low force is applied to the soil plug seals (force = pressure x inlet auger

vapor flow rates are critical design interface parameters with the upstream cold trap and must be efficiently

casing diameters (with a constant screw diameter to casing gap) is not linear. 
</p>
<p>4. Auger Motor 
 The auger dryer motor is responsible for rotating the auger screw and subsequently moving the regolith through 
the dryer. The motor’s RPM determines the transient time; defined as the time required for the regolith to travel 
from the auger’s inlet to the exit. This flow rate must be balanced with the residence time (defined as time to heat 
the regolith to the lunar water’s sublimation temperature) to maintain production goals. The motor is sized in 
proportion to the auger’s degree of fullness, the screw diameter, the auger length, the regolith’s bulk density, and 
the desired flow rate. Moreover, the motor must be variable to account for different regolith types (water %, 
density, particle size distribution, etc.) and reversible. The higher degree of RPM accuracy of the motor controller, 
the more accurate the production target can be achieved. 
</p>
<p>The scope of this project did not include motor development. COTS motors were used for laboratory testing to 
prove system functionality and sized so the optimal power setting was mid-range to allow for an increase or 
decrease in RPM. A torque cell was used to monitor the torque required as a function of RPM and lunar simulant 
density. This project provided data used to verify motor size predictions. The current TRL for lunar auger motors 
operated in ground testing is TRL 4. Further research is required to develop a motor that can run continuously in 
the extreme lunar environment. The primary concern is rejecting heat in the lunar vacuum and operating in a dusty 
environment. The motor is installed on the auger discharge side to avoid conflict with dusty hopper operations, 
but there is still dust that will be produced from dry soil being discharged at the exit. 
</p>
<p>Three motor configurations were tested during mechanical testing and shown in 
Fig. 7. The first motor (Maxor 443 in-lb) was procured with the test article in 2017 
for Mars soil processing. One of the first observations made during ground testing 
was the increased torque required when using a Lunar versus Mars simulant. The 
Lunar simulant in an auger behaves more like flue dust (material factor for 
modelling) than dry sand (Mars simulant). The lunar simulants’ smaller particle size 
and increased cohesion required approximately 30% more torque for the same flow 
rate resulting in the gear box on the first motor failing. A COTS 1,200 in-lb motor 
was assembled that functioned for moving soil but demonstrated the importance of 
an accurate motor controller. Without the ability to variably control motor current 
to hit the desired RPM (based on torque fluctuations as soil packs and the discharge 
plug grows/shrinks as the discharge gate is closed/opened), the desired flow rates 
could not be obtained in steady state. This motor was replaced with a Maxor 817 in-
lb and used for all remaining mechanical tests. However, it was observed that wet 
simulant (mud) requires more torque to move than dry simulant. Ground testing in 
laboratory environment allowed ice to melt to measure the torque requirements in a 
worst case scenario. Spikes above the rated intermittent torque capacity were 
observed and a spur gear design was planned to mitigate this risk. Unfortunately the 
project was canceled prior to implementing the spur gear and the risk was accepted for the one and only thermal 
test performed.

<p>5. Cold Trap 
 A cold trap is a device that separates condensable vapors from a process gas mixture thorough a low 
temperature surface and is also referred to as a cryo-pump, cryo-trap, or Meissner trap in industry. The purpose of 
the cold trap subsystem as part of LADI is to convert the water vapor generated in the auger dryer into water ice 
and temporarily store until transported to the crater ridge for processing. Terrestrial cold traps and condensers are 
a common well-developed technology but operating in lunar conditions offers multiple challenges including but 
not limited to 1.) Low Lunar surface pressures lead to sublimated vapor, which limits traditional pressure-driven 
fluid transport methods, and 2.) Ice deposition (i.e. de-sublimation) in the cold trap chamber is limited by the low 
thermal conductivity of ice. While a generic cold trap subsystem can be designed to integrate with various upstream 
water processing subsystems (i.e. multiple small units in parallel), optimization is dependent on the environment 
(Moon or Mars) and upstream process; specifically flow rate, impurities, temperature, and pressure. The scope of 
the LADI project did not include cold trap technology development. However, government, academia, and industry 
are developing this technology independently, and partnerships were created to understand the benefits and 
challenges of each subsystem. 
</p>

<p>B. Thermal Models 
Thermal Desktop (TD) thermal models were created for both the lunar and laboratory environments with the 
</p>
<p>intention to prove concept feasibility and predict laboratory performance. The objectives include: 
• Predict performance and size the downstream cold trap (fur future vacuum testing) and the condenser (for 
</p>
<p>ground testing in laboratory). 
• Predict the heater set points required for ground testing. Specifically, determine the residence time required 
</p>
<p>to sublimate (vacuum) or vaporize (laboratory) the water ice in the regolith. 
• Determine the feasibility of creating water at the desired flow rates while operating below the triple point of 
</p>
<p>water in a lunar environment. This model uses simple geometric shapes that are scalable to allow predictions 
of performance for subscale, pilot, and full-scale water processing missions. 
</p>
<p>• Use geometry predictions from the screw conveyor dryer sizing tool to optimize heat transfer between the 
bulk particle motion and the heated housing due to the gap between the screw’s outer diameter and inner 
diameter of the auger casing. This allows a trade study of auger robustness versus thermal efficiency.  
</p>
<p>• Predict chiller setpoints and flow rates for maintaining ice inside the hopper during ground testing. Unique 
sublimation models and new property files were created for the extreme lunar environment increasing the 
state-of-art of lunar thermal modelling.  
</p>
<p>1. Water / Ice Properties 
The standard REFPROP 3-phase water property file could not be used due to the extreme lunar environment 
</p>
<p>requiring temperatures below the 258K minimum and an updated file could not be produced due to REFPROP 
software limitations. In Thermal Desktop, no single property file for 3-phase water was available for the expected 
operating range of 100K to 373K. Three different property files are used for water in this model created specifically 
for this project based on primary literature research 8, 9 for solid ice, solid ice / vapor, and liquid vapor. 
</p>
<p>2. Lunar Regolith Properties 
The regolith’s density, thermal conductivity, and specific heat are required by TD to estimate thermal 
</p>
<p>performance. The regolith and fluid’s thermal mass is modeled separately allowing the regolith’s density and 
specific heat correspond to dry regolith. The bulk density of dry regolith was assumed to be 1,300 kg/m3. The 
specific heat was obtained using the same equation from Woods-Robinson et al. 10 as in the Screw Conveyer Dryer 
Model. Since the regolith has more radial temperature divisions than the fluid, to not be overly conservative the 
thermal conductivity of wet regolith was used. The thermal conductivity is calculated assuming a heterogeneous 
two-phase mixture with components in parallel, which is the estimated upper limit for such a mixture 10. The lunar 
auger and cold-trap subsystem design intends to operate below the triple point of water (273.16K and 611.657 Pa). 
Therefore, water will only exist in the solid or vapor phase. Below the triple point of water the thermal conductivity 
of ice is used and above the triple point the thermal conductivity of vapor is used. The thermal conductivity of dry 
regolith is taken as the average of thermal conductivity fits of Apollo 11, 12, and 14 data 14, 15, 16 for fines with bulk 
densities of 1,300 kg/m3. 
</p>
<p>3. Lunar Auger Dryer ISRU Model 
The lunar model treats regolith drying as a continuous 
</p>
<p>process. The auger dryer housing, shaft, and silica insulation are 
modeled as finite difference solids. The heater is modeled as a 
finite difference shell. The regolith is modeled as a finite 
difference solid with advection and heat transfer occurring in 
one direction. This allows TD to treat the regolith as a moving 
material with a specified velocity. The regolith is modeled as a 
slice of a hollow cylinder. It’s inner and outer radius contact the 
shaft and case respectively. Its angle is calculated to match the fullness percentage in each auger dryer subsection 
(inlet, heated, outlet). This simple geometry allows the auger dryer subsystem to be modeled without meshing and 
its dimensions to be easily changed enabling parametric scaling studies. An example thermal model of a pilot plant 
auger in the lunar environment on simple CLPS lander is shown in Fig. 14.  
</p>

each lump can contain multiple phases (e.g. solid/vapor). Each phase is assumed to 
be in perfect equilibrium for simplicity. The model outputs the temperature, 
pressure, and quality of each volume. Water is split into four separate paths, each 
with three control volumes as shown in Fig. 15 (heaters and insulation is not shown).

<p>5. Hopper Cooling Loop Model

not accurately simulate lunar feed conditions.

<p>6. Discrete Element Model Simulation 
Discrete Element Method (DEM) is a particle-scale numerical modeling technique for the bulk behavior of 
</p>
<p>granular materials such as coal, ores, soil, rocks, aggregates, pellets, and powders. This method allows millions of 
particles to be simulated allowing the study of dynamics at steady state, across transition boundaries, and even 
under alternate gravity environments. However, DEM is computationally intensive and simulation durations can 
take weeks to months to complete. While multiple simulations can be run in parallel, computational time must be 
reduced to be able to use the predictions in the next design iteration. The strategy adopted was to develop a 
converging physics model that can be verified and validated with laboratory test data. These models can then be 
altered to observe the effects on flow rate and plug formation when motor RPM, regolith density, or auger 
geometry is modified. For example, after a model is completed and verified, changing the auger geometry (pitch, 
casing and screw diameter, shaft diameter, etc.) to optimize flow would be significantly less expensive than 
removing an auger from the system, modifying in machine shop (or procuring a new auger), then reinstalling. This 
tool can also be used to make predictions on scaling the auger for subscale, pilot plant, or full scale missions. DEM 
simulations can significantly save on ground test costs.  
</p>
<p>In a series of related NASA SBIR contracts spanning multiple years, Grainflow developed 
simulation tools and tested ISRU hardware for storing, handling, conveying, and size-sorting 
regolith. Grainflow’s inter-particle force models varied particle size, shape, and cohesion 
to validate bulk physical deformation and flow behavior using the lunar mare simulant 
JSC-1A. The irregularly shaped lunar regolith was modelled using the 4-sphere 
tetrahedral ‘clusters’ shown in Fig. 22 and demonstrated that sphere-cluster particles generate rotational resistance 
and bulk material shear-dilatant effects mimicking to the physical response of granular solids. While planned for 
future simulations, LADI used spherical particles to simplify the model and reduce computational time. For 
engineering-scale simulations, scaling for the inter-particle interactions demonstrated that assemblies of larger 
‘computational particles’ can be used as low-resolution surrogates to approximate the bulk deformation and flow 
behavior of lunar regolith and further reduce computational time. This confirmed that computational time can be 
reduced while maintaining a realistic simulation by increasing each particle’s size and cohesion force while 
reducing the total number of particles in the system.  
</p>
<p>The image in Fig. 23 was generated by Lawrence Livermore 
National Laboratory’s VisIt open source visualization package 
running on a Linux PC. The goal of the model was to examine 
transition effects between the three auger sections at steady state 
and predict plug behavior at the inlet and outlet. The casing shown 
on top as an empty auger with a reduced length heated section; the 
bottom shows clear casing and is composed of static assemblies of 
400K 2.5 mm small spherical particles turning at 6.7 RPM. The auger 
and shaft rotate in unison as a single rigid body about the z-axis. To reduce processing time per run, the model was 
developed as a condensed version of the breadboard test stand. The inlet and outlet shaft and auger flight 
dimensions are identical to the breadboard test article while the length of the heated section was reduced from 8-
ft to 1.5-ft. The primary drawback of this approach is the lack of fine definition of a true particle-scale flow 
behavior. However, verification of the modeled behavior against observed ground testing provided confidence in 
the simulation-predicted behavior. 
</p>
<p>During ground testing, clogging was observed in the inlet tube from 
the hopper feeding the auger. Based on this information, the DEM auger 
simulation was modified to include a vertical section of flow coming 
from the hopper as shown in Fig. 24. Several simulations were completed 
using different cohesion force values to optimize processing time in 
relation to particle size. Cohesion is increased in the model to make a 
fewer number of “large” particles stick together and behave like a bulk 
fluid of small particles (e.g. cohesion is not noticeable between two 
marbles). Unfortunately, the project was canceled shortly after a 
converging model was completed preventing a low-g simulation from 
being run. The runs that were in progress were allowed to complete but 
were never converted into video. 
</p>

<p>7. Integrated SE&amp;I Modelling 
The MAIT software was developed by the Jacobs Engineering team within the Crew and Thermal Systems 
</p>
<p>Division (EC) at JSC. The purpose of the tool is to create a system analysis framework using MATLAB that can 
interface with existing subsystem models. This framework uses MATLAB and Excel, where model integration 
and analysis is performed via MATLAB and system model setup is performed in Excel or Thermal Desktop. This 
combines a user-friendly Excel based interface with a powerful numerical analysis tool. The goal is to develop the 
capability to swap in different subsystem models and compare a full ISRU system solution where component-level 
changes interact with connected subsystems and optimization is performed at the system level. For example, this 
tool can then be used to compare an oxygen production system versus a water production system with all the 
associated subsystem models included using the same mission architectural assumptions. The flow chart in Fig. 
25 shows how MAIT interfaces with subsystem models to perform different analysis functions. 
</p>
<p>The LADI team worked closely with the MAIT programmers to integrate the SCD sizing tool, the LADI thermal 
model, and GRC’s ice growth model (for tank sizing). The strength of this tool is its ability to handle complex 
interactions by using the output of one subsystem as the input for another and integrate separate complex subsystem 
models into one simulation. Recycle loops are supported, MAIT will iterate through the affected subsystems until 
convergence is achieved. Depending on user defined requirements, the model can either be run either parametrically 
or in optimization mode (using MATLAB’s Optimization Toolbox). Parametric analyses examine the effects of 
specific parameters on the outputs. Specifically, MATLAB is used to determine and run all possible combinations 
of inputs within a defined range. Optimization allows searching the outputs for minima, maxima, or a target value 
(i.e. mass or power). Fig. 26 demonstrates the complexity of a lunar water processing simulation. Each arrow 
represents an input or output required for system optimization. These are the mechanical and electrical interfaces 
between each subsystem that must be defined and are the requirements used for sizing each subsystem. When 
developed together, the system will function as a single unit. 
</p>

<p>V. Ground Testing 
The goal of ESTA test 8I055 was to demonstrate the mechanical feasibility and thermal performance characteristics 
of the Lunar Auger Dryer for ISRU (LADI) breadboard screw conveyor dryer (SCD) at ambient pressure and 
temperature conditions using a high fidelity Lunar regolith simulant. As described in the subsequent breadboard test 
stand description, the test article is unique because it can be reconfigured for either observing flow or heated 
operations. Ground testing was divided into two phases, mechanical and thermal testing.  
</p>
<p>• Phase I - The primary objective of mechanical testing was to verify inlet and outlet plug seal performance 
on the breadboard test stand. A plastic casing was used to allow visual observation of both warm and cold 
simulant properties while measuring and calculating parameters such as percentage full, torque, and mass 
flow rates as a function of motor speed (RPM). The simulant’s plug capability to seal was tested by adding 
an inert, low-pressure gas source near the vapor outlet. This phase did not include heated operations. 
</p>
<p>• Phase II - The primary objective of thermal testing was to determine the optimal parameters for condensing 
water as lunar simulant is transported and heated through a stainless-steel auger casing. Water ice was 
mixed in with the simulant to model Lunar conditions and the original plan included testing both a 
homogenous and a heterogenous simulant/ice mixture. The thermal aspects of heating the simulant, water 
release rates, and the ability to seal at both auger ends without the use of isolation valves at elevated 
temperature was observed.  
</p>
<p>The test matrix and schematic for the planned mechanical and thermal test phases is in Appendix A-F respectively. 
Unfortunately the project was canceled before completing the planned test matrix; only one thermal run was 
performed. It was originally planned to test two heater configurations (see schematic B and C) to compare the yield 
to residence time. The test matrix is included for potential future work.  
</p>

• Create a soil column to strengthen the inlet plug and allow higher water vapor

<p>5. Discharge Gate 
The purpose of the discharge gate is to manipulate the length of the discharge plug 
</p>
<p>during operation. Typically, longer plugs can withstand higher internal auger pressures 
but require stronger motors due to the increased torque demands required by more 
material travelling through the auger. As shown in Fig. 31, each of the variable augers 
tested have flights that end before the discharge gate. In general, the farther the distance 
is from the end of the flights to the discharge gate, the longer the plug length. However 
the plug will not translate through the auger casing indefinitely due to the friction 
caused by the cohesion of the simulant and its ability to compress (increasing in 
density). This can lead to formation of an immovable plug that causes backflow thru 
the auger. DEM models can predict this behavior but must be verified with testing. As 
simulant continues to travel through the auger and accumulates at the end of the auger 
flights, a pile is formed as more simulant is pushed towards the exit. The more cohesive 
the soil, the faster the plug is formed.  
</p>
<p>The discharge gate promotes plug formation by restricting flow when partially or 
fully closed, but it never completely prevents the soil from exiting the auger. This is 
due to the auger shaft penetrating the gate and creating a “U” shape as shown in Fig. 
38 (Top). The discharge box cover is removed in this figure although a clear cover was 
available for mechanical testing and a stainless steel cover for thermal testing. One of 
the secondary mechanical test objectives was to determine the optimal discharge gate 
angle for maintaining a steady state plug length by observing the plug formation over 
various test conditions. For breadboard ground testing, the discharge gate lever was operated manually, i.e. the 
discharge gate can be opened or closed by moving the gate lever. The optional weights shown in Fig. 38 (Bottom) 
were never required to maintain plug formation.  
</p>
<p>The typical operational procedure was to start testing with the gate closed to restrict flow out the discharge. If 
the torque approached the maximum torque rating of the motor, the gate was fully opened to decrease flow 
resistance and plug length. The gate was later closed again if the plug could not maintain the desired internal auger 
pressure. A level sensor was attached to the gate lever to record the gate angle measured during testing as shown 
in Fig. 38 (Bottom). However, an optimal gate angle could not be determined due to the many variables associated 
with testing, including but not limited to RPM, soil density, soil compressibility, and inlet auger pressure.  
</p>
<p>A future design should include a gate that opens and closes vertically significantly reducing the length of the 
discharge box (eliminating swing). By measuring the torque required for moving the lunar feedstock at the RPM 
settings needed to hit production targets, the discharge gate can be programmed to open or close with a linear 
actuator based on the torque feedback from the motor controller. 
</p>

<p>3. Heaters and Thermal Control Panel 
The purpose of the heaters is to increase the wet 
</p>
<p>regolith’s temperature to sublimation by heating the 
feedstock through the auger’s drying section.  Ideally the 
heaters will be integrated into the casing radially to 
prevent ice deposition inside the auger walls and may 
apply the heat at a constant or variable rate. The heaters 
and thermal control panel used for thermal testing was 
initially sized for Mars water processing and shown in Fig. 
44. An FY20 formulation study3 demonstrated that despite 
the different regolith flow rates and inlet temperatures from 
Mars compared to the Lunar environment, the systems are similar. In short, the heaters must break hydrated bonds 
to release water on Mars while the Moon feedstock starts at cryogenic temperatures and must heat ice to 
sublimation resulting in similar intensive power requirements. The flow rates are based on assumed water content 
in the soil and mission production targets; therefore, the mass flow rates are similar and can be adjusted by varying 
the RPM on the motor. 
</p>
<p>Heat is supplied to the simulant through the auger casing wall by nine resistive band heaters clamped to the 
outer diameter of the casing. The heaters are controlled from 3 independent zones (with 3 heaters per zone) using 
internal temperature feedback ramping up to a desired output temperature setting. The heat profile can be 
programmed to handle a wide range of test scenarios and residence times such as modeled in the ‘Lunar Auger 
Dryer ISRU Model’. The breadboard test stand also can be reconfigured into two different length configurations 
by manipulating the heater location as shown in Fig. 45.  This allows alternate residence times to be tested. For 
example, for the same motor RPM settings, the power sent to heaters in the short auger configuration (top) can be 
increased to rapidly vaporize water while the long auger configuration (bottom) can use reduced power and 
increase residence time for the same effect at exit.

<p>4. Condenser and Chiller 
The condenser is a relic of laboratory ground testing. The design and configuration of the condenser and chiller 
</p>
<p>selected is discussed in the ‘Condenser &amp; Vapor Inlet Model’ Thermal Model Section. In the lunar environment, 
a cold trap will need to be designed to match water vapor flow rates exiting LADI with a radiator large enough to 
disperse the heat radiatively. Multiple cold trap designs are discussed in Technical Approach ‘Cold Trap’ Section.  
</p>
<p>VI. Conclusion 
Nineteen mechanical test runs and four functional/thermal runs were completed using Exolith Lab’s Lunar Highlands 
Simulant (LHS-1) increasing the breadboard system to TRL 4. Five unique auger geometries, and three motors 
configurations were used to optimize steady state flow. Torque, RPM, mass flow rate, gate angle, and power were 
measured while observing discharge plug behavior. Plots and video of each run, as well as detailed documentation 
discussing the test facility used, test procedures, and frozen-ice simulant preparation are available but outside the 
scope of this paper. Despite the abrupt stop due to budget constraints, LADI successfully made progress on multiple 
lunar water extraction capability gaps. A mechanical and thermal test regime was completed proving concept 
feasibility, and the data was used to validate numerous models. These subsystem models were integrated into a system 
level ISRU model allowing parametric studies to be rapidly performed leaving NASA in a strong position to continue 
the technology development in the future. Some potential starting points for future work include: 
</p>
<p>• Create an optimized system solution by defining key system integration interfaces using updated subsystem 
hopper and cold trap models.  
</p>
<p>• Use DEM simulations to optimize flow and mixing as well as make predictions in low gravity. Depending 
on these results, an auger using additive manufacturing may allow more flexibility in the lunar design. 
</p>
<p>• Use MAIT SE&amp;I model to design both a pilot plan and full-scale EDU designed to operate inside a thermal 
vacuum chamber.  
</p>
<p>• Explore EDU of a subscale unit designed to fit inside a suborbital rocket’s locker or as a payload on a 
Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) mission. This small scale design could be flow in 
microgravity to identity and mitigate solid / vapor flow risk in lunar gravity. 
</p>
<p>• Explore a modified auger design using a casing surrounding the auger’s heated section to create a shell and 
tube heat exchanger to replace the resistance band heaters. The working fluid could be heated by beaming 
power into the PSR from the ridge. 
</p>
<p>• Research possibility of integrating ion transport technology (between auger water vapor discharge and cold 
trap) developed by UTEP’s LuSTR research. 
</p>
<p>• Complete the thermal test matrix and update the thermal model. 
• Depending on the results of sample return prospecting missions, consider using a higher fidelity simulant 
</p>
<p>for qualification and acceptance testing. 
• Complete long-term abrasion testing of the inner casing diameter using a lunar simulant (essentially 
</p>
<p>sandpaper). 
• Complete a Finite Element Analysis (FEA) trade study to reduce the risk of deflection of the casing during 
</p>
<p>vibration launch and shock from landing when using a thin walled casing. 
</p>
<p>When LADI is picked back up by either NASA, industry, or an international partner, there are numerous options and 
tools available to continue technology development. 
</p>
