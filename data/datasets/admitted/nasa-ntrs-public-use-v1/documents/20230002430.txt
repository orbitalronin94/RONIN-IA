The Active Response Gravity Offload System (ARGOS) at NASA Johnson Space Center 



(JSC) is an analog environment that can offload pressurized suited subjects for various 



reduced gravity simulations. The suit is suspended from a robotic overhead crane by a cable 



connected to the suit via a gimbal with an adjustable pivot point (i.e. offload attachment). 



There has been increased interest in providing planetary pressurized suited training at 



ARGOS in preparation for lunar missions. Determination of the appropriate gimbal pivot 



point location for a given subject is vital for a high-fidelity functional lunar simulation. 



Interactions between the pivot point location and human-spacesuit center of gravity (CG) can 



result in righting moments that may lead to artificially stable or unrealistically challenging 



configurations. Changing the pivot point location is time consuming and repeated adjustment 



can result in loss of valuable pressurized suited time. This paper aims to share knowledge 



obtained from the offloading characterization efforts during pressurized suited testing at 



ARGOS and document the ongoing process to define an appropriate pivot point location 



through iterative quantitative and qualitative assessments. Human-spacesuit CG locations for 



the ARGOS lunar simulation were estimated using a 3D body scan and density model 



combined with spacesuit hardware CAD and specifications. Early pilot testing of the gimbal 



revealed that setting the pivot point coincident with the modeled CG location was not always 



possible due to the current gimbal design, and small pivot point shifts had noticeable effects 



on subject stability. Fourteen subjects performed a series of CG-related tasks in the 



Exploration Extravehicular Mobility Unit (xEMU) to assess simulation characteristics. 



Through iterative testing, this task list evolved to streamline the process needed to efficiently 



identify a suitable pivot point for a given subject. The developed methodology will be critical 



for pivot point selection during astronaut training in the ARGOS environment.

I. Introduction 



RESSURIZED spacesuit testing in a lunar analog environment has been gaining increased interest in recent 



years as teams prepare for a return to the Moon. Numerous analog environments have been developed by NASA1-



3, and all come with different advantages and limitations. The Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory (NBL) offers unthetered 



freedom of movement and a large work volume with multiple suited operation capability, however water drag impacts 



dynamic motions. Parabolic flight provides realistic reduced gravity offload, but the cabin volume is limited and task 



durations can’t exceed 30 s1. The Active Response Gravity Offload System (ARGOS) at NASA Johnson Space Center 



(JSC) is an analog environment which provides a reduced gravity simulation at any chosen gravity level between 0-



1g4,5. ARGOS expanded the limitations of previous offload devices such as the partial gravity simulator (POGO) by 



additng translational capability, improving gimbal mechanisms, and enhancing simulation quality through robotic 



control1,4,6. The system actively tracks and follows the subject’s translational motions while providing a constant force 



offload (e.g. 1/6g) through a feedback-controlled overhead robotic crane4. 



The primary ARGOS gimbal mechanism that has been used for pressurized testing is the Spreader Bar Exploration 



Gimbal paired with an exploration PLSS (Portable Life Support System) mockup, or MockPLSS. The MockPLSS is 



a weighted rigid structure that attaches to the Exploration Extravehicular Mobility Unit (xEMU) hatch to mimic the 



mass of an exploration PLSS, or xPLSS, and simultaneously provides an attachment point for the gimbal. Rotational 



movements occur through a passive gimballing mechanism that attaches to the MockPLSS at a common pivot point 



for pitch and roll, and a common axis of rotation overhead for yaw. Thus, the lifting/offloading of the human-spacsuit 



occurs through one location on the gimbal called the gimbal 



pivot point. Adjustment of this gimballing mechanism is a 



non-trivial process as the overall system center of gravity (CG) 



changes across subjects and postures. Given that there are 



hundreds of possible gimbal configurations, a standardized 



and repeatable approach was developed to determine a 



subject’s gimbal setting. This required calculation of the CG 



of the overall system and computer modeling of how this CG 



location correlates to the actual gimbal hardware pivot point 



positioning. This paper highlights the methods used to 



calculate and model the system CG as well as the results 



gained through various measures across multiple xEMU 



subjects participating in engineering tests conducted at 



ARGOS.

B. Correlating the System CG to the Gimbal Hardware 



 



1. Gimbal Mechanism Mapping 



 The ARGOS gimbal mechanism consists of three layers of adjustment capability. The first is a slider along the 



side of the MockPLSS that offers three different positions spaced 1.0 in (2.5 cm) apart. The second and third levels of 



adjustability are provided by a sidearm piece with 11 linear bolt positions that can also be rotated around a mounting 



plate in 16 different clocking orientations (Figure 4b). This adjustablility provides for 528 different gimbal positions 



across a wide area. Numbering and color schemes were applied to the mapping to quickly translate pivot point dots 



on the map to actual hardware settings during testing (Figure 5). This mapping scheme and translation method between 



the computer models and the actual hardware had not previously been put into practice at ARGOS and made for much 



more effective real-time identification of new pivot point settings when needed during testing. 



2. Pivot Point Selection 



For each new subject-suit-gimbal environment, the system CG is calculated and aligned with the ARGOS gimbal 



hardware model. In early testing, pivot points were selected systematically to understand the characteristics of the 



system. Currently, an initial target pivot point is identified based on previous test experience, anthropometric similarity 



to previous test subjects, and the objectives of the given test day. The test conductors then determine a pivot point as

C. Gimbal Evaluation Tasks 



The tasks used to evaluate the suitability of a given gimbal setting for a particular subject have evolved throughout 



testing as more experience and knowledge was gained on what tasks were most effective at identifying sub-optimal 



settings. The tasks generally focused on analyzing whole-body sway and reaches commonly used in external load 



carriage CG assessments8,9 as well as functional tasks such as object pickup and prone-to-recover. An example of the 



gimbal evaluation tasks used is shown in Table 1. While not comprehensive of all of the expected tasks of a planetary 



EVA, the list shown represents tasks that were found to be challenging and characteristic of unstable or overly stable 



planetary EVA performance. This downsized list allowed time for multiple pivot point setting assessments with each 



subject to assess an appropriate setting for the rest 



of the test objectives of the day. For example, if 



lunar geology tasks were the main focus of an 



ARGOS simulation, then more time was given to 



find a setting to best accomplish those tasks, as 



opposed to tests where walking was the primary 



objective which is easier to manage in less optimal 



settings. By putting brand new subjects through 



these challenging postures and balance limits at the 



start of the test, we were also able to minimize the 



impacts of general learning effect in the ARGOS 



suited environment on subsequent objectives for the 



run. Testing of these tasks was deemed as non-



human research since it was hardware evaluation-



focused, thus not subject to the NASA Institutional 



Review Board (IRB), however the protocol was 



reviewed and approved through the NASA Test 



Readiness Review (TRR) process.

III. Analysis 



A. Identifying the Pivot Point 



“Functional Zone” 



Through these tasks and 



subjective feedback, generalized 



findings were identified in regards to what can be expected for settings that fall within the “functional zone” which 



lies above and aft of the system CG. Settings that are too far forward of the system CG resuled in extreme hunching 



(Figure 6), while settings that are too low tend to have a very unstable knife-edge tippiness at the waist. Both types of 



settings result in high cognitive effort to manage the resulting moments. Settings that are high and aft are less 



challenging to manage, however if the pivot point is too high it can feel like the gimbal is artificially pulling up and 



restricts leaning postures, while if it’s too aft there are unrealistically comfortable upright and sitting postures (Figure 



7) that are possible, and in both cases it becomes difficult to assume and maintain ground-based postures. 



A dedicated pivot point assessment test allowed for 10 different pivot point settings to be tested on one experienced 



xEMU subject on the same day (Figure 8a). In this dedicated test there were several pivot point settings tested in both 



the fore/aft direction, as well as up/down, system CG-coincident, and forward of the system CG. Up until this point 



there were limited opportunities to test more than two settings per subject, and those changes typically focused on the 



fore-aft direction. The feedback gained through testing these settings in rapid succession, repeating the same tasks 



over and over, provided great insight into the bounds of the functional zone. Comparing metrics across the ten settings,

The functional zone was further refined with subsequent subjects who were allotted 2-4 different pivot point

sensitive than up/down shifts. Even as small as a 0.2 in (0.6 cm) fore/aft shift was noticeable by subjects, whereas 



up/down shifts of as much as 1.0 in (2.5 cm) showed minimal performance differences. Variability within the 



functional zone still exists across subjects in how well they can accomplish a variety of tasks, in part due to differences 



in anthropometry and flexibility. However, placing the pivot point in this region has been shown to provide consistent 



reduced gravity effects and has been effective for completing planetary EVA-type tasks.

B. Lessons Learned and Forward Work 



1. Learning Effect 



Although our evaluations were not aimed at assessing the existence or duration of a learning effect at ARGOS, it 



was apparent that it does exist for every new subject. Experienced subjects with a pre-defined gimbal setting are ready 



to go within minutes of leaving the donning stand, but new subjects need a half to a full day of experience before they 



are familiarized enough to move through tasks comfortably. There are multiple techniques used to complete various 



tasks, and subjects differ in what they prefer. There are also postures like kneeling to pick up an object that require 



proper foot placement that can be difficult for less experienced subjects to target initially. Allowing them time to try 



different methods to find what works best for them at the beginning of the day makes the rest of the test day more 



efficient. It has been observed that the learning effect will impact test objectives, thus it is recommended to account 



for necessary time for subjects to become familiar with the test environment. 



 



2. Dedicated CG Runs 



Dedicated CG evaluation assessments where a subject can continuously evaluate multiple gimbal settings greatly 



improves our understanding of the suit-gimbal interactions. By testing multiple settings at the fringes and extremes of 



the zone we were already working within we were able to better define the “functional zone” as well as form a better 



picture of what “bad” settings look like in each direction of pivot point movement. This greatly supported our ability 



to quickly evaluate a subject’s posture and listen to their feedback to efficiently adjust the pivot point to improve any 



gimbal-related challenges they were experiencing. A dedicated CG run is highly recommended for any new suit in the 



ARGOS environment, keeping in mind that the subject must also have sufficient experience before evaluating settings 



for comparative data. 



 



3. Human Factors of Gimbal Design 



The current bar gimbal was designed to maximize the options for pivot point placement relative to the hardware 



while maintaining critical factors of safety. No input was available at the time on where the pivot point should be or 



how often it might need to be adjusted. This study highlighted the importance of human factors in the design of 



hardware that interfaces directly with subjects and engineers operating ARGOS in real time. Every pivot point

IV. Conclusion 



Suited operations at ARGOS have significantly increased in recent years as it is expected to become an important 



training facility for lunar exploration missions. An important concern about any analog environment is how 



realistically it represents the environment it’s strying to mimic. While we recognize the importance of achieving a 



“realistic” lunar simulation at ARGOS, we do not currently have comparable data to be able to say what postures and 



methods of task completion are realistic with our current pivot point selections. This will require data from parabolic 



flight or actual lunar analysis in a modern planetary spacesuit to make these comparisons. In lieu of such data, we 



carefully choose pivot point settings that minimize any obvious gimbal influence to the subject. A dynamic gimbal 



which can sense and adjust the subject’s overall system CG throughout a variety of tasks would help to mitigate the 



limitations of the static model predictions, however this technology is not yet available. Through continuous testing, 



the functional zone definitions we have developed for the xEMU has consistently been shown to be an effective and 



acceptable means to accomplish test team objectives and is currently being applied toward other suit architectures 



being utilized at ARGOS.
