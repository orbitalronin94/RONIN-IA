<p>ABSTRACT 
</p>
<p>NASA has developed an expendable heavy lift launch vehicle capability, the Space Launch System (SLS), to support lunar 
</p>
<p>and deep space exploration.  The uncrewed Artemis I was the first flight of this new launch vehicle and tested critical systems 
</p>
<p>for the upcoming crewed Artemis II flight to the moon.  Accelerations were recorded at a multitude of locations on Artemis, 
</p>
<p>the Mobile Launcher (ML), and the Crawler Transporter (CT)during the rollout of Artemis I from the Vehicle Assembly 
</p>
<p>Building (VAB) to Launch Pad 39B March 2022 and is referred to as the Artemis I Dynamic Rollout Test (DRT).  While 
</p>
<p>Artemis I was at Launch Pad 39B, the Wet Dress Rehearsal (WDR) was performed to demonstrate launch readiness and 
</p>
<p>acceleration measurements were also recorded.  Finally, Artemis I rolled back from Launch Pad 39B to the VAB in April 
</p>
<p>2022, where acceleration measurements were also recorded and is referred to as the rollback portion of DRT. 
</p>
<p>Because the forces during rollout and at the launch pad acting on Artemis I, the ML, and the CT are not directly measurable, 
</p>
<p>Operational Modal Analysis (OMA) techniques, instead of traditional Experimental Modal Analysis (EMA) techniques, were 
</p>
<p>used to identify modal characteristics.  The OMA analysis of DRT and WDR directly builds upon the lessons learned from 
</p>
<p>the OMA analysis of an earlier rollout of the ML from the VAB. 
</p>
<p>DRT and WDR dynamic characteristics will be used to support SLS Integrated Modal Test finite element model correlation 
</p>
<p>efforts and Exploration Ground System ML and CT finite element model verification and validation, which are part of the 
</p>
<p>Building Block approach the Space Launch System program has implemented.  The dynamic characteristics extracted from 
</p>
<p>DRT as well as the rollout acceleration time histories themselves will be used in the development of generic rollout forcing 
</p>
<p>functions that will provide refined estimates of the Artemis IV rollout forces, which will have the heavier and larger SLS 
</p>
<p>Block 1B launch vehicle and Mobile Launcher 2 (ML-2).   
</p>
<p>This paper briefly describes Artemis I, the ML, and the CT physical characteristics, DRT rollout/rollback and WDR data 
</p>
<p>collection, the challenges in implementing OMA techniques due in part to the CT harmonics, and how these challenges were 
</p>
<p>overcome to obtain the Artemis I DRT configuration and WDR configuration modal characteristics.  </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Keywords:  Artemis I, Operational Modal Analysis, NASA, Rollout, Space Launch System, Keyword Four 
</p>
<p>INTRODUCTION 
</p>
<p>While the rollout environment has historically produced relatively small launch vehicle structural loads for the National 
</p>
<p>Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Apollo and Space Shuttle programs in comparison to launch and ascent 
</p>
<p>loads, these relatively small structural loads are inputs to structural fatigue analyses [1-5].  The same holds true for the Space 
</p>
<p>Launch System (SLS) developed by NASA to support lunar and deep space exploration [6, 7].  Because the rollout forces are 
</p>
<p>not directly measurable, Operational Modal Analysis (OMA) techniques, instead of traditional Experimental Modal Analysis 
</p>
<p>(EMA) techniques, provides an empirical means to identify the integrated system rollout modal characteristics.  OMA 
</p>
<p>techniques were applied to the Artemis I Dynamic Rollout Test (DRT) and Wet Dress Rehearsal (WDR) test data to identify 
</p>
<p>their fundamental modes during rollout and while on-pad, respectively.  These identified modal characteristics will be used to 
</p>
<p>assist in model correlation efforts and to better understand the rollout environment.   
</p>
<p>   
Figure 1.  Artemis I rollout to Launch Pad 39B (left and center) and at launch pad (right) 
</p>
<p>The Artemis I DRT occurred in March 2022 (rollout) and April 2022 (rollback) and consisted of the Mobile Launcher (ML) 
</p>
<p>being transported by the Crawler Transporter (CT-2) while transporting the Artemis I integrated vehicle from the VAB to 
</p>
<p>Launch Pad 39B (rollout) and back to the VAB (rollback).  The Artemis I integrated vehicle had the SLS Block I launch 
</p>
<p>vehicle, which will also be used for Artemis II and Artemis III flights.  Figure 1 shows the CT transporting Artemis I 
</p>
<p>integrated vehicle on the ML out to Launch Pad 39B during DRT.  When the CT is moving, below 15 Hz the primary 
</p>
<p>excitation comes from the rollout ground forces generated by the CT with the major contributors being the impulsive forces 
</p>
<p>generated by the CT Truck Tread Shoes making initial contact with the ground and when they pass under the CT Truck 
</p>
<p>rollers.  Lateral oscillations of the interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage Umbilical (iCPSU) that are being driven by the rollout 
</p>
<p>ground forces can be seen in the video of the Artemis I DRT rollout around 2:57:37 elapse time [8].  When the CT is stopped 
</p>
<p>or when the ML and Artemis I integrated vehicle are at Launch Pad 39B, where the ML is being supported on the six large 
</p>
<p>steel posts referred to as the Mount Mechanisms (MM), the primary excitation consists of wind loading on the ML Tower 
</p>
<p>and the Artemis I integrated vehicle.  The Wet Dress Rehearsal (WDR), which occurred between the DRT rollout and 
</p>
<p>rollback, consisted of the ML being supported on the six MM at Launch Pad 39B.  Figure 1 shows Artemis I integrated 
</p>
<p>vehicle on the ML sitting at Launch Pad 39B during the WDR.  In addition, when the ML was at Launch Pad 39B it was 
</p>
<p>supported with four Extensible Columns positioned near the perimeter of the flame hole providing additional vertical support 
</p>
<p>to the ML.  Extensible Columns are not a new concept and were used to support the ML used for Apollo Saturn V launches.  
</p>
<p>Figure 2 shows the Extensible Columns and the Side Flame Deflectors.  Also at Launch Pad 39B, the ML was connected to 
</p>
<p>the Ignition Over Pressure Sound Suppression (IOPSS) water supply piping.  The IOPSS piping and particularly the 
</p>
<p>Extensible Columns significantly change the dynamic characteristics of the ML from that seen inside the VAB where it is 
</p>
<p>only supported on the six MM’s.   </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>  
</p>
<p>Figure 2.  Launch Pad 39B extensible columns and side flame deflectors 
</p>
<p>OMA assumes the system is linear time invariant and the excitation is broadband random in nature, spatially distributed, 
</p>
<p>spatially uncorrelated, and stationary.  There are three challenges with applying OMA techniques to the DRT Rollout and 
</p>
<p>Rollback.  The first challenge is the excitation is not broadband random in nature nor is it spatially distributed when the CT is 
</p>
<p>moving.  Instead, when the CT is moving the primary excitation is generated by the CT Trucks.  The CT Trucks produce 
</p>
<p>impulsive forces from their tracks, which produces vibration that transmit up through the CT into the ML and the Artemis I 
</p>
<p>integrated vehicle.  These impulsive force time histories form two sets of dominant harmonic excitations that manifest 
</p>
<p>themselves as narrowband Gaussian random processes in the frequency domain.  Harmonic excitation of a system can lead 
</p>
<p>both the OMA time-domain and frequency-domain techniques to incorrectly identify a system harmonic response as true 
</p>
<p>structural modes.  If the CT harmonics were composed of sinusoidal excitations having nearly constant amplitudes and 
</p>
<p>frequencies, current OMA harmonic reduction tools are well suited to remove them from the measured acceleration time 
</p>
<p>histories and thus avoid this error.  However, because the CT harmonics are narrowband Gaussian random processes, which 
</p>
<p>lie in the frequency range of the fundamental modes, it is simply not possible to remove them without removing the signal 
</p>
<p>content from the structural modes of the integrated system.  The second challenge is the high modal density and many closely 
</p>
<p>spaced modes.  The rule of thumb for OMA analysis is that time histories having a duration of 1000 time periods of the 
</p>
<p>lowest frequency mode are sufficient.  Due to closely spaced modes, 1000 time periods may not be sufficient and 
</p>
<p>significantly longer durations may be needed.  The third challenge is the Artemis I DRT and WDR configurations have 
</p>
<p>significant nonlinear characteristics, which is in part due to the Vehicle Stabilization System (VSS) that connects the upper 
</p>
<p>portion of the SLS Core Stage to the ML Tower to stabilize and restrain the motion of Artemis I and the umbilicals. 
</p>
<p>The SLS program has implemented a Building Block approach [12 - 14] as part of its certification process for the Artemis I 
</p>
<p>flight and in refining the Artemis I rollout forcing functions.  As part of this, modal characteristics have been identified from 
</p>
<p>the modal test of the ML [9, 10], which was performed in June 2019 inside the VAB, the ISVV-010 rollout of the ML on the 
</p>
<p>CT from the VAB to Launch Pad 39B immediately following the ML Modal test [11], and the Integrated Modal Test (IMT) 
</p>
<p>performed in September 2021 inside the VAB of the integrated Artemis I vehicle on the ML.  It should be noted that for the 
</p>
<p>IMT neither the umbilicals nor the VSS were deployed nor were there extensible columns or IOPSS.  Hence the DRT and 
</p>
<p>WDR modal dynamics characteristics are significantly different from those of the IMT and therefore provide important 
</p>
<p>supplemental information to help reduce uncertainty in the test correlated Artemis I, ML, and CT Finite Element Models 
</p>
<p>(FEM’s) to achieve well test correlated FEM’s that play a key role in the Building Block approach.  
</p>
<p>This paper will briefly describe the Artemis I DRT and WDR configurations, how Artemis I DRT and WDR data was 
</p>
<p>collected, the challenges in implementing OMA techniques, and how these challenges were overcome to obtain the Artemis I 
</p>
<p>DRT and WDR configuration modal characteristics.   
</p>
<p>Mobile Launcher and Crawler Transporter 
</p>
<p>The Mobile Launcher (ML) is a very large and heavy steel structure designed to support SLS during its buildup and 
</p>
<p>integration inside the Vehicle Assembly Building (VAB), transportation from the VAB out to Launch Pad 39B, and provides 
</p>
<p>the launch platform at Launch Pad 39B.  The ML consists of the Tower and Base structures.  The ML Tower is an open 
</p>
<p>beam/truss steel structure weighs 4 million pounds, is 355 feet tall, and the upper levels are 40 square feet with floors every 
</p>
<p>20 feet for personnel access.  The ML Tower provides lateral support to the integrated SLS launch vehicle via the Vehicle 
</p>
<p>Stabilization System (VSS) and supports the fuel, power, and data umbilicals running to SLS and MPCV.  The ML Tower </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>also provides crew access to the Orion Multi-Purpose Crew Vehicle (MPCV) Crew Module (CM) via the Crew Access Arm 
</p>
<p>(CAA).  The ML Base is a two-story structure 25 feet high, 165 feet long, 135 feet wide, weighing 6 million pounds.  ML 
</p>
<p>weighs approximately 10.6 million pounds and is over 380 feet tall [15].  ML Deck 0, the top surface of the ML Base, 
</p>
<p>supports the SLS at eight attachment points located at the bottom of its two boosters via the ML Vehicle Support Posts 
</p>
<p>(VSP).  Figure 3 shows the layout of the ML umbilicals [16].  For the Artemis I DRT and WDR, the umbilicals and Vehicle 
</p>
<p>Stabilizer System (VSS) were connected, but the CAA was retracted and rotated to the side of the ML Tower as shown in 
</p>
<p>Figure 1. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 3.  ML umbilicals (from http://NASASpaceFlight.com) 
</p>
<p>The Crawler Transporter (CT) performs all lifting and transport operations of both the rollout of the ML with the SLS 
</p>
<p>integrated vehicle from the VAB to Launch Pad 39B and the rollback of the ML from Launch Pad 39B back into the VAB 
</p>
<p>after launch.  CT-2 is a recently upgraded version of one of the original CT’s used for the Apollo and Space Shuttle programs 
</p>
<p>[17 - 19] with their design based upon heavy mining machinery.  These upgrades allow the CT-2 to carry the heavier loading 
</p>
<p>imposed by the integrated SLS launch vehicle and ML.  CT-2 is the size of a baseball infield, with a top speed of 1 mph 
</p>
<p>loaded and 2 mph unloaded.  CT-2 weights 6.3 million pounds total, height of between 20 feet and 26 feet depending on its 
</p>
<p>jacking level.  Jacking, Equalization, and Leveling (JEL) of the ML/SLS is performed by the JEL hydraulic system.  Four 
</p>
<p>hydraulic cylinders at each corner are pressurized by redundant pumps and provide for lifting and leveling control of the 
</p>
<p>stack.  The JEL’s keep the stack level while negotiating the 5% incline of the ramp to Launch Pad 39B.  The JEL’s support 
</p>
<p>the weight of the CT chassis, 2.2 million pounds, and the weight of the ML and SLS.  For the Artemis I DRT the JEL’s 
</p>
<p>supported a total weight of 15.3 million pounds, 10.6 million pound ML, 2.2 million pound CT Chassis, and 3.5 million 
</p>
<p>pound Artemis I integrated vehicle.  The CT maximum load capacity is 18 million pounds.  The CT provides vertical support 
</p>
<p>for the ML at four (4) pickup points.  Lateral mechanical constraints for the ML are provided at three of the four pickup 
</p>
<p>points to minimize ML-to-CT support constraint stresses.  Figure 4 is an aerial view of the CT-2 showing the four pickup 
</p>
<p>points and the lateral mechanical constraints (yellow arrows) at each pickup point.  These lateral mechanical constraints at 
</p>
<p>each pickup point are designed to provide a lateral kinematic connection between the ML and CT so that ML does not impart 
</p>
<p>static lateral loads into the CT and vice versa.  Because of this Corner B has no lateral mechanical constraints.  However, 
</p>
<p>accelerometers mounted on the ML and CT sides of these ML/CT pickup points shows that friction prevents slipping from 
</p>
<p>occurring when the CT is transporting the ML. </p>
<p></p>
<div class="annotation"><a href="http://nasaspaceflight.com/">http://nasaspaceflight.com/</a></div>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 4.  Aerial view of CT-2 showing pickup points [5] 
</p>
<p>The CT has four trucks, each having two tracks.  Each CT truck weighs approximately 1 million pounds.  Each track has 57 
</p>
<p>shoes with each shoe weighing 2,100 lb.  Each track is driven by two 375 hp electric motors (four per truck) that are powered 
</p>
<p>by four 1,000 kW DC generators that in turn are powered by two Alco 16 cylinder diesel engines.  While the CT’s internal 
</p>
<p>drive train gearing does produce harmonic excitations, the most significant harmonic excitations are generated by the truck 
</p>
<p>tracks and rollers [2].  One set of dominant harmonics is due to the initial slap of the track shoes making contact with the 
</p>
<p>ground, referred to as the shoe slapping harmonics, which are a function of CT speed and shoe spacing.  Another set of 
</p>
<p>dominant harmonics is due to the track shoes passing underneath the 11 support rollers of each truck, referred to as the roller 
</p>
<p>crossing harmonics, which are also a function of the CT speed and the spacing between the support rollers.  The amplitude of 
</p>
<p>these track shoe and roller crossing harmonics is also a function of CT speed, with the amplitudes increasing with increasing 
</p>
<p>CT speed.  The Crawlerway, the 40 foot wide 4.2 mile long prepared surface the CT traverses between the VAB and Launch 
</p>
<p>Pad 39B, underwent repair 2013- 2014 timeframe to prepare the Crawlerway to be able support the transport of SLS [20].  
</p>
<p>The physical characteristics of the Crawlerway directly affect the reaction forces generated by the CT Trucks.  The 
</p>
<p>Crawlerway can handle weights of up to 26 million pounds.  It takes the CT approximately 8 hours to transport the ML or the 
</p>
<p>ML with SLS on it between the VAB and Launch Pad 39B. 
</p>
<p>Figure 5 shows the CT truck tracks and Figure 6 shows the relationship between these CT harmonics and the Artemis I DRT 
</p>
<p>configuration fundamental modes.  Note that these CT harmonics are dispersed within the frequency range of these 
</p>
<p>fundamental modes.  Although the harmonic frequencies of these two sets of CT harmonics remain relatively constant for a 
</p>
<p>constant CT speed, the continuous variations due to the shoes not engaging all rollers, variations in the amplitudes and 
</p>
<p>relative phasing between the impulsive CT shoe forces, and variation in the Crawlerway surface results in the CT harmonics 
</p>
<p>manifesting themselves in the frequency domain as narrowband Gaussian random processes (i.e., kurtosis close to 3).  This 
</p>
<p>makes it very challenging to model analytically and to remove the effect of these CT harmonics.  Figure 7 shows the CT 
</p>
<p>speed profiles during both the DRT rollout and rollback.  Note that the CT does not turn around, but simply drives in reverse 
</p>
<p>when traveling from Launch Pad 39B back to the VAB, hence the CT speeds during the DRT rollback are negative.  All 
</p>
<p>times are given with respect to the Eastern Daylight Savings Time (EDT) time zone. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 5.  CT during ISVV-010 rollout June 2019 (left) and rollout conducted September 2019 (right) </p>

<p>ARTEMIS I DRT AND WDR INSTRUMENTATION 
</p>
<p>For the Artemis I DRT and WDR, data from of over 300 accelerometers located on Artemis I, ML, and CT was recorded on 
</p>
<p>two independent data acquisition systems (DAQ’s), the SLS Mobile Data Acquisition System (MDAS) and the accelerations 
</p>
<p>on the CT channels were acquired with the CT Data Acquisition System (CTDAS).  Accelerations on Artemis I and the ML 
</p>
<p>were recorded on MDAS.  The accelerometers on Artemis I were a subset of those used during the Integrated Modal Test, 
</p>
<p>specifically accelerometers were removed from the aft end of the Core Stage in the Engine Section area due to scheduling 
</p>
<p>constraints.  In addition, CTDAS recorded 4 CT JEL pressures (one at each truck) and CT Speed.  The anemometer data 
</p>
<p>collected at the top of the ML Tower (i.e., wind speed and wind direction with respect to ML orientation) was recorded with 
</p>
<p>the independent Sensor Data Acquisition System (SDAS).  SDAS also recorded the permanently installed instrumentation 
</p>
<p>that included accelerometers and strain gages whose purpose is to measure responses during prelaunch and liftoff.  This set of 
</p>
<p>accelerometers was not assessed in this OMA analysis because it had been previously found from the OMA analysis of the 
</p>
<p>ISVV-010 rollout that these accelerometers did not have sufficient sensitivity nor low enough sensor noise level.   
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 7.  Artemis I DRT CT speed profiles:  rollout (left) and rollback (right) (EDT time zone) 
</p>
<p>The MDAS, CTDAS, and SDAS had different sampling frequencies and were not time synchronized.  The MDAS and 
</p>
<p>CTDAS sampling frequencies were significantly higher than required to identify the target modes and resulted in very large 
</p>
<p>data files that were challenging to handle and analyze.  Figure 8 shows the test display model (TDM) with the locations and 
</p>
<p>orientation of the ML and CT accelerometers used to animate the OMA identified test mode shapes and the Artemis I DRT 
</p>
<p>FEM.  The Artemis I WDR TDM is the same as the Artemis I DRT TDM except the CT portion has been removed.  It has 
</p>
<p>become increasingly popular to partially back expand test mode shapes to a larger set of grid points or even to the full FEM.  
</p>
<p>Caution should be exercised when doing so because small errors in the test mode shapes, due to ambient noise, sensor noise, 
</p>
<p>and nonlinearities in the test article, combined with differences between the FEM and the test hardware, which produces 
</p>
<p>errors in the back expansion matrix.  These back expanded mode shapes can lead to erroneous local deflections in the back 
</p>
<p>expanded test mode shapes that can mislead their interpretation and FEM correlation efforts.  If the animation of the test 
</p>
<p>mode shapes back expanded so there are only all three translational degrees of freedom (DOF) at each instrument location is 
</p>
<p>insufficient for the test engineer to visually interpret them, then it is recommended the instrumentation set be revised.  The 
</p>
<p>TDM shown in Figure 8 only has grid points located where accelerometers were mounted and consisted of triangular plate 
</p>
<p>elements and trace lines.  Triangular plate elements were used to connect adjacent grid points to provide depth of field to help </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>with visualizing mode shapes.  Note that the shading of the triangular plate elements could be turned off if a purely “trace 
</p>
<p>line” type of TDM is desired.   
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 8.  Artemis I DRT TDM (left) and FEM (right)

</p>
<p>(MLP) in March 2017, Space Shuttle CT unloaded and rollouts with the MLP in February 2005, and the Partial Stack rollout 
</p>
<p>(space Shuttle CT transporting an MLP with two solid Rocket Boosters connected with a thrust beam at their forward 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>the orbiter for installation and servicing of payloads at the Launch Pads 39A and B, as well as servicing access to certain 
</p>
<p>systems on the orbiter.  The RSS can be seen in the far left of Figure 11.

<p>ARTEMIS I DRT AND WDR FEM 
</p>
<p>The Artemis I DRT FEM consisted of 17 different superelement components that needed to be assembled into a single 
</p>
<p>residual run along with physical representations of the ML, CT, MPPU, and various parts of the SLS stack.  The total weight 
</p>
<p>of the Artemis I DRT FEM was 21.5 million pounds.  The approximate weight breakdown was the CT, ML, and all 
</p>
<p>associated hardware weighed 18 million pounds the unfueled SLS stack weighed about 3.5 million pounds primarily due to 
</p>
<p>the two boosters.   
</p>
<p>There are many benefits to having a FEM and the associated Test Analysis Model (TAM) prior to any modal testing effort.  
</p>
<p>For an OMA testing effort however, it’s importance cannot be understated.  It is critical for OMA analysis to help focus 
</p>
<p>modal parameter identification efforts to help ensure that all target modes are identified.  In addition, the TAM mass matrix 
</p>
<p>allows for comparisons of test and FEM mode shapes using a cross-orthogonality criteria and comparisons of test shapes to 
</p>

<p>DATA QUALITY CHECKS AND PREPROCESSING 
</p>
<p>Time-domain and frequency-domain data quality checks have always played an important role in traditional modal testing 
</p>
<p>(i.e., EMA) to help ensure obtaining valid modal parameters.  However, because OMA does not have measured input 
</p>
<p>excitation into the system as EMA does, these data quality checks become more important and increased rigor should be 
</p>
<p>applied when performing them.  Skipping data quality checks can lead to corrupted time histories from which the OMA 
</p>
<p>analysis can produce “reasonable looking” answers that are not valid.  Hence, the temptation of rushing to perform any OMA 
</p>
<p>(or EMA) analysis prior to completing rigorous data quality checks should be avoided [26].   
</p>
<p>The standard time-domain data quality checks should include inspecting time histories for data dropouts, underflow (i.e., 
</p>
<p>digitization error), overflow (i.e., clipping), excessive low frequency signal drift, excessive skewness/asymmetries, 
</p>
<p>excessive/unexpected excursions or transients, oscillation blooms (i.e., ringing), and other unexpected behaviors.  This helps 
</p>
<p>to identify bad/questionable channels and channels that may be able to be “corrected”.  Acceleration time histories that have 
</p>
<p>overall amplitude levels that are out of family with expectedly similar acceleration time histories potentially indicates 
</p>
<p>bad/questionable channels and channels that may be able to be “corrected”.  Scaling issues could be due to incorrect 
</p>
<p>sensitivities entered into the DAQ channel table.  Standard statistics (e.g., mean, max, min, standard deviation, kurtosis, and 
</p>
<p>crest factor) should also be computed and reviewed.  These time-domain data quality checks should be applied to both the 
</p>
<p>raw unfiltered test data as well as the filtered test data that is ultimately used in the OMA analysis.  While this helps to verify 
</p>
<p>the health of the channels, it does not necessarily verify the as installed accelerometers are consistent with the channel table.  
</p>
<p>A useful data quality check that helps to identify channel table errors is to take the acceleration time histories on the ML at 
</p>
<p>the four ML/CT pickup points and apply them to the physical FEM.  Comparison of the lowpass (LP) filtered test simulated 
</p>
<p>and test measured acceleration time histories can quickly identify channel table errors, particularly polarity issues.   
</p>
<p>The standard frequency-domain data quality checks should include computing and comparing Power Spectral Densities 
</p>
<p>(PSD’s), also known as Auto Spectral Densities (ASD’s) of channels that are expected to be in-family and identifying 
</p>
<p>harmonic excitation due to CT Harmonics and rotating machinery operating in both the CT and ML Base, etc.  These 
</p>
<p>standard frequency-domain data quality checks should be applied to both the raw unfiltered time histories as well as the 
</p>
<p>filtered time histories that are used in the OMA analysis in order to identify bad/questionable channels and channels that may 
</p>
<p>be able to be “corrected”.  Again, while this helps to verify the health of the channels, it does not necessarily verify the as 
</p>
<p>installed accelerometers are consistent with the channel table.  PSD’s were also inspected to see if they possessed distinct 
</p>
<p>resonance peaks at the Artemis I DRT and WDR FEM modal frequencies, CT harmonics if the CT was moving, and 60 Hz 
</p>
<p>harmonics due to electronic noise.  These PSD’s were also compared to the accelerometers manufacture’s specified sensor 
</p>
<p>noise floor PSD's to obtain a preliminary estimate of the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) on each channel.  PSD waterfall plots 
</p>
<p>were used in determining stationarity (i.e., time invariant behavior) and nonstationarity (e.g., transients, time dependent 
</p>
<p>behavior, etc.) characteristics of each acceleration time history. 
</p>
<p>Low frequency signal drift, which is an artifact of the signal conditioning, can produce can have detrimental effects upon 
</p>
<p>both the OMA time-domain and frequency-domain techniques.  It can cause the OMA time-domain techniques to try and fit 
</p>
<p>extraneous modes to them and the singular values can have misleading peaks.  Unwanted high frequency content can have 
</p>
<p>similar detrimental effects.  Therefore, acceleration time histories should be bandpass (BP) filtered prior to being input into 
</p>
<p>the OMA analysis to only include the frequency range of interest in order to focus the modal identification process and 
</p>
<p>minimize the computational burden on the OMA software. 
</p>
<p>Filtering used in this analysis consisted of digital 4th order Butterworth filters.  Butterworth filters were chosen because of 
</p>
<p>their monotonic magnitude characteristics.  4th order was chosen to help ensure filter stability.  To quadruple the steepness of 
</p>
<p>the filter rollup and rolloff (i.e., obtain the performance of a higher order filter without the risk of having an unstable filter) </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>and to eliminate phase distortion, the time acceleration histories were passed forwards and backwards through this digital 
</p>
<p>filter twice.  However, keep in mind that while running time histories forwards and backwards through a digital filter 
</p>
<p>multiple times increases the filter’s performance (i.e., steepness of the rollup and rolloff), it comes at the cost of accentuating 
</p>
<p>the filter transients at the beginning and ending of the time histories.  If transients cannot be tolerated, butterflying techniques 
</p>
<p>can be used on the acceleration time histories prior to filtering, which will eliminate transients at the beginning and ending of 
</p>
<p>the bandpass filtered acceleration time histories.  For this study, the transients had an insignificant duration with respect to 
</p>
<p>the total duration so that excluding them did not impact the OMA analysis.   
</p>
<p>The validity (i.e., stability, performance, transient behavior) of any filter being used should be verified by inspection of the 
</p>
<p>filtered time histories should be performed to identify any filtering transients that may occur at the beginning or ending of the 
</p>
<p>filtered time histories.  The left side of Figure 17 compares the unfiltered and BP filtered acceleration time histories, which 
</p>
<p>shows the BP filtered acceleration time history has a filtering transient at its beginning.  This BP filtered acceleration time 
</p>
<p>history also had a filtering transient that occurring at its end.  The filter validity should also be verified by comparing PSD’s 
</p>
<p>of the unfiltered and filtered time histories.  The right figure of Figure 17 compares the PSD’s of an unfiltered and BP filtered 
</p>
<p>acceleration time histories.  Note the PSD computed on the BP filtered acceleration time history excluded the transients at the 
</p>
<p>beginning and end.  This verified the validity of this BP filter.   
</p>
<p>

</p>
<p>coefficients for the low frequency target modes for this test DOF).

<p>TIME SYNCHRONIZATION OF MDAS AND CTDAS DATA 
</p>
<p>Unfortunately, time stamping of data channels does not ensure they are adequately time synchronized.  In adequate time 
</p>
<p>synchronization results in cross spectral densities having erroneously large phase angle distortions, which results in the OMA 
</p>
<p>identified test mode shapes having erroneously high complexity.  With the ultimate goal of obtaining real valued OMA 
</p>
<p>identified mode shapes, this phase angle distortion leads to the real portion of the complex valued OMA identified test mode 
</p>
<p>shapes not well capturing the underlying dynamics.  This will negatively impact any model correlation effort.  Therefore, it is 
</p>
<p>important that data channels be adequately time synchronized.   
</p>
<p>Both the MDAS and CTDAS had IRIG-B timing, however this did not provide sufficiently accurate time synchronization for 
</p>
<p>OMA analysis.  A common trigger signal was collected on both MDAS and CTDAS for post processing time 
</p>
<p>synchronization, however, attempts to use it were not successful.  Fortunately for the Artemis I DRT, there were MDAS 
</p>
<p>triaxial accelerometers on the ML side of each of the four ML/CT pickup points and CTDAS triaxial accelerometers on the 
</p>
<p>CT side.  These pairs of MDAS and CTDAS triaxial accelerometers were spatially close enough together that over the 
</p>
<p>frequency range of interest they had essentially the same acceleration time history (i.e., no relative motion) and could be used 
</p>
<p>for time synchronization.  The MDAS and CTDAS acceleration time histories were first LP filtered to remove frequency 
</p>
<p>content that was significantly above the frequency range of the target modes.  This helped to visually identify common 
</p>
<p>dominant low frequency events in both, which could be used to estimate the time shift between them.  Without LP filtering 
</p>
<p>these pairs of MDAS and CTDAS accelerometers have significant relative motion at higher frequencies, which would not 
</p>
<p>allow them to be time synchronized.  Visually inspecting the pairs of MDAS and CTDAS acceleration time histories showed 
</p>
<p>the MDAS and CTDAS clocks during Artemis I DRT rollout differed by about 1 minute at times and 2 minutes at other 
</p>
<p>times.  It is unknown why the relative timing difference was not constant.  During Artemis I DRT rollback their clocks 
</p>
<p>differed by an approximately consistent 17 sec. Based upon these clock differences, the lag window used to compute the 
</p>
<p>cross-covariances was determined.  Note that the cross-covariance function was used instead of the cross-correlation function 
</p>
<p>in order to eliminate the impact of either the MDAS or CTDAS acceleration time histories having low frequency signal drift, 
</p>
<p>which would result in them having nonzero means over the lag window they were analyzed.  Figure 19 shows the same two 
</p>
<p>events in the MDAS and CTDAS acceleration time histories at CT Corner A ML/CT pickup points, which indicates the 
</p>
<p>MDAS and CTDAS clocks differed by approximately 54 sec. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 19.  Comparison of CT Corner A ML/CT pick up point acceleration time histories: CTDAS (top) and MDAS (bottom)  
</p>
<p>These acceleration time histories, which had already been LP filtered, were upsampled to a higher sampling frequency based 
</p>
<p>upon the desired maximum absolute phase angle error at the highest frequency of interest (e.g., if a maximum absolute phase 
</p>
<p>angle of error of 0.5 deg at 20 Hz was desired then the upsampling frequency would need to be at least 14.4 kHz).  Care 
</p>
<p>needs to be taken to minimize high amplitude high frequency artifacts that the upsampling process introduces.  Figure 20 
</p>
<p>shows two methods of upsampling with the method shown on left introducing significantly higher amplitude high frequency 
</p>
<p>artifacts than the method on the right. </p>
<p></p>

</p>
<p>time histories and the peak in the cross-covariance that occurs around 54 seconds.  The time steps the cross-covarions peak 
</p>
<p>occurred at was determined and used to precisely time shift the MDAS and CTDAS acceleration time histories.  This time 
</p>

</p>

</p>

<p>The time synchronized MDAS and CTDAS acceleration time histories were then down sampled to the desired slower 
</p>
<p>sampling frequency and HP filtered to remove any low frequency signal drift.  Finally, the time synchronized BP filtered 
</p>
<p>MDAS and CTDAS acceleration time histories were overlaid as the final check of their time synchronization.  This final 
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>shedding frequencies, it was adequate to roughly time synchronized them using just the MDAS and SDAS clocks.

</p>
<p>OMA analysis was performed on both the Artemis I DRT Rollout and Rollback time segments, which included time intervals 
</p>
<p>when the CT was stopped, traveling at nearly constant speeds, and the CT speed was varying.  OMA analysis was performed 
</p>
<p>on one WDR time segment where SLS was partially fueled.  The WDR data collected when the SLS was partially fueled is 
</p>
<p>the only modal data collected on Artemis I with the Core Stage in a fueled state (the Artemis I IMT had the Core Stage 
</p>
<p>empty) and thus provides important supplemental information. 
</p>
<p>Investigations into the best set of projection channels for the Artemis I WDR was performed.  The left figure in Figure 22 
</p>
<p>shows 3 Projection Channels automatically selected by the OMA software, which consisted of three horizontal test DOF with 
</p>
<p>one at the top of Artemis I, one on the Right Booster, and one at an upper level of the ML Tower.  Manual selection of 
</p>
<p>Projection Channels was used to determine if the Projection Channels could be improved upon.  The manual selection was 
</p>
<p>based upon visual inspection of the FEM target modes and not wanting Projection Channels of test DOF near the CT Trucks 
</p>
<p>to try and minimize the effects of the CT harmonics.  Also, the ISVV-010 and Partial Stack OMA analysis showed that 
</p>

<p></p>

</p>

<p>As with the OMA analysis of the ISVV-010 rollout, multiple OMA mode identification techniques were required to identify 
</p>
<p>all target modes [11].  OMA analysis started with the time-domain Stochastic Subspace Identification Extended Unweighted 
</p>
<p>Principal Component (SSI-UPCX) technique.  Relaxing the SSI-UPCX parameters was done to and obtain more modes </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>

</p>
<p>damping values, overly complex mode shapes, and poor quality mode shapes.  Next, the Curve Fitting Frequency Domain 
</p>
<p>(CFDD) technique was used to verify the SSI-UPCX identified modes and was used judiciously to identify additional modes 
</p>
<p>corresponding to dominant singular value peaks, which had positive modal damping, that the SSI-UPCX technique had 
</p>
<p>missed.  Then, the Enhanced Frequency Domain Decomposition (EFDD) technique was used to verify the SSI-UPCX and 
</p>
<p>CFDD identified modes and used judiciously to identify additional modes corresponding to dominant singular value peaks, 
</p>
<p>which had positive modal damping that the SSI-UPCX and CFDD techniques had missed.  Finally, the Frequency Domain 
</p>
<p>Decomposition (FDD) technique was used to verify the SSI-UPCX, CFDD, and EFDD identified modes and to identify 
</p>
<p>modes for dominant singular value peaks not already captured, whether they were believed to be due to CT harmonics or not.

<p>A FEM having a symmetric positive definite mass matrix M and a symmetric positive semidefinite stiffness matrix K has real 
</p>
<p>valued mode shapes Φ that are orthogonal to the mass and stiffness matrix (i.e., ΦTMΦ and ΦTKΦ are diagonal matrices).  If 
</p>
<p>these real valued mode shapes are normalized so that ΦTMΦ is the identity matrix, then ΦTKΦ is a diagonal matrix with the 
</p>
<p>square of the modal frequencies in units of rad/sec on its diagonal and these modes are called real normal modes.  If a FEM 
</p>
<p>having a symmetric positive definite mass matrix M, a symmetric positive semidefinite damping matrix C, and a symmetric 
</p>
<p>positive semidefinite stiffness matrix K has complex valued mode shapes Ψ, these complex valued mode shapes are not 
</p>
<p>orthogonal with respect to M, C, or K (e.g., ΨTMΨ ≠ diagonal matrix) [27].  Therefore, the self-orthogonality (ORTHO) and 
</p>
<p>cross-orthogonality (ORTHO) are not valid criteria for comparisons of these complex valued mode shapes.  Note that the self </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Modal Assurance Criteria (MAC) and cross Modal Assurance Criteria (XMAC) remains a valid comparisons of complex 
</p>
<p>valued mode shapes. 
</p>
<p>Real valued test mode shapes that “best” approximate the complex valued OMA mode shapes are required for correlating to 
</p>
<p>the Artemis I DRT and WDR FEM’s, which have real normal modes.  This was achieved by normalizing the complex valued 
</p>
<p>OMA mode shapes by rotating them (i.e., multiplied by ejθ) to “best” lie on the real axis in a least-squares sense.  The left 
</p>
<p>figure in Figure 26 shows the unnormalized complex valued OMA mode shape and the right figure shows the normalized 
</p>
<p>complex valued OMA mode shape.  It can be seen the unnormalized complex valued OMA mode shape was rotated 
</p>
<p>clockwise approximately 55 degrees (i.e., multiplied by ejθ, where θ ≈ − (55deg)(2π rad/360deg) = − 0.96 rad) to “best” lie on 
</p>
<p>the real axis.  Then the real portion of these normalized complex valued OMA mode shapes were taken to obtain real valued 
</p>
<p>OMA mode shapes.  Finally, these real valued OMA mode shapes were then back expanded to have all three translational 
</p>
<p>DOF at each accelerometer location using the Artemis I DRT or WDR TAM’s and animated using the TDM for visual 
</p>
<p>interpretation. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 26.  Complex valued mode shape: unnormalized (left) and normalized (right) 
</p>
<p>For each time interval analyzed, the XMAC and XORTHO were used to compare the real valued OMA mode shapes 
</p>
<p>identified by each OMA technique and normalized as discussed above.  Mode shapes were considered equivalent if their 
</p>
<p>XORTHO value was ≥ 0.9 and their modal frequency difference was less than 0.1 Hz or +/- 5%, which was based upon 
</p>
<p>NASA-STD-5002a criteria [25].  From these XORTHO comparisons, a preliminary final mode set from each time interval 
</p>
<p>analyzed was constructed with a confidence assigned to each OMA mode based upon the number of OMA techniques that 
</p>
<p>identified it.  Figure 27 shows the first 13 OMA modes of a total of 57 OMA modes identified during the Artemis I DRT 
</p>
<p>rollback when the CT was moving at a nearly constant speed (TSS3_RB_2).  Most modes have either low confidence or very 
</p>
<p>low confidence.  Modes suspected of being due to CT harmonics highlighted in red.   
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 27.  Preliminary final OMA mode set of Artemis I DRT rollback when the CT moving at a constant speed (Modes 1 – 
</p>
<p>13 only) (normalized to lowest frequency mode) </p>

<p>Figure 28 shows the XORTHO of FEM target modes and the real valued OMA mode shapes showing there are significant 
</p>
<p>differences between them except for the first three modes.  The mode comparison table shows the cumulative root sum 
</p>
<p>square (CRSS) of each of the real valued OMA mode shapes with respect to all FEM target mode shapes is close to or at 
</p>
<p>100%.  This indicates all real valued OMA mode shapes are within the vector space of the FEM target mode shapes (i.e., the 
</p>
<p>real valued OMA mode shapes can be well approximated by a linear combination of the FEM target mode shapes).  Not 
</p>
<p>shown, the CRSS of each FEM target mode shape with respect to all real valued OMA mode shapes is also close to or at 
</p>
<p>100%, which indicates all FEM target mode shapes are within the vector space of the real valued OMA mode shapes.  This 
</p>
<p>collectively shows that OMA analysis had identified all FEM target mode dynamics. 
</p>

<p>The preliminary final mode set identified during the Artemis I DRT rollback when the CT was moving at a nearly constant 
</p>

</p>
<p>(TSS3_RB_1), and those identified when the CT was moving at a different nearly constant speed (TSS5_RB_1).  The 
</p>
<p>ORTHO of the first 27 modes of this combined OMA preliminary final mode sets is shown in Figure 29.  Modes that were 
</p>
<p>within 0.1 Hz or 5% modal frequency and had ORTHO off-diagonal values ≥ 0.90 were judged to be the same mode.  The 
</p>
<p>similarity between these modes only holds for a few of the lowest frequency OMA modes, even though the OMA mode 
</p>
<p>shapes look very similar.  This indicates Artemis I DRT configuration has nonlinear characteristics since the OMA modes are 
</p>
<p>dependent upon the CT speed.

<p>CONCLUSION 
</p>
<p>OMA techniques will not replace traditional modal testing (i.e., EMA), but can provide important supplemental information 
</p>
<p>and insights into the structural dynamics of hardware in its operational environment.  CT harmonics, which lie within the 
</p>
<p>frequency range of the fundamental target modes, manifest themselves as narrowband Gaussian random processes continue 
</p>
<p>to be a significant challenge when applying OMA analysis to rollout data because they are difficult to analytically remove.  
</p>
<p>Artemis I DRT and WDR configurations displayed nonlinear characteristics, which were dependent upon the CT and ground 
</p>
<p>wind speeds.  OMA techniques were successfully applied to the Artemis I DRT and WDR data for identifying preliminary 
</p>
<p>final sets of modes that captured all Artemis I DRT and WDR FEM target mode dynamics, respectively.  By applying OMA 
</p>
<p>techniques to time intervals that have the CT moving at different, but nearly constant speeds, which shifts the CT harmonics, 
</p>
<p>it is possible to compare the OMA modes from these different sets to identify true structural modes.  However, the challenge 
</p>
<p>is that due to the nonlinearity of Artemis I DRT configuration, the true structural modes from each of these time intervals can 
</p>
<p>be significantly different, with the differences increasing with increasing frequency.  Further work is needed to look at 
</p>
<p>comparing OMA modes of selected components of the Artemis I DRT configuration (e.g., excluding umbilicals) to determine 
</p>
<p>what is causing the differences in the OMA mode shapes and obtain a final complete mode set of OMA modes.  
</p>
<p>OMA analysis of the Artemis I DRT and WDR has also provided invaluable insights into what to expect during a possible 
</p>
<p>Artemis IV DRT.  Artemis IV will use the larger and more powerful SLS Block 1B launch vehicle and be integrated on and 
</p>
<p>launch from the larger ML-2.  This will lead to the Artemis IV DRT target modes having lower frequencies and a higher 
</p>
<p>modal density than that of Artemis I DRT.  Hence OMA analysis of the Artemis IV DRT will be more challenging than that 
</p>
<p>of Artemis I DRT and require careful planning and execution to help ensure success. 
</p>
