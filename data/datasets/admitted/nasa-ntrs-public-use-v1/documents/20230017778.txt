<p>Validation of Gas-Liquid Sharp Interface Model in Loci-Stream
for Propellant Tank Self-pressurization under Normal Gravity
</p>
<p>Chintan S. Patel ∗
</p>
<p>Qualis Corp (Jacobs Space Exploration Group), NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, AL
</p>
<p>Hong. Q. Yang †
</p>
<p>CFD Research Corporation (Jacobs Space Exploration Group), NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, AL
</p>
<p>Brandon R. Williams ‡
</p>
<p>NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, AL
</p>
<p>Deep space missions require advances in cryogenic fluid management (CFM) for long term
storage of propellants. One of the important phenomena is self-pressurization due to heat
leakages into the tank. Managing self-pressurization is one of the key technologies for deep
space exploration and long-term space missions. The complex interactions involving natural
convection, thermal gradients, turbulence, and phase change near the gas-liquid interface cannot
be modeled using reduced order or nodal analysis models, and 3-D CFD analyses are necessary
to fully characterize the dynamics. CFD analyses pose their own difficulties. The requisite CFD
tool to tackle this problem need to be modular with the ability to incorporate various physics
models, efficient, and computationally scalable for simulating flight size tanks. In this paper, we
discuss modeling approach for self-pressurization in situations where the liquid interface is static
and validate NASA MSFC’s Loci-Stream CFD solver for predicting self-pressurization in a test
tank in normal gravity. This tank was designed to capture quantifiable and accurate data for
understanding various two-phase fluid phenomena and to validate modeling tools. Specifically,
we demonstrate the capability of the Loci-Stream solver with a two-phase sharp-interface
treatment to predict self-pressurization of a tank with an unperturbed gas-liquid interface.
This validation highlights the reliability of our modeling approach and our solver to serve as a
design and analysis tool for NASA’s CFM application needs.
</p>

<p>II. Introduction
</p>
<p>Cryogenic propellants promise higher specific impulses than storable hypergolic fuels, but storage for long-duration
missions must be demonstrated. NASA’s near-future long-term space missions necessitate advancements in cryogenic
</p>
<p>fluid management (CFM), which includes safe, low cost, and reliable long-term propellant storage. Consequently,
NASA STMD (Space Technology Mission Directorate) has established the CFM Portfolio Project to improve CFM
technologies for upcoming missions. Long-term in-space storage of cryogenic propellants including upper stages and
potentially propellant depots is very important to meet the technological challenges of NASA’s future exploration
missions. One of the challenges in cryogenic fluid management is that cryogenic propellant tanks in space are exposed to
incident solar radiation, which heats the liquid in the tank over time. The increase in temperature results in an increase
in pressure as the liquid cryogen vaporizes, i.e., self-pressurization.
</p>
<p>During the time interval when the tank is ’locked up’, heat transfer through the tank walls results in an energy
increase in both the liquid and the ullage space vapor. This energy increase is not uniformly distributed in either the
liquid or vapor, but rather higher temperatures occur in the topmost layers of both the liquid and vapor regions due to
natural convection in both 1-g and low-g conditions. This phenomenon is described as thermal stratification and has
been reported by numerous observers [1–4] in early space programs. Associated with this temperature stratification
is a rise in tank pressure due to a combination of heat transferred to the ullage vapor plus possible vaporization of a
portion of the liquid. A means for predicting the amount of pressure rise as it varies with time and tank wall heat flux is
needed to properly plan for propellant fluid management, to determine tank structural stresses, to determine tank venting
requirements, and possibly to foresee the need for additional pressurant gas. The extent of liquid temperature rise,
separate from its effect on the tank pressure, is also needed to determine the impact on pump performance. Excessive
temperatures can result in cavitation due to a reduction in Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH), thus either damaging the
pump or adversely affecting the pump performance.
</p>
<p>Numerous recent studies have focused on predicting the pressurization process in cryogenic propellant tanks [5–16]
and have produced analytical, experimental, and numerical results. These studies play a critical role in the design of
cryogenic storage systems since tank pressure must be controlled for long-duration space missions.
</p>
<p>Due to the existence of thermal stratification within propellant, simple thermodynamic analysis cannot accurately
predict the rate of pressure rise within the system. High-fidelity computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations play
an essential role in predicting the actual thermodynamics of pressurization. The objective of this study is to build upon
the on-going development and validation of our finite-volume-based CFD model to predict the thermal stratification and
self-pressurization of any propellant tank. The final goal is the development of modeling capabilities required for the
design of CFM systems to accomplish tasks such as liquid acquisition, thermal control of the cryogenic tank, and fluid
transfer.
</p>
<p>Unlike a typical CFD code, the computational tool for the CFM application requires multi-phase and multi-physics
capability as tank pressurization involves both liquid and gas phases and their interaction. At the same time, numerical
modeling tools need to have sufficient fidelity to answer critical design and operational issues and must be validated
by comparing them with test data. Several well-established methods exist for dealing with interfaces in fluid flow.
</p>
<p>2</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>These methods include the front tracking [17], the level set method [18, 19], and the volume of fluid (VOF) methods
[20, 21]. For CFM applications, ensuring mass conservation and energy conservation for both phases are critical. In
these situations, VOF methods are found to be superior. Although, when the gas-liquid interface is unperturbed, VOF
methods can be computationally expensive without adding any advantage. Therefore, here we build upon the previously
developed lumped-gas-liquid-CFD model [16] in Loci-Stream and extended it to model both fluid phases with 3-D CFD
for added flexibility in ullage modeling to accommodate more physics such as tank venting, autogenous pressurization,
etc. While this approach is restricted to situations where the gas-liquid interface is static and known a priori, the primary
advantage is even better conservation than VOF methods, improved stability, and reduction in simulation time by a
factor of up to 100 vs the VOF methods.
</p>
<p>Loci-Stream [22][23] is a pressure-based, all-speed CFD code for generalized meshes in the Loci framework that has
been applied and validated over a wide range of problems. Loci-Stream uses SIMPLE, SIMPLEC, and PISO algorithms
for pressure-momentum coupling and provides various turbulence models which can be executed in Reynolds-Averaged
Navier-Stokes (RANS) or Large Eddy Simulation (LES) mode. Loci-Stream is built upon a flexible and scalable
Loci [24] framework, which confers it significant parallel scalability and has been used on production problems up to
thousands of CPU cores [22–26].
</p>
<p>Previously, self-pressurization validation using the NASA K-site experiment was performed with a lumped-gas
liquid CFD approach and with the VOF approach. Here, we expand the validation by demonstrating the applicability of
our model to different tank scale and different heating mechanism to validate Loci-Stream for self-pressurization of
a desktop size zero boil-off tank (ZBOT) where the tank is heated with localized heat source with high heat flux (vs
evenly distributed heat load encountered in K-site self-pressurization [16]). Such localized heating could arise in tanks
due to heat flow through struts or contact with other structural elements.
</p>
<p>In the following sections, the experimental set-up, governing equations, newly implemented Loci-Stream two phase
sharp interface CFD model for gas-liquid interface, mass transfer model, and finally a comparison of the simulation
results and experimental data are presented.
</p>
<p>III. Experiment Setup and Test Description
The Zero-Boil-Off Tank (ZBOT) experiments are designed to understand two-phase fluid physics phenomena that
</p>
<p>control tank pressurization and pressure control [27]. ZBOT-1 is the first of the three sets of the ZBOT experiment
series, conducted both on ground in normal gravity and aboard the International Space Station (ISS) 1. In this study, we
focus on the self-pressurization test conducted in normal gravity using a strip heater.
</p>

<p>A. Test Article
The cylindrical double-domed tank (Figure 2) is cast from acrylic and polished to optical quality. The tank cylinder
</p>
<p>has radius of 5.08 cm and the height of 20.32 cm. The strip heater is mounted on the outer wall. The tank bottom is

(Figure 3) were placed inside the tank (on vertical rakes) and on the tank internal and external walls.
</p>
<p>A non-polar refrigerant Perfluoro-n-Pentane (𝐶5𝐹12, PnP) is used as a simulant because it boils near room temperature
at atmospheric pressure and was deemed safe for usage on ISS. The PnP was degassed to be free of any non-condensable
gas to avoid non-condensable effects.
</p>
<p>B. Experimental Procedure
The tank was filled to 90% of its volume with PnP. A uniform temperature initial condition was achieved through
</p>
<p>mixing and hold before the test began. A 1.0 W constant heat flux was applied for 3.5 hours. This thermal energy warms
the liquid PnP in contact with the tank wall which buoys up and forms natural convection currents. Warm liquid PnP at
the gas-liquid interface evaporates to pressurize the tank. Observations of pressure and temperature evolution in the
tank are discussed along with the CFD validation results.
</p>

<p>D. Two Phase Sharp Interface Model
While VOF is a high-fidelity model for capturing the evolution of a gas-liquid interface, the accuracy and generality
</p>
<p>come with a higher computational cost compared with other methods. For the long duration self-pressurization scenarios
described above, that computational cost may become a significant disadvantage. Because the interface is static, other
methods where the gas and liquid domains are simulated in a segregated but coupled fashion are also possible.
</p>
<p>A lumped gas sharp interface model was developed into Loci-Stream previously to overcome timestep size and
stability limitations of VOF for low momentum and quiescent-interface flows [16]. This method was well-suited for
self-pressurization problem in CFM due to largely quiescent ullage and the dynamic primarily being driven by liquid
dynamics. While that method works very well for the problem, the obvious disadvantage is in foregoing understanding
of ullage dynamics when they are important. Therefore, we generalize the method here to two phase sharp interface
model. While self-pressurization generally does not require resolution of ullage dynamics, this method is essential for
modeling many CFM problem, for example, autogenous pressurization. A brief description of the model follows.
</p>

<p></p>
</div>

equal across the interface (Equation 14). Interface normal velocity is assumed to be zero - even when phase change

<p>Phase change is modeled using a mass source term. Two models are implemented to model heat transfer and mass

<p>1. Balance Method
In this method, the ullage gas in contact with the sharp interface is assumed to be at equilibrium condition (i.e.,
</p>
<p>saturated at all times). Then the temperature of the gas side of the interface can be calculated using saturation condition
at current ullage pressure. Clausius-Clapeyron and Antoine equations are the most often used relationships between
saturation temperature and pressure. Both methods are implemented. Antoine equation (Equation 12) which superior in
closely approximating saturation curve is chosen for our simulations.
</p>
<p>The "balance" refers to thermal balance at the interface. It is assumed that the interface is always in thermal
equilibrium, i.e., the heat flux to the interface from the liquid is equal to heat flux to the interface from gas plus the flux
from the latent heat of evaporation (Equation 16). This equilibrium yields phase change mass transfer rate, ¤𝑚.
</p>

<p>2. Kinetics - Balance Iterative Method
This method combines the kinetic relationship for phase change mass transfer rate provided by the HKS (Equation
</p>
<p>10) and thermal balance at the interface (Equation 16). We solve these equations iteratively using secant method to
obtain the interface temperature, 𝑇𝑖 . Here, the interface is not assumed to be saturated.
</p>
<p>The sharp interface approach provides all the benefits of the lumped gas liquid CFD approach [16]. First, the use of
computationally expensive VOF advection and geometric reconstruction of the VOF method is circumvented. Second,
the stringent Courant number stability constraint for explicit timestepping in the VOF method is also avoided. Since
Loci-Stream is an implicit solver in time, an order of magnitude or even higher timestep can be used with sharp interface
method. Third, the problem of small computational instabilities at the gas-liquid interface inherently present in the VOF
method on any practical non-orthogonal mesh is also resolved, allowing careful resolution of thermal gradients and
velocity profiles with mesh refinement that is impractical for the VOF method.
</p>
<p>E. Turbulence Modeling for Two Phase Interface
Turbulence modeling at the two-phase interface with two-equation RANS models is tricky. Menter (1994), Wilcox
</p>
<p>(1998) and others have shown that eddy viscosity based turbulence models generate non-physically high turbulence near
the interface compared to the experimental observations [30]. Modifications must be made to two equation model in
this region. The most common approach is promulgated by Egorov [31], in which 𝜔-equation is modified to a more
wall-like treatment to damp the turbulence. This approach has shown to be effective in wide-ranging applications [32].
The intuition for the method is that when high density ratio fluids are interacting, the lower density fluid essentially
"sees" the higher density fluid as a wall. Yet the method is applied to both phases symmetrically and an asymmetric
approach where the heavier fluid sees the lighter fluid as a slip-wall for purpose of turbulence modeling may be more
appropriate [30]. We note that a high value of the damping factor in Egorov’s method is recommended but the clear
consensus for these values does not exist, and the choice is mesh dependent [30].
</p>
<p>7</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Here, we forgo the complexity and some disadvantages of the Egorov method and retain the insight and advantages
with a simpler model. We implement two turbulence treatments at the interface that bound the expected behaviors. In the
first method, the interface is treated as wall-like for turbulence boundary conditions. In the second method, the interface
is treated as slip-wall-like for turbulence boundary conditions where boundary turbulence is simply extrapolated from
the nearest non-boundary cell. For the ullage gas, we always employ the wall-like turbulence treatment. For the much
heavier liquid phase, either one of the techniques may be appropriate depending on the physics. We end this section by
noting that the symmetric wall-like turbulence treatment is akin to using Egorov’s method with a very high damping
factor.
</p>
<p>V. Modeling
</p>
<p>A. Set-up
The most salient features of the experiment are to be captured with CFD. Modeling is rendered as simple as possible
</p>
<p>while ensuring retention of all important physics. This approach saves significant computational time and enables
exploration of validation sensitivities to confidently apply the solver and our modeling approach to a wide scale/range
of CFM problems. Tank internals are insignificant to the problem and are removed. The mixing nozzle at the tank
bottom is the largest structure and was suspected of inducing additional turbulence and was initially modeled but the
interfacial physics and the tank pressurization were unaffected by the presence of the nozzle and the nozzle was not
included in further investigations. The vacuum jacket thermally isolates the tank, and the radiation between the tank and
the vacuum jacket is considered low enough to be inconsequential to the problem and not modeled.
</p>
<p>An axisymmetric flow assumption is made as no important circumferential flow- and thermal- dynamics is expected.
The computational domain contains 1◦ sectors of the tank fluid and tank walls.
</p>
<p>B. Mesh
Unstructured hexahedral meshes are used for this validation for their computational efficiency. Four mesh resolutions
</p>
<p>were considered (Figure 4). The coarsest mesh (Figure 4e) has 4 mm nominal cell edges on the axisymmetric plane
which is equivalent to 0.3 million cells for a full 3-D model. Successive refinement yields equivalent cell counts of 1.0
million (Figure 4c), 4.1 million (Figure 4b), and 16.0 million (Figure 4a). The boundary layer mesh in Figure 4d has the
equivalent of 1.9 million cells. It is possible to simulate the meshes with very high cell count due to the high timestep
afforded by the sharp interface method. Later, it is shown that even the coarsest mesh can capture sufficient dynamics of
the problem.
</p>
<p>C. Initial and Boundary Conditions
A uniform initial temperature initial condition was set based on the temperature probe data. The tank pressure was
</p>
<p>set to the corresponding saturation condition for PnP at 307.15𝐾 .
Adiabatic wall conditions were prescribed at the external tank wall. A 1𝑊 heat flow was applied to strip heater on
</p>
<p>the tank external wall, which translates to a rather high heat flux of 241.857 W/m2. Symmetry boundary conditions
were applied on the sector planes.
</p>
<p>D. Fluid Properties
Fluid and material properties were evaluated from the current National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)
</p>
<p>databases [29]. Liquid PnP is assumed to be a Boussinesq fluid to model buoyancy, while the ullage is modeled as
a compressible ideal gas. For the vapor phase, constant laminar conductivity, constant viscosity, and temperature
dependent specific heat were supplied. The specific heat was defined with a single piecewise fourth order polynomial.
</p>
<p>The liquid (Table 1), gas (Table 2), acrylic (Table 3) and steel (Table 4) properties are listed in the tables below.

<p>F. Simulation Details
Time integration is computed implicitly with the first-order backward Euler method. A first order upwind scheme is
</p>
<p>used for spatial discretization. Although second order temporal and spatial discretization schemes are available, they
were found to have little impact on the solution. Timestep size and grid resolution sensitivity is presented in forthcoming
sections.
</p>
<p>The Antoine equation (Equation 12) is used for the calculation of the saturation condition with coefficient
𝑎 = 3.995149, 𝑏 = 1000.001236, 𝑐 = −51.789504. The kinetics - balance iterative method was used for phase change,
which provides more modeling flexibility than the balance method because it does not assume equilibrium condition
at the gas-liquid interface. The accommodation factor coefficient in the phase change model is set to 1.0 × 10−3.
Pressurization sensitivity to this parameter is also reported in the ensuing sections.
</p>
<p>The Loci-Stream solid heat module is used for conjugate heat transfer analysis. A tight fit between the acrylic and
steel was assumed and small thermal contact resistance of 1𝑒 − 16 was supplied for their contact surface.

<p>VI. Results
A well-reasoned modeling approach must come together with capable solver and low conservation errors to produce
</p>
<p>a robust model that does not require any tuning parameters for validation. Such a model can be then extended to similar
classes of problems with confidence. Using the baseline mesh (Figure 4c) and the solution parameters described above,
Loci-Stream was able to predict tank pressurization with remarkable accuracy (Figure 5). Not only the final pressure but
the pressurization rate is also captured quite satisfactorily (Figure 6)
</p>
<p>Fig. 5 Change in Initial Pressure Compared with the Experiment
</p>
<p>The pressurization is transient over the entire experiment (Figure 5), i.e., constant pressurization rate is not achieved
by the end of the experiment. The initial transient is a result of the still-developing temperature field in the interfacial
region. Once the interfacial heat balance between the ullage and the liquid stabilizes the pressurization becomes almost
linear. With some difficulty, this linear rate can be estimated using reduced order or lumped parameter solvers. But a
careful resolution of convection currents and interfacial thermal dynamics with robust mass and energy conservation is
necessary to predict pressurization in experiments like this where convective currents are still developing.
</p>
<p>Figure 7a shows the temperature distribution at 120 minutes. Two phenomena are important to note. First, there is
no noticeable temperature gradient at the gas-liquid interface. Second, there is no noticeable temperature gradient in
the upper half of the liquid, but below the strip heater a linear temperature distribution exists. Unlike the previously
validated K-site self-pressurization where heat flux through the tank wall is modeled uniformly [16], in the ZBOT
experiment the heat is only applied to a small section of the liquid. Due to lack of direct heat flux to the ullage there
is no direct pressurization of the tank through heating of the gas. Such pressurization would drive condensation and
associated latent heat transfer at the interface. Here, heat transfer at the interface is primarily due to conduction, and
convection and latent heat exchange due to phase change play minor role. As demonstrated in the upcoming Mesh
Resolution Sensitivity section, this lack of latent heat driven thermal gradient makes it possible to use a coarse mesh at
the interface to successfully predict pressurization.
</p>
<p>As shown in the Figure 7b, the strip heater induces a buoyancy-driven natural convection current in the upper half of
the liquid. This primary current also induces a counter rotating weak current in the bottom half. These two currents
remain stable and do not mix convectively - maintaining thermal stratification. Thus, the heat transfer to the tank bottom
is primarily due to conduction and exhibits a linear temperature variation. A solver must resolve these distinct currents
to predict correct distribution of thermal energy and correct pressurization.
</p>

experiment across the tank walls and tank fluids. This observation reinforces both accurate flow-field resolution and

<p>A. Mesh Resolution Sensitivity
Mesh independence was ensured by simulating the five mesh resolutions shown in Figure 4. Each mesh is able to

<p>B. Flow Regime and Turbulence Model Sensitivity
First, the SST model was evaluated with a wall integration approach (i.e., "gridding down to the wall" with 𝑦+ &lt;= 1).
</p>
<p>We found that the SST model failed to predict the necessary viscosity ratios to produce enough mixing in the tank
liquid and consequently the convective current traveling up to the interface stays more stratified and results in higher
interfacial temperature and consequently higher pressurization than the experiment. In fact, the mixing was so low that
the pressurization trend is nearly identical to the laminar solution. Similar to SST (Figure 12b), the SST-IDDES (Figure
12b) approach also under-predicts the flow mixing and yield a pressurization trend that is similar to the laminar solution.
However, the 𝑘 − 𝜖 model using wall functions was able to capture the mixing dynamics effectively. The performance
of the 𝑘 − 𝜖 model with a wall integration approach could not be assessed at the time because current 𝑘 − 𝜖 model
implementation does not support it. The support is being added currently and will be assessed in near future.
</p>

<p>VII. Conclusion
Our modeling approach and the Loci-Stream sharp interface CFD model has been validated against the ZBOT-1
</p>
<p>self-pressurization ground experiment. Excellent comparison with the experimental pressure and temperature data
without any tuning parameters demonstrate that essential flow and thermal dynamics are understood and modeled,
while extraneous details were removed. This practice not only saves computational resources but also hones engineer’s
ability to distill important dynamics for problem at hand and is essential for modeling more complex system. The newly
implemented two phase sharp interface module allows using two orders of magnitude higher timestep than VOF would
allow and speeds up computation time 100x without any loss in predictive capacity.
</p>
<p>The validation enhances confidence in our solver’s ability to tackle successively more complex CFM problems. In the
future, we anticipate applying the Loci-Stream model to the ZBOT ISS experiment for validation of self-pressurization
in microgravity.
