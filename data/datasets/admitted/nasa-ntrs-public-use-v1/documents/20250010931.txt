Validation of Computational Fluid Dynamics Model of
Self-pressurization in a Flight-scale Liquid Hydrogen Tank



Chintan S. Patel ∗



Qualis LLC (Amentum Space Exploration Division), NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, AL



A computational fluid dynamics (CFD) methodology, developed and demonstrated to
satisfactorily model multiple cryogenic fluid management (CFM) operations, is applied to predict
self-pressurization of a flight-scale liquid hydrogen tank in four ground tests. Axisymmetric
two-phase CFD analyses are performed using the Loci-Stream-VOF CFD solver with the frozen
gas-liquid interface method. Conjugate heat transfer modeling accounts for thermal transport
between the tank walls and propellant fluids. Turbulent natural convection flow is modeled with
three turbulence closures of differing fidelity — Menter’s shear stress transport (SST), improved
delayed detached eddy simulation (IDDES), and wall-adapted local-eddy-viscosity (WALE) —
which show similar predictive capabilities. The asymmetric interface turbulence transport model
ensures accurate phase change and thermal gradient predictions at the two-phase interface.
Phase change is modeled with gas-kinetic-theory-based Hertz-Knudsen-Schrage formulation.
The 50% fill-level test is validated for the CFM Modeling Portfolio with relative pressure-change
error of -2.2% and satisfactory temperature predictions. Predictions are independent of
the spatial and temporal resolution, and phase change model’s accommodation factor. The
predictions for 25% and 90% fill-level tests were unsatisfactory, prompting a CFD sensitivity
analysis of pressurization rate on spatial distributions of heat leak rates reveals that the tank
wall redistributes most heat leakage to the liquid. However, the heat leak rate through the tank
wall to the vapor phase governs the pressurization rate. The sensitivity analysis also produced
spatial distributions of heat leak that accurately predict tank pressure and temperature for
25% and 90% fill-level tests; however, the findings warrant further investigations on spatial
distribution. This study provides insights into self-pressurization mechanisms and along with
the sensitivity analysis technique provide a way to improve self-pressurization predictions for
NASA’s spaceflight missions.

II. Introduction



Cryogenic fluid management (CFM) is a critical enabling technology for the National Aeronautics and Space
Administration’s (NASA) Moon and Mars human-landing missions. CFM addresses the challenges of safe, low-cost,



and reliable in-space and on-surface long-term propellant storage; pressure control and propellant positioning within
tanks for safe engine operations; and in-space and on-surface propellant transfer.



NASA’s Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) has established the CFM Portfolio Project (CFMPP) to
advance CFM technologies to enable future spaceflight missions. In addition to advanced hardware development and
demonstration, there is a need for reliable predictive modeling of CFM systems and their operations to support rapid,
reliable, and low-cost spacecraft design and operation. The CFM Modeling Portfolio within the CFMPP improves the
modeling capabilities.



CFD modeling is indispensable for high-fidelity predictions and for providing insight into flow phenomena that are
difficult or impossible to measure or test. Under the CFM Modeling Portfolio, the Propulsion Fluid Dynamics Branch
(ER42) within the Engineering Directorate at NASA Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC) is assessing, improving,
developing and validating the Loci-Stream-VOF CFD solver to predict CFM operations in support of current and future
flight projects such as the Human Landing System and Commercial Lunar Payload Services.



One of the key CFM challenges is self-pressurization of the cryogenic tanks. The cryogenic propellant tanks in
space are exposed to incident solar radiation and heat leakages due to engine operations. These heat leakages pressurize
the tank over time by providing thermal energy to evaporate the liquid propellant and to warm the ullage gas. A reliable
method to predict tank pressurization in time accurate manner is needed to determine structural stresses, determine
venting requirements, plan for propellant management operations, predict a need for pressurant requirements, and plan
mission’s propellant requirements. The extent of liquid temperature rise, separate from its effect on the tank pressure,
is also needed to determine the impact on pump performance. Excessive temperatures can result in cavitation due to
a reduction in Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH), thus either damaging the pump or adversely affecting the pump
performance.



Long simulation durations, the fine mesh resolution required to capture the thin thermal boundary layer and interface
thermal gradients, two-phase turbulence modeling, and sensitivity to small heat leaks make self-pressurization of a
cryogenic flight-scale tank a deceptively challenging computational problem.



Several studies [1–13] have been carried out in the past for this purpose including experimental, analytical, and
numerical studies of various fidelity. Due to the existence of a sharp thermal gradient near the tank walls and gas-liquid
interface, CFD analyses are the most accurate in their predictions of both time-accurate tank pressure and temperatures
due to the ability to model the complex flow dynamics that is typical to this problem.

NASA STMD’s CFM Modeling Portfolio defines the highest validation rating (4/4) when validation metric agreement
is within 10% and a 3/4 rating when the agreement is within 10–20%. The validation claims in this study refer to these
criteria.



The present work applies a CFD modeling methodology — previously demonstrated to successfully predict
self-pressurization [12, 13], autogenous pressurization [14], pressure control using an axial jet [14] and spray bar
[15], and non-condensable pressurization (planned publication) — to predict self-pressurization of a flight-scale liquid
hydrogen tank. The present goal is to develop modeling capabilities and insights to enable analysis and design of CFM
systems with improved reliability.

A. Testing Facilities
Flight-scale ground tests of self-pressurization were conducted at NASA MSFC’s test stand 300 during 1996-1998



with the multipurpose hydrogen test bed (MHTB) tank (Figure 1a). The 5083 aluminum tank was a double domed
cylindrical tank of diameter 3.05𝑚 with 3.05𝑚 total height including two 2:1 oblate hemispheres. The test tank with
cryogenic insulation and supporting equipment was enclosed in a cylindrical environmental shroud (Figure 1b). The
tank was equipped with a thermal venting system (TVS) and a recirculation pump near the bottom of the tank and a
spray bar/heat exchanger along the major axis of the tank. Two vertical rakes in the tank housed 24 temperature probes
for characterizing the fluid temperatures. Entire assembly was housed in a vacuum chamber with liquid nitrogen cold
walls. [16]

B. Experimental Procedure
The tank was filled with liquid hydrogen to a desired fill level and then vented to within a set pressure range for



several hours to cool the tank structure. The ambient heat leaks to the tank were calculated from boil-off measurements
using a thermodynamic model. Boil-off testing also set-up consistent initial condition for tank self-pressurization
and TVS tests. Two heat leak conditions were tested. For low heat leak set-up, the walls of the vacuum chamber
containing the tank were cooled with liquid nitrogen to minimize the heat leakage. The high heat leak set-up consisted
of additional venting and recirculation hardware at the bottom of the tank which introduced more conductive heat
leakage. Additionally, the liquid nitrogen was not circulated through the vacuum chamber walls. [16]



After a desired conditioning of the hardware was achieved, the tank was sealed, and the self-pressurization test
began. When the set upper pressure limit of 137.9𝑘𝑃𝑎 was reached, the self-pressurization test concluded. Then the

IV. Computational Model
The governing equations and models described below are implemented in Loci-Stream CFD solver — a pressure-



based, all-speed CFD solver for generalized meshes in the rule-based programming framework Loci [17, 18]. Loci [19]
is a rule-based declarative framework that automatically assembles the numerical solution components of an arbitrary
set of partial differential equations and enables solution on large parallel computing systems for efficient parallelization
across thousands of CPU processes. This technique enhances the flexibility of simulation tools, reducing the complexity
of CFD software that includes various boundary conditions, complex geometries, and different physical models.

1. Frozen Two-phase Interface Model
The frozen or stationary gas-liquid interface model, also referred to as sharp interface, takes advantage of a



stationary/quasi-stationary interface. The frozen-interface method enforces a sharp, mesh-defined phase boundary,
eliminating volume of fluid (VOF) advection and geometric reconstruction. An order of magnitude larger timestep is
afforded by time-implicit frozen-interface method compared to typical time-explicit VOF methods with strict Courant
number based stability criteria. The spurious/parasitic interfacial velocities commonly observed in VOF advection are
also avoided, allowing careful resolution of thermal gradients in interfacial region without the stability and affordability
problems faces by VOF transport.



Velocity is continuous at the interface/phase boundary. Tangential velocities on the gas and liquid sides of the
interface are equal(Equation 5)and so is tangential shear stress (Equation 6). Interface normal velocity is assumed to
be zero (Equation 7) — even when phase change occurs because the phase change mass transfer and corresponding
velocities are very small.

3. Turbulence Modeling
Menter’s 2003 shear stress transport (SST) [22] is used for Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) turbulence



closure. An improved delayed detached eddy simulation (IDDES) approach [23] is also utilized for turbulence modeling.
IDDES is a hybrid RANS/LES (Large Eddy Simulation) approach: near-wall regions and unresolved scales — high-shear
or high-vorticity regions where mesh refinement for LES is impractical — are modeled with RANS equations and
resolved scales are modeled with sub-grid scale (SGS) modeling. An implicit LES approach is employed where
grid-induced dissipation provides the sub-grid scales. A blending/shielding function 𝑓𝑑 distinguishes the resolved (LES)
and unresolved (RANS) regions: RANS mode is indicated by 𝑓𝑑 = 1 and SGS mode is indicated by 𝑓𝑑 = 0.



A wall modeled LES (WMLES) approach using wall-adapting local eddy-viscosity (WALE) model [24] is also
investigated and compared with the IDDES and SST predictions.



4. Interface Turbulence Transport
Menter (1994), Wilcox (1998) and others have shown that eddy-viscosity based turbulence models predict non-



physically high turbulence near the interface compared to the experimental observations [25]. A review of the two-phase
literature [14] shows that, near the gas-liquid interface, turbulence on the gas side resembles a wall viscous boundary
layer, whereas the liquid side has either slightly enhanced or unchanged turbulence kinetic energy. [26–30] RANS,
hybrid RANS, and WMLES models must therefore be modified near the interface to capture these altered turbulence
characteristics.



A common approach proposed by Egorov [31] modifies the 𝜔-equation with a source term that adds a wall-like
treatment to reduce turbulence kinetic energy at the interface. This approach has shown to be effective in wide-ranging



5











applications [32]. The physical rationale is that, for two-phase flows with high density ratios, the lower-density fluid
effectively "sees" the higher-density fluid as a wall. However, Egorov’s method is typically applied symmetrically to
both phases. An asymmetric treatment in which the near wall turbulence characteristics on the heavier fluid side are free
of boundary layer effect (i.e., slip-wall-like turbulence) is more appropriate [25].



These observations are encapsulated in our simple but highly effective interface turbulence model for frozen interface
method [14, 33], in which the gas side of the interface is treated as wall-like while the liquid side is unaffected and
has no boundary influence on the turbulence characteristics. This asymmetric turbulence-damping technique has been
instrumental in accurate predictions in multiple tests [14, 15], including the present work and a separate study planned
for future publication.



B. Geometry
MHTB tank’s internal structure is not expected appreciably affect the self-pressurization and was therefore removed



from the computational domain. An axisymmetric sector of 1-degree is modeled because no significant circumferential
flow features are expected. All the circumferential velocities are zeroed out in the governing equations.



A uniform tank thickness of 0.5 in was modeled for conjugate heat transfer modeling. Insulation layers are not
modeled. The heat leak rates obtained from boil-off calculations are applied to the external tank wall.



C. Mesh
Three hexahedral unstructured mesh refinements are created to ensure mesh insensitive predictions. Nominal cell



edge length, outside the wall boundary layer and interface refinement regions, varied from 4 cm to 1 cm. All meshes
have refinement near the gas-liquid interface to resolve velocity and thermal gradients arising due to altered turbulent
transport and phase change. A representative set of meshes are shown in Figures 2 and 3. Based on previous validations
[14, 33], interface normal first-cell height of 1 × 10−4 m was enforced for all meshes. Baseline mesh has 4 cm nominal
mesh size and wall-normal height of 1 × 10−4 m. Baseline mesh showed that median 𝑦+ ≤ 1 can be obtained with
1 × 10−3 m first-cell height albeit with maximum 𝑦+ ≈ 7, so a 2 cm nominal cell size mesh was created with 1 × 10−3 m
wall first-cell height and referred to as a ’Coarse Boundary Layer (BL)’ mesh. Refined mesh had 4 times smaller
nominal cell size of 1 cm. A mesh independent solution is obtained and discussed in the Results section.

Fluid properties are evaluated from the current National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) databases [21].

3. Interface Mesh Resolution
The wall-like turbulence treatment on the gas side of the interface requires sufficient resolution to accurately capture



turbulence transport — akin to a boundary layer. The high temperature gradient near the interface also must be resolved
for reliable heat transfer and phase change prediction because the phase change model (Equation 12) is highly sensitive
to the local saturation condition and therefore to the interface temperature (Equation 10).



A first-cell height of at least 1× 10−4 m normal to the interface has been found to be necessary to resolve temperature
gradient on the gas side of the interface (Figure 8). The self-pressurization rate is directly affected by resolution of this
thermal gradient. This finding is consistent across two-phase CFD modeling of CFM operations. [14, 15, 33]



4. Natural Convection Currents
The wall heat leakage forms natural convection currents in the liquid phase. At Rayleigh number of 1.2𝑒15, the



natural convection currents in liquid are turbulent. The characteristics of convective vortices depend on fluid properties,
tank shape and the intensity of the heat flux. These currents transport warmer liquid to the gas-liquid interface and
increase the interface temperature which increases the tank pressure through interfacial heat and mass transfer. Resolving
these convecting cells — larger eddies at the minimum — is therefore essential for accurate pressure prediction and
requires wall integrated boundary layer modeling, i.e., wall 𝑦+ ≤ 1 to resolve sharp thermal and velocity gradients, and
sufficient interior mesh refinement in the liquid region to resolve flow vorticity, and turbulence transport independent
of mesh. The development and eventual stabilization of the large convective cells is demonstrated in Figure 9. The
sharp 90-degree turning of the liquid at the gas-liquid interface (for 50% fill-level test) also produces high vorticity and
periodic elliptical vortices which must also be resolved well with refined interface normal mesh.

3. Interfacial Thermal Transport
The vapor phase, which is typically warmer than the liquid, transfers a fraction of the absorbed heat leak ( ¤𝑄𝑠𝑔)



to the liquid interface, denoted as ¤𝑄𝑔𝑖 . In the liquid phase, natural convection (in presence of gravity/acceleration)
transports thermal energy from the gas to liquid (𝑄𝑙𝑖) via buoyant warm liquid; in microgravity, thermal conduction
transfers thermal energy. If heat transport from the liquid to the interface exceeds than that from the vapor, evaporation
occurs (Equation 12).



Gravity and turbulence strongly affect interfacial thermal transport. Gas-side interface is wall-like, i.e., no eddy
viscosity, owing to the presence of the high density liquid, while the liquid-side turbulence transport is unaffected by
presence of low-density vapor (see Interface Turbulence Transport). Due to this asymmetric turbulence transport, liquid
has almost 145 times higher effective conductivity 𝑘𝑒 𝑓 𝑓 ,𝑙 , which results in evaporation at the interface (Equation 12).



Because the heat leakage rate to the vapor ¤𝑄𝑠𝑔 is larger and more time-varying than the vapor-to-interface heat rate
¤𝑄𝑔𝑖 , (Figure 17), ¤𝑄𝑠𝑔 primarily governs change in the pressurization rate.



H. Initial Condition Effect on Pressurization Rate
Incongruent initial conditions were also suspected to cause the CFD prediction mismatch with 25% and 90% fill-level



tests. Two temperature initializations are tested for investigation. Method 1 uses the temperature probe data from the
tests to initialize fluid temperature, and the wall temperature is set equal to the adjacent fluid. Uniform stationary
velocity field was initialized.



After identifying the importance of heat leak distribution, Method 2 was tested: starting with Method 1 initialization,
boiloff is simulated until the ullage temperatures attain a steady state. In Method 2, solid temperatures differ from the
adjacent fluid. Velocity field is initialized from the end of the boiloff simulation. Method 2 improves initial pressure
prediction but does not affect the long term pressurization rate. (Figure 21)

VI. Conclusion
A two-phase frozen gas-liquid interface CFD methodology — previously applied to successfully validate multiple



CFM tests for the CFM Modeling Portfolio — is applied to four MHTB self-pressurization tests. With application of
asymmetric interface turbulence transport, SST, IDDES, and WALE turbulence closure models are found to be similarly
effective at predicting time-accurate pressures and temperatures.



The 50% fill-level test is validated with high accuracy for the CFM Modeling Portfolio: the relative average
pressurization rate prediction error is −2.2% and temperature predictions are satisfactory. Deviation in temperature
prediction near the top and bottom regions on the tank is attributed to simplified model that does not include detailed
tank structures and localized penetration heat leaks. Predictions are independent of the spatial and temporal resolution
and insensitive to the phase change accommodation factor in the HKS equation.



A first-cell height of 0.1 mm at the gas-liquid interface accurately resolves the turbulent characteristics and,
consequently, thermal gradients and phase change. The same resolution is also found to be necessary to ensure 𝑦+ ≤ 1
at the tank walls for accurate thermal transport in the tank boundary layer.



The 25% and 90% fill-level tests were not well predicted, which prompted a detailed investigation, including
a sensitivity study of spatial distribution of heat leak. To the author’s knowledge, this is the first application of a
previously validated CFD methodology to perform such sensitivity analysis; historically, these distributions were
obtained from thermal/multi-nodal models. The CFD sensitivity analysis provides detailed insights into the mechanisms
of self-pressurization process.



The analyses revealed that, owing to the thermal properties of the fluids and the tank wall, most of the heat leak
from the tank wall is distributed to the liquid. However, tank pressurization rate is primarily governed by the rate of heat
leak to the gas phase. The vapor heat leak sources — insulation/wall leak, penetration leaks, and radiation leaks —
should be measured with high accuracy in tests designed for developing and validating analytical models.



For minimizing self-pressurization during future spaceflight missions, the study indicates that the primary focus
should be on reducing heat leakage to the vapor phase.



The sensitivity analyses also produced calibrated spatial distributions of heat leak that accurately predict tank
pressure and temperature. However, further investigation of the identified spatial heat leak distributions is warranted.
A 3-D CFD model with more detailed tank structure and penetrations could also be used for exploratory sensitivity
analyses.



The study highlights the need for conducting high-fidelity sensitivity analyses when predicting self-pressurization
for flight vehicles in operating conditions that have not been well-characterized. Such analyses could help: (1) increase
conservatism/margins where previous analyses may underpredict pressurization rates, and (2) permit narrower design
margins where higher-fidelity CFD analyses results reduce uncertainties of lower-fidelity predictions. This study serves
as an important step toward improved understanding and modeling of self-pressurization for supporting long-term deep
space exploration missions.
