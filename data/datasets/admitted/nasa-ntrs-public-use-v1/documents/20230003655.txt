Background Information 





Flash Drought





Indicators should be focused on leading impacts (NOAA, 2021)








Image Credit: cmophot.net











In recent years, researchers have coined the term, “flash drought”, referring to the rapid onset or intensification of drought-like conditions. Flash droughts can happen in a matter of weeks or months, so quickly that most go unperceived. Flash droughts can occur when conditions just a couple of months prior were unfavorable for the development of drought (Otkin et al., 2018). Flash droughts are caused by extended periods of low precipitation, unusually high temperatures, strong winds, and/or incoming radiation, which causes exceptionally high evapotranspiration (ET) rates (Flash Drought | Drought.gov, n.d.).


Flash drought can be defined in a number of ways. According to a National Oceanic Atmospheric Association (NOAA) review of how flash drought is defined in scientific literature, Soil Moisture (SM) is the 2nd most common indicator, while vegetative health is the least common indicator. While that may be indicative of where the literature has been focused, at the same NOAA workshop it was agreed that “indicators should be focused on leading impacts” like vegetative health and SM (NOAA, 2021).

Community Concerns


In 2016, 92% of Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, & Tennessee experienced severe flash drought conditions that led to:





Widespread wildfires





Disputes over water rights





Deterioration of pasture and rangeland 





Crop sale losses in the hundreds of millions





Monitoring, forecasting, and communication tools are needed to offset potential losses from flash drought events. 








Image Credit: Waldemar











Another notable example of a SEUS flash drought occurred in 2016 with 92% of Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, and Tennessee experiencing “severe drought” or worse in the fall of 2016 according to the United States Drought Monitor (USDM). The event resulted in widespread wildfires, reservoir shortages that led to interstate disputes over water rights, significant deterioration of pasture and rangeland, and crop sale losses in the hundreds of millions of dollars (Park Williams et al., 2017). The occurrence of a flash drought similar to those listed above could cost the Southeast hundreds of millions of dollars with effects lasting several years. For this reason, the development of non-elusive, region-specific flash drought monitoring, forecasting, and communication tools are needed to alert both states and their practitioner’s of possible flash drought events, offsetting the potential loss caused by a flash drought event.

The study area for this project is the southeastern United States focusing on Alabama, Georgia, Florida, Tennessee, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Virginia.  The Southeastern U.S. has a subtropical and humid climate with various extreme weather and climate events such as heatwaves, cold temperature outbreaks, flooding, drought, tropical storms, winter storms, and tornadoes (Anderson et al., 2013).  Average temperatures for the entire region consist of a high of 22°C (72°F) and a low of 9°C (48°F).  These average temperatures for states in the southeast depend on latitude and proximity to the ocean (Allmon et al., 2010)

The team partnered with the Alabama Office of the State Climatologist (AOSC), the National Integrated Drought Information System (NIDIS), and the National Coordinate Soil Moisture Monitoring Network (NCSMMN). The partners were concerned about the effects of flash drought on crops during water sensitive growing periods. Partners can use the results of the project to better manage agricultural areas within the Southeast and to develop materials to be shared with stakeholders residing in drought vulnerable regions. The objectives were (1) to create a climatology time series using NASA Sport and (2) to create flash drought susceptibility maps using soil moisture data. (3) our final objective is to create a circular flash drought crop calendar detailing the sensitive stages of the crop.

 John Case, one of the product managers for NASA’s Short-Term Prediction and Transition Center – Land Information Center (SPoRT-LIS), provided high-resolution (about 3 -km) gridded root zone soil moisture products from 2000 to 2022 from SPoRT-LIS. This data was used to make a climatological time series analysis. Google Earth Engine (GEE) was used to acquire and process Surface Reflectance Tier 1 Landsat 7 Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus (ETM+; 1999-2022), Landsat 8 Operational Land Imager (OLI; ) (2013-2022), and Landsat 9 OLI-2 (2021-2022) data from January 2000 to November 2022 (Table 1). The team used the data to calculate the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) and land surface temperature (LST). Lastly, Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM) data from Oregon State University was utilized to create spatial climate datasets indicating short and long-term precipitation trends.

 For this presentation, the team decided to focus on an area in the Southeast United States that contains a variety of agriculture to describe the indicators of flash drought. The team chose to focus on North Alabama for this presentation because the Imagery containing NDVI analysis was available for the 9-month time frame of March to November for the years 2015 to 2016.  Landsat satellites orbit the earth every 16 days (about two and a half weeks), passing over the same area twice a month. The satellite may not capture images of the exact locations in its second orbit.  After filtering the images to remove cloud cover, NDVI pixels may not be present in the same area across the different months. Due to these restraints, the team could not calculate NDVI across the entire SEUS for each month. This is why they choose to focus on Agricultural area in North Alabama.

Results – When? September.


Image Credit: Magda Ehlers











A flash drought in September can be especially damaging to crops planted in the middle to late fall. A flash drought in September can decline pastureland which effects hay production, make the ground hard affecting peanut harvest, and dry out vegetation creating fuel for wildfires (Liberto, 2019). Cotton bloom also occurs in the fall in North Alabama. During its blooming period, cotton requires 2.1 inches of water/week to prevent moisture stress (Reeves, 1997). So, a flash drought in September could be potentially damaging to cotton.

The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index uses information of reflectance of two spectral regions: visible light region and near infrared region. The relationship between these two spectral zones and the vegetation depends on the state of the vegetation. In this way, it is possible to link the NDVI to the state of the vegetation. The NDVI ranges in value from -1 to +1. The NDVI can be seen both as observed and as its anomaly. Observed: The observed NDVI is given on a monthly basis and ranges in value from -1 to +1. The state of the vegetation is determined by how near the NDVI is to +1. For instance, a forest will have an NDVI value that is closer to +1 than a desert region, which will have a number that is closer to 0.

Errors/Uncertainties


There were infrequent Landsat data to compare with other datasets





Not enough time to validate the NDVI analysis 





The time-period was reduced by 18 years to keep consistency across datasets 





Missing Landsat Data


August 2016

















The team encountered several errors and uncertainties while working on the project. First, there was infrequent Landsat data to compare with other datasets. Landsat orbits the Earth every 16 days leaving some data missing. Another uncertainty was encountered with validation. The team was only able to validate known instances of flash drought cited in literature, such as the 2016 flash drought in September and October. The last error/uncertainty was that the time period of study was reduced from 1982-2022 to 2000 to 2022.

Conclusions





A flash drought was detected in North Alabama in September of 2016 via three different drought indicators: precipitation, soil moisture, and vegetation health.





The three normalized anomalies highlight greater than average drought indicator values in September 2016 as normalized against September values over 22 years. The efficacy of such indicators can be compared via how well they detected flash drought.








Future projects could focus on analyzing Landsat data to assess NDVI for flash drought susceptibility maps creation. 

















For this slide the team will summarize the findings and provide information on the future work section.





The flash drought that occurred in North Alabama in September of 2016 was detected with the indicators Precipitation, Soil Moisture, and Vegetation health. Normalized anonmalies highlight greater than average drought indictor values. A future project could focus on analyzing landsat data to assess NDVI for flash drought susceptibility maps creation.
