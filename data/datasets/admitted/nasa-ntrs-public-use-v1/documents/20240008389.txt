SLS First Flight: Assessment of Artemis-I Motor Performance


Nikolas Ciaston[footnoteRef:2], and Brad Roe[footnoteRef:3] [2: Analyst, Performance Analysis, Propulsion Systems, Promontory Division] [3: Analyst, Analysis Methods and Processes, Propulsion Systems, Promontory Division] 



Northrop Grumman Space Systems


Timothy Kibbey[footnoteRef:4] [4: Motor Performance and Advanced Design Subject Matter Expert, NASA Marshall Space Flight Center] 



National Aeronautics and Space Administration





NASA’s Artemis-I mission, which launched from Kennedy Space Center on November 16, 2022 and lasted 25 days, was the first flight test of the new Space Launch System vehicle as well as its twin solid rocket boosters. This flight test was the culmination of many years of development, testing, construction, and assembly completed by NASA and a large consortium of corporate partners, including Northrop Grumman. The solid rocket boosters are updated five-segment versions of those used during the Space Shuttle program, re-designed to provide enhanced performance and lifting capability. Booster motor performance had been characterized over the course of seven ground static tests conducted between 2009 and 2022, including three development tests, two qualification tests, and two supplemental flight support tests. The Artemis-I flight provided the first opportunity to assess solid rocket motor performance in a flight environment for any potential flight-ground biases and to ensure that measured flight performance was within requirement and analytical model limits and expectations.





Motor performance was characterized on the Artemis-I flight using a series of pressure transducers installed in the head-end of the combustion chamber of each booster. Using these pressure measurements, combined with physics-based models and empirical factors derived from ground testing, an accurate representation of overall motor performance could be reconstructed and then assessed relative to pre-flight predictions, established requirements, and ground test results. 





Artemis-I post-flight motor performance assessments indicate that the Artemis-I boosters were close to pre-flight predictions, consistent with previous static test results, and met all performance requirements. No significant flight versus ground static test biases were observed. All data gathered show that the boosters and SLS vehicle performed as-designed, the Artemis-I mission was a success, and has paved the way for future crewed SLS missions and the larger Artemis program.

II. SLS Booster Overview





The primary component of the SLS booster is the five-segment solid rocket motor, designated RSRMV, which is a modification of the Space Shuttle Reusable Solid Rocket Motor (RSRM) and has previously been ground static tested seven times, from 2009 to 2022. Propellant formulation, case design, and nozzle material are similar to the heritage configuration. However, the propellant grain geometry, burn rate, and nozzle geometry changed which, along with the additional segment, results in the very different motor performance needed to meet the SLS’s increased needs.





All RSRMV segments are labeled and shown in Figure 3. The forward segment propellant, shown graphically in Figure 3, has a star region at the forward portion with a tapered transition to a center perforated (CP) region at the aft portion of the segment. The forward-center, center-center, and aft-center segments have identical propellant core geometries consisting of a tapered CP with a leading-edge chamfer. The aft segment propellant consists of a multi-tapered CP with a leading-edge chamfer and a nozzle cutout region in the aft portion of the segment. The RSRMV nozzle, shown in Figure 4, is lined with carbon cloth phenolic liner, which protects the nozzle hardware and provides ablative cooling. A multi-layer flex bearing allows the nozzle to vector in any direction. The flight booster configuration, assembled from all components discussed above for a single booster, is shown below in Figure 5.

III. Prediction and Reconstruction Methodology


1. Artemis-I Pre-Flight Prediction





A total of seven ground static tests of the RSRMV were conducted between 2009 and 2022 to characterize and certify motor performance over a range of temperatures, with various configurations and material changes, as shown in Table 1. The first three tests were classified as demonstrations of motor performance across the operational temperature range. The next two qualification tests certified motor performance as meeting all design requirements and certified the booster as a whole for being ready for flight. The final two “flight support” tests were conducted to gather additional specific data pertaining to nozzle liner material, a different propellant AP vendor, and also to utilize unused motor segments originally built for Artemis-I.





Data gathered from these ground tests, including head-end and aft-end chamber pressure, thrust, case hoop strain, and more, were used to construct analytical models of nominal and dispersed motor performance. These models are provided to NASA to convey motor performance expectations used for certification, loads analysis, and mission planning. As such, the Artemis-I pre-flight prediction was provided to NASA more than a year prior to the launch and serves as a basis of assessing measured flight performance, as described below.

In addition to the empirical factors reconstructed from the static test motor population, predicted Artemis-I performance also incorporated the measured propellant mass of each motor, measured nozzle throat diameters, and the weighted-average burn rate of the subscale batch check motors fired concurrently with the propellant casting of the 10 motor segments. The primary outputs of the pre-flight prediction were the transient profiles of head-end chamber pressure, vacuum thrust, and total overboard mass flow rate, as well as the average propellant burn rate of each Artemis-I motor. 1. Artemis-I Post-Flight Reconstruction and Data Analysis





As described above regarding static test motors, Artemis-I motor production data and measured flight pressure data were combined with physics-based tools to reconstruct steady-state motor performance, including but not limited to thrust, mass flow rate, and average burn rate. Production data includes measured propellant mass, nozzle throat diameter, and subscale average burn rate. That is, the same parameters and values used in the pre-flight prediction. The schematic for the post-flight reconstruction process employed to assess Artemis-I flight performance is shown in Figure 6 (b). 





The flight reconstruction process is largely similar to the pre-flight prediction process, where the main difference is the use of measured flight data instead of some of the empirical parameters assumed for the prediction. The activity represented by the “Ballistics Analysis” box is also different. The methodology used to reconstruct flight performance is nearly identical to that used for static test motors but deviates due to lack of critical measurements. Additional instrumentation installed on static test motors provide a means to quantify axial pressure drop, or the pressure recovery factor, as it is known within the 1-D ballistics code. 





Additionally, thrust measured by the static test stand is used to quantify thrust efficiency. Since aft-end pressure and thrust are not measured in-flight, the static test average of the two corresponding empirical parameters was assumed in the Artemis-I reconstruction. Finally, since the Artemis-I boosters were not recovered and dissected post-flight, the amount of nozzle throat erosion had to be assumed based on the static test population. 


















































IV. Recorded Flight Performance Data





Head-end chamber pressure was measured on both Artemis-I boosters by four transducers providing both low-speed and high-speed data. The transducers were installed in the head-end of the forward segment and housed by the forward assembly, as shown in Figure 7. 








Figure 7: OPT Location in Reference to Booster Assembly


The signal from one of the pressure transducers was split and sent to both the low-speed and high-speed systems, resulting in four pressure transducers supplying five data streams for each booster. Data from both data streams were sent to the Orion flight computer and telemetered to the ground for data analysis. 





Analyses of all pressure data have shown that the pressure transducers and related avionics systems performed well and as designed. Post-flight performance assessments are largely based on the median pressure reading within each motor over the motor operational time. Figure 8 shows the pressure differential between each low-speed pressure channel and the composite median values of each booster. All gauges were within 2 psi of their respective median at all times, which is a similar level of gauge-to-gauge variability observed previously on static test motors and indicates excellent performance of the transducers and avionics system.











Figure 8. OFI Pressure Gauge Comparison to Median Pressure Trace


V. Results and Discussion





Overall, Artemis-I motor performance was similar to pre-flight predictions, bounded by specification requirements and math model statistical expectations, and consistent with the static test population. The performance of the two Artemis-I motors were also, critically, very similar to one another. These comparisons will be described in detail for various aspects of motor performance below. As indicators of the level of similarity in booster flight pair performance, the total measured propellant masses for the Artemis-IA and IB boosters were within 0.01%. Additionally, the average burn rates of the subscale batch check motors tested with the propellant casting were identical to four decimal places.


1. Reconstructed Data Comparison





The reconstructed performance of the Artemis-IA and IB boosters matched well with their pre-flight predictions. 





Figure 9 shows a comparison of the measured and predicted pressuretime curves for both Artemis-I boosters. The measured pressures for both boosters are in good agreement with the pre-flight prediction for the majority of motor burn time. Additionally, current tail-off prediction methodology, which applies an assumed flight/static test bias to average static test performance, seems to have represented actual performance reasonably well. The assumed tail-off bias was derived from historical RSRM data and will be discussed in greater detail below.











Figure 9. Artemis-I Measured Head-End Pressure versus Pre-flight Prediction





Figure 10 and Figure 11 show reconstructed vacuum thrust comparisons for both the Artemis-IA and IB boosters. Specifically, Figure 10 shows the reconstructed vacuum thrust calculated from low-speed pressure data for both boosters. Reconstructed thrust results for both boosters show that delivered performance matched well with the pre-test predictions. Figure 11 compares delivered Artemis-I vacuum thrust imbalance against the associated requirement bounds. This comparison clearly indicates the Artemis-I boosters were well-matched, exhibiting thrust imbalance significantly below the requirement bounds.

Figure 12, Figure 13, and Figure 14 compare the head-end pressure measured in each RSRMV motor tested to-date; each highlighting a particular aspect. Figure 12 provides a high-level overview of the pressure results, where the curves have been adjusted to the established reference PMBT and burn rate conditions. This full-scale comparison conveys good overall similarity in the population. Figure 13 frames the comparison in terms of trace shape variation, where each curve represents the differential from the nine-motor population average, as a percentage. The trace shape variation exhibited by the Artemis-I boosters compares well to the previous RSRMV static test motors, displaying minimal motor-to-motor variation. 








Both Figure 12 and Figure 13 exhibit slightly larger variation in performance in two primary intervals: the early portion of the curve, up to 0.2, and later in burn around 0.7 to 0.9 of normalized time. The late-burn portion is attributed to configuration changes that occurred during the course of the RSRMV development and testing program, most notably to changes in the aft-segment case configuration and changes in insulation thickness as data were gathered and conservatism could be eliminated. The observed spread during the early-burn portion of the population is bounded by motor performance requirements and analytical models provided to NASA for integrated vehicle simulations. This behavior will continue to be monitored going forward as more data are analyzed and assessed.

Burn Rate





The time-averaged burn rates reconstructed from measured Artemis-I flight data were found to differ by only 0.0001 in/sec between the two motors and less than 0.001 in/sec from the pre-flight prediction. These differentials are well within expected levels represented in motor performance requirements and dispersion models. Comparing to the static test population of reconstructed burn rates shown in Figure 15, the Artemis-I boosters fall close to the average and are within 1 variation from the population average. With the current manufacturing process, this is a continued confirmation that the SLS boosters are providing reliable and consistent performance.

Pressure Oscillation Performance





As with ignition, high-speed pressure data were used to assess the chamber acoustic response of the Artemis-I motors. Results computed from the redundant pressure channels on each booster were very consistent, and thus shown for only one channel per booster below.





Figure 19 shows that the dynamic pressure spectrograms for the Artemis-I motors, (a) and (b), are consistent with that the mean of the RSRMV database (c), with the differences in timing and amplitude representative of typical motor-to-motor variation. The RSRMV motors all show some activity in the first three longitudinal acoustic modes. Activity begins approximately one third of the way into motor operation with a series of “sweeping” structures in the 2-L mode, which decrease in frequency with time, generally attributed to vortex shedding. Overall, the acoustic response of Artemis-IA and IB boosters compare well with each other and with the rest of the RSRMV population. 





Figure 20 and Figure 21 compare the maximum acoustic amplitudes observed over time on each SLS test motor for the 1-L, 2-L, and 3-L modes, respectively. As with the contour plots, these figures show some small differences in the acoustic response of Artemis-IA and IB relative to one another and to the rest of the population. These differences represent typical motor-to-motor variation and do not indicate any significant population excursions or change in response associated with the flight environment. It should be noted that the RSRM population did exhibit a different 1-L response among flight motors than for static test motors. Specifically, the 1-L oscillation amplitudes late in motor operation were lower on flight motors than on static test motors, as can be seen in Figure 21 (a). Based on Artemis-I flight data shown in Figure 21 (b), RSRMV does not appear to have a comparable bias, nor any other discernable bias. This conclusion will be reassessed as additional flight data are obtained.

VI. Conclusions





The Artemis-I mission was the first flight test of the Space Launch System vehicle and the five-segment solid rocket booster. This provided the first opportunity to assess performance of the SLS booster in-flight and compare to the seven previously tested static motors. Head-end chamber pressure measurements recorded on each booster were used to assess Artemis-I motor flight performance. These along with measured production data and standard analysis methodologies were used to predict and reconstruct performance for the Artemis-I flight set. 





Based on post-flight reconstruction and data analysis, the performance of both Artemis-I boosters was close to pre-flight prediction and consistent with the seven-motor static test population. This assessment was made relative to ignition, steady-state, and tail-off performance, including head-end pressure and vacuum thrust as well as specific performance metrics such as propellant burn rate and specific impulse. Additionally, the chamber acoustic response was assessed using high-speed pressure measurements. All assessments indicated that Artemis-I performed as expected, consistent with the static test population and within specification requirements and analytical dispersion model bounds. These results combine to show that the current nominal and dispersed math models, analysis methodologies, and assumptions are adequate and appropriate. This conclusion will be reevaluated as additional flight data become available.
