<p>Introduction 
</p>
<p>  Energy is the basis of life on the planet and of human civilization. Humans added to nutritionally derived 
energy when they adopted fire, chemical combustion, which could be created whenever and wherever needed. In the 
Industrial Age, fossil chemical energy sources such as coal, natural gas, and petroleum were the dominant energetics 
options and they vastly increased human capabilities and impacts [ref. 1]. One of those impacts, unfortunately, has 
been climate change. More recently, nuclear has been added to the energy mix, providing some 20% of the electrical 
grid generation in the U.S. Energy is a sizable portion of the gross domestic product (GDP) and is a key determiner 
of design for extant and projected capabilities of industries such as agriculture, housing, manufacture, transportation, 
space faring, econometrics, and national security. There have been extensive efforts to ideate, discover, and develop 
alternative/improved energy sources and utilization approaches. These efforts have been greatly accelerated by 
</p>
<p>1</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>climate change and the resulting need to shift to renewable energy sources, those not emitting CO2, and away from 
fossil fuels for most applications [ref. 2]. The now rapid developments in space exploration, national security space, 
and commercial deep space have highlighted serious needs to shift (given the importance of weight) from a 
dominant dependence in space faring on chemical energetics to others with greater energy density and longevity. 
The current contenders or alternatives for such are solar, fission nuclear, fusion nuclear, radiologics (termed herein 
nuclear batteries), low energy nuclear reactions (LENR), and positrons. 
   Space faring, including space exploration, commercialization, and colonization, requires serious levels of power 
and energy. It is required for in-space and on-body propulsion, habitats and transportation, In-Situ Resource 
Utilization (ISRU), manufacturing, life support, robotics, satellites, sensors, and construction. The current power and 
energy sources being applied and under development include solar energy, chemical fuels, radioisotope 
thermoelectric generator (RTG) nuclear batteries, and fission nuclear reactors. There are problems with each of these 
including reductions in solar intensity farther from the Sun and due to dust, ISRU resource processing requirements, 
storage, transfer of chemical fuels and the weight, energy density, and safety of the current nuclear approaches [ref. 
1]. Alternative energy sources are sought to reduce cost, weight, and improve safety, efficiency, and functionality. 
Particularly interesting alternatives include the recent invention of very high energy density, low weight nuclear 
batteries that have orders of magnitude greater energy density than RTGs and up to orders of magnitude less weight 
than reactors with scalability from milliwatts to tens of megawatts. This approach appears to be capable of powering 
everything for space faring, from small sensors to tens of megawatts for Variable Specific Impulse Magnetoplasma 
Rocket (VASIMR), which would provide fast, 200-day Mars round trips with 6,000 seconds of Isp. Additionally, in 
space this battery could power tethers working off the Earth’s magnetic field to fuellessly collect space debris and 
repurpose such via in-space remanufacturing. Additional frontier power and energy approaches include 
regeneration, utilization of heat losses via various energy conversion methods to improve efficiency, reduced weight 
and cost of energy generation, and heat rejection systems. Also, there are much more efficient and smaller multi-
phase space radiator approaches.  
   The purpose of this report is to describe a newly invented radiologic, weak force nuclear battery termed Nuclear 
Thermionic Avalanche Cell (NTAC) with superb scalability, energy density, and low weight and consider its 
characteristics, capabilities, competition, and applications both for space faring and many terrestrial needs going 
forward including energy extraction/remediation of nuclear waste [ref. 3]. 
</p>

However, due to the magnitude of impinging high energy photons and energetic electrons liberated, there are 
subsequent interactions after the primary interaction, such as the secondary, the tertiary, etc. of residual energy

<p>overall performance that differentiates it from others, delivering very high current density delivered to its power 
circuit. The photoionic process in NTAC liberates and delivers not only the electrons (~103 C/cm3) in the outermost 
shell of atoms, but also a huge number of electrons (105 C/cm3) from the intraband of atoms by impinging energetic 
photons and beta particles. Combined, the liberated electrons become avalanche electrons (high current density) 
flowing into the power circuit.  
Inner-most K-shell electrons of heavy atoms require only ~150 keV to be liberated by photo-electric (pe), 
photonuclear (pn), Compton scattering (Cs), or electron/positron pair production (pp) processes. The intraband 
electrons liberated by gamma-ray photons through pe, pn, Cs, or pp processes still carry several tens to hundreds of 
keV which is sufficient to knock out further vast numbers of intraband electrons through the secondary, tertiary, and 
even higher order collision interactions from neighboring atoms. Through such interaction processes, emissions of 
additional Auger electrons, X-ray fluorescence, and Compton edges are observed and subsequently interact further 
with neighboring atoms to liberate more electrons. Through such sequential interactions, a large number of electrons 
(105 C/cm3) liberated from the IB, including the outermost shell, of atoms are led into and utilized for a power 
circuit. This process is only possible when a high-grade energy source (&gt; 1 keV) is used for knocking out the IB 
electrons. The power output is determined by the increased number of electrons running through a power circuit. 
The increased power output using a high-grade energy source enables high specific power and accordingly 
portability. 
Through the photoelectric (pe) and photonuclear (pn) effects, Compton scattering (Cs), and electron/positron pair 
production (pp), the absorbed energy is proportional to the absorption cross-section (σt = σCs + σpe + σpn + σpp), the 
atomic number of matter (Z), and the thickness in the primary interaction. γ-rays can penetrate an electron band 
structure of atom into its nucleus, disrupting the shell electrons from their probability space.  Moreover, γ-rays also 
interact with IB electrons directly, liberating secondary and tertiary electrons in the avalanche process.  
As an example, the number of lanthanum (La) atoms per gram is 4.33 x 1021/g or 2.67 x 1022/cm3.  Suppose that 29 
of 57 La electrons are stripped off as avalanche electrons. The energy required to strip off 29 electrons out of an La 
atom is 17.641 keV for L-edge and 38.925 keV for K-edge electrons [ref. 4]. The available electrons per cm3 then is 
7.74 x 1023/cm3, or 124,042 C/cm3 (≈105 C/cm3 or ≈107 C/kg), which is five orders of magnitude higher in energy 
density than conventional systems based on free or dopant density-dependent valence band electrons only. As

The key features of the NTAC system include: the use of a high-grade energy source, such as γ-ray (&gt; 100 keV) and

avalanche of free electrons for more power generation through bound-to-free and free-to-free transitions of 
intraband electrons of atoms after photon coupling, the power scaling from mW to MW, which is determined by the 
amount of radiation source, the thermal energy generated by the scattering of energetic electrons that is useful to 
keep holding electrons thermalized to be more active and mobile (the rest will be converted by the MJ-TE 
generator), the lowest system α among other energy conversion systems, lack of a specific doping requirement for p-
n junctions, the simple system with no moving parts within the NTAC system, and the long duration of operation 
determined by the activity rate of radioisotopes. 
The use of nuclear energy generation, either in space-faring or in terrestrial applications, requires extreme attention 
to human safety, given the well-known adverse health effects of exposure to ionizing radiation. Early on in space 
faring, a detailed set of requirements were developed for launching nuclear material into space. These included 
protection from fires, crushing and explosions, as well as the radiation, to ensure the radiation shielding is not 
breached. NTAC utilizes high energy gamma emitters. Neutrons are, at the same energy level, adjudged to be 10X 
of a health hazard compared to gammas. But of the other forms of radiation, alpha and beta, gamma is considered 
the most worrisome. To provide both better protection for humans and reduce requisite shielding weight, the NTAC 
battery is designed to utilize most of the gamma energy, making it partially self-shielding.

Metallic Junction TE (MJ-TE)

<p>MJ-TE [ref. 6] is a new type of thermoelectric generator (TEG) that converts heat to electricity ~6X more efficiently 
than existing technologies for the same mass, providing ~5X the power for unprecedented flexibility for NASA 
mission designers.  Metals as conductors have orders of magnitude more free electrons (~103 C/cm3) than 
semiconductors with limited available electron populations of 3 C/cm3 within the Brillouin phase space, which is 
heavily dictated by dopant density. Metals have electrical and thermal conductivities higher than semiconductors. 
Potential disparity between a pair of metals is much higher than in semiconductors, which is determined by the 
dopant density of positive type and negative type (p-n) junction materials. Even assuming that potential disparity 
across the p-n junction is not much different, the MJ-TE has a very high number of electrons available for free-to-
free transition with fast mobility in metals.  To evaluate the viability of the new TE generators, a two-dimensional 
heat transfer MATLAB simulation was constructed to calculate efficiency and power output. The simulation results 
of high Seebeck coefficient alloys, Chromel (90%Ni-10%Cr) and Constantan (55%Cu-45%Ni), revealed efficiencies 
of around 20-30% [ref. 7]. These improvements can be realized by controlling parameters such as the number of 
layers of junctions, lateral junction density, and junction sizes for both serial and parallel connected junctions as 
shown in the right of Figure 7. 
The MJ-TE technology [ref. 6] provides design flexibility for critical NASA missions by: (a) ~5X the power for the 
same mass for deep space spacecraft and rovers when utilized in Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators (RTGs); 
(b) reducing the weight/cost of solar cells and batteries; (c) significantly reducing lunar surface power infrastructure 
costs and mass by enabling laser based wireless power distribution of the NASA Kilopower reactor, and (d) 
converting waste heat to electricity in radiators or other components on spacecraft, rovers, and landers, increasing 
overall power system efficiency. 
MJ-TEs achieve this massive increase in thermal-to-electrical conversion efficiency by dramatically increasing 
carrier charge density. Performance of existing semiconductor-based p-n pair TEGs has already hit its theoretical 
efficiency limit as electron density is constrained by the dopant density to ~1 C/cm3.  In contrast, MJ-TE has 1000X 
higher carrier charge density achieving ~103 C/cm3 using two dissimilar metals (chromel and constantan) in 
junctions similar to industry standard thermopiles but structured at the micron scale. Figure 7 shows two charts for 
the performance of multi-layer MJ-TE as compared to MMRTG [Multi-Mission Radioisotope Thermoelectric 
Generator] and Kilopower.  The secondary advantage of this design is its micron scale structure using industry 
standard production processes, where existing deposition technologies can be used, and industry is available for 
prototyping and production.

The NTAC Competition 
 
NTAC’s uniqueness includes excellent scalability from powering phones to tens of megawatts to power MHD 
[MagnetoHydroDynamic], or other in-space high-thrust propulsion systems. It also has extraordinarily light weight 
per kW produced (termed Alpha), is largely self-shielding and very high kW production per kg of isotope. These 
merits exceed the usual competition (thermal conversion RTGs and fission reactors) by large amounts. The 
competing approaches to NTAC for long term power generation without chemical combustion requiring chemical 
“fuels” include advanced fission reactors of various designs and extant nuclear batteries/RTGs. Farther term 
potential competition includes nuclear fusion, the “inexpensive” antimatter (positrons), and LENR [refs. 11, 12].  
 
RTGS/Nuclear Batteries - The current most efficient nuclear battery is the RTG, long used by NASA, and 
employing the isotope Pu-238. It has a very long half-life, which is especially well suited for outer planet use, which 
involves years long missions. The issue or shortfall regarding these RTGs is their poor energy density, some 10ish 
watts per Kg of isotope, vs. the NTAC level of up to 22 kWs per Kg of isotope (but with a much shorter half-life at 
that level). Another issue with these RTGs is utilization of low efficiency thermal-electric energy conversion. 
Overall, their weight is much greater than an order of magnitude larger for the same power output than for NTAC. 
Other current nuclear batteries have even lower energy density except for a new design somewhat similar in concept 
to NTAC studied under the NASA NIAC program. 
   
Fission Reactors - Thus far, the only countries launching nuclear reactors into space have been Russia and the U.S. 
Russia launched 31 BES-5 fission reactors into space to power RORSATs starting in the 1960s using thermal 
electric energy conversion. Also starting in the 60s, Russia launched the TOPAZ small fission reactor (10 Kwe) 
using thermionic conversion. The U.S. launched the SNAP 10 [A] in the 1960s and in the 1980s started development 
of the SNAP 100. Unfortunately, the latter project was canceled before flight. More recently in the U.S., several 
micro nukes have been studied including Rapid-L and AMTEC, with the most recent one, Kilopower, on a 
development path for utilization on the Moon [ref. 13]. There are several notable benefits of fission reactors 
compared to chemical. The most important is years of high-power output. Before the invention of advanced nuclear 
batteries, fission reactors were the only cogent source of high-power levels over years. The historical down sides of 
fission reactors are many including launch safety, safety in use (especially regarding human missions), major size 
and weight driven by radiation protection and energy conversion, and protection from crushing, fires, impact, and 
explosions. There are ongoing efforts that offer somewhat reduced size, weight, and improved safety. Worldwide 
there is a plethora of small/miniature reactors under development. There are also traveling-wave reactors and a 
Russian vortex pebble bed design with greatly improved power density. Going forward, human missions will require 
24/7/365 availability of high-power levels for habs and ISRU. As a result, fission reactors, probably supplanted or 
supplemented by nuclear batteries, will be a mainstay of Moon and Mars human exploration, exploitation, and 
colonization [ref. 14]. All nuclear, and other power generation development will need to optimize advanced radiator 
and energy regeneration approaches to reduce system weight, size, and cost.

is now usually termed LENR (Low Energy Nuclear Reactions) was an experimental discovery with replication 
issues at the time and lacked an acceptable theory. Now, three decades of extant worldwide experiments [ref. 15] 
indicate “something nuclear” is real. However, there does not yet exist a cogent, verified theory and therefore LENR 
has been looked at with askance by the physics community. There are now extant recent weak force and other weak 
neutron-based theories (not “hot” fusion) involving surface plasmons, electroweak interactions explicable via 
QuantumElectroDynamics, surface collective effects, heavy electrons, ultraweak neutrons, and utilizing neutron 
generation to obviate coulomb barrier issues. There are now many patents and LENR is beginning to evolve into the 
marketplace. Given a validated theory to engineer, scale, and make safe, LENR would obviously be a major world 
energy revolution, especially with observed energy density levels greatly surpassing those of chemical. In fact, 
LENR has been observed in the tens to up to many thousand times chemical energy density levels. In the Widom-

Positrons – Positrons are positive electrons and are the affordable anti-matter. Medical pet scans utilize positrons. 
When they annihilate with an electron, producing two 511 Kev gamma rays, there is essentially a 100% mass to 
energy conversion. Therefore, their energy density is some E9 times chemical, which is order(s) of magnitude more 
than fission/fusion which involve fractional mass-energy conversion. There is no radioactive residue. This is the 
highest energy density source known and it can be produced in accelerators, with beta decay, and other 
methods/phenomena, including laser irradiation. The gamma produced can be used to heat tungsten, other materials, 
and used for propulsion or employed for electricity production via photoelectronics. The major issue with utilization 
of positrons for space power and energy is positron storage. Storage approaches have included Penning traps and as 
positronium and are an active area of research. Storage times on the order of 1,000 minutes have been mentioned 
with projections for storage duration exceeding a year. The alternative to storage is to use suitable isotopes and 
generate positrons as needed, which is the approach for medical pet scans. Studies of positron powered thermal 
rockets indicate Isp levels of 1,000, a bit greater than fission nuclear rockets at possibly reduced kg/kW (alpha) [ref. 
20]. 
</p>

<p> 
Fusion – For many decades, fusion energy has been an unrealized energetics vision regarding power and energy. 
This includes space applications, especially propulsion, for which there exists a plethora of designs and alternatives. 
Compared to fission energy, fusion is much more difficult to achieve. How difficult is a function of the “fuels” 
employed. Also, fusion can have a somewhat greater energy density, different, and usually lower, radioactivity 
hazards, and requires less expensive and more abundant fuel than fission. A plethora of approaches and fuel 
combinations have been conceptualized and studied over the years, including multitudinous fusion powered rocket 
designs. Thus far, fusion has not successfully produced a net positive energy output, even for terrestrial power where 
weight is not the serious issue it is in space. Anti-matter/positron power and energy is farther along than fusion and 
has orders of magnitude greater energy density. Currently, there are many “mini” fusion concepts under study. Some 
of these utilize highly densified deuterium, which itself requires far more study. However, if it is as suitable as 
envisaged, densified deuterium would greatly reduce the difficulty of establishing the conditions for fusion to occur. 
Key fusion space power and energy development issues include establishing and stabilizing the requisite conditions 
long enough for fusion to occur, energy conversion, radiation related safety, and overall weight and efficiency/useful 
net positive energy as well as cost competitiveness [refs.21, 22]. 
 
  Space Applications of NTAC 
</p>
<p>Recent invention of advanced nuclear batteries with up to orders of magnitude greater energy density and much 
reduced overall weight (alpha down to order one, up to 22 kWs/kg of isotope vice the usual 20 Watts/kg) which last 
for years, opens an entirely different option set for in-space transportation and on-planet/body operations [e.g., ref 
3].  The new nuclear battery designs could power nearly everything for space faring including in-space and on-body 
spacecraft and habs, ISRU, manufacturing and on-body and in-space transportation. Estimates indicate they could 
power VASIMR, a high thrust magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) propulsion system with an Isp of 6,000 seconds. This 
could enable a 200-day round trip to Mars, which would greatly alleviate in-space radiation and micro g health 
concerns. Other potential utilization includes powering satellites and deep space mining. For on-body surface and 
above surface (often necessitated by rugged terrain) transportation, there are three obvious possibilities regarding 
utilization of this new nuclear capability: lower speed, nuclear ramjets, and nuclear rockets. On Mars, such a nuclear 
battery could supply CO2 breathing propulsive lift and thrust for in-atmosphere long haul, as well as short haul via 
intaking CO2 from the atmosphere and pressurizing it via electric motors turning axial flow compressors, which is 
exhausted downwards using ejector nozzles to provide lift and thrust. For higher speeds up to high supersonics, the 
new nuclear batteries could either power heating and additional compression for an atmospheric ramjet or heating 
for a conventional rocket, with or without addition of chemical energy using on-planet ISRU derived propellant or 
propulsive mass (e.g., regolith, atmospheric CO2, volatiles). There are a multitude of ISRU applications for such 
nuclear batteries including microwaving the regolith to extract water and cooling surfaces to condense CO2, along 
with autonomous and lunar night operations. Electrolysis of ice into hydrogen propellant and oxidizer or production 
of methane for return (using chemical propulsion) fuel from Mars, requires far more power than current technologies 
can deliver (except much larger and more expensive nuclear reactors). Projections of full-scale performance suggest 
that the new nuclear batteries can provide essentially all on-body/planet energy requirements, including those that 
require portability. Alternatives include nuclear fission, solar, and wind. The latter two are less effective at Martian 
conditions and the former is larger, heavier and overall, more expensive by far. Also, the scalability of NTAC 
obviates the need for extensive cabling, energy distribution, and application energy storage associated with the 
larger, heavier fusion reactors. 
   For other than Mars missions/colonization, NTAC could power Moon transportation/activities including water 
extraction and processing, asteroid to/from transportation and mining, essentially all space activities requiring power 
and energy. The very favorable power density, size, and weight reductions, prime features of NTAC, along with 
concomitant reductions in cost and increases in capabilities, makes NTAC an enabler for sooner, faster, and more 
extensive deep space commercialization and development. In addition, using NTAC to power tethers to affordably 
and faster clean up space debris is an even more basic space enabler, as we are rapidly approaching a Kistler 
syndrome regarding collisions which would close out LEO and thereby close out space. NTAC enables atmospheric 
breathing powered entry, descent and landing (EDL) on Mars and bodies with atmospheres, even for very large 
payloads, removing a major impediment for humans to Mars travel. Power beaming is another obvious NTAC 
powered possibility. Overall NTAC enables “energy-rich” space faring. 
</p>

<p>     
  Terrestrial Applications of NTAC 
</p>
<p> 
  The NTAC attribute of being largely self-shielding increases the number of potential terrestrial applications. 
Terrestrial applications of nuclear energetics require rigid oversight and measures to ensure that radiological 
material and effects are not impacting human health. NTAC is a radiological battery that has the energy density of a 
reactor at far less size and weight. Typical nuclear reactor terrestrial applications include electricity generation, 
desalinization, hydrogen production, powering industrial processes, and producing gas products. NTAC could 
replace reactors for all these applications and its scalability down to small sizes would proffer additional utilizations. 
Nuclear waste is an increasingly critical and expensive issue. Currently, such radioactive waste is stored above 
ground, with concerns regarding security. This waste still contains on the order of 85% of the energy and could be 
used in an NTAC design to generate electricity, a new nuke use with major value in terms of energy generation and 
nuclear waste safety. There is also interest in using NTAC to power deep mining activities and an important 
application going forward would be to use NTAC to power robotics. Other, besides electrical energy production 
functions of nuclear reactors, NTAC applications include industrial processes and desalinization . Ships and 
submarines are powered by nuclear reactors now. Going forward, these (including robotic versions) could be 
powered by NTAC. Other maritime applications include seabed sensors, ocean mining, and powering underwater 
habitats. 
 
</p>
<p>Concluding Remarks 
</p>
<p> 
   The unique attributes of NTAC, including small size, ultra-low weight, high energy density, scalability, lower 
cost, and long life, posits a future, both in space and terrestrially, that is green, low-cost and uniquely energy rich. In 
space, NTAC will greatly increase affordable capabilities and accelerate the human exploration, pioneering and 
colonization of deep space and its commercialization beyond GEO. Terrestrially, NTAC can solve nuclear waste, 
while in space it can solve space debris, both major-to-existential issues of concern. Via powered EDL and fast 
transits in space, NTAC enables increased in space human health and safety. Thus far, the cost and other issues 
associated with chemical fuels/energy and the weight of reactors have dictated how fast and what could be 
accomplished in many arenas. NTAC constitutes a power and energy revolution and a consequent revolution in 
space faring and solutions to serious terrestrial issues.
