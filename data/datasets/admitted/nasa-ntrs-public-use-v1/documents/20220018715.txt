for NASA Small Spacecraft Missions 

Thomas M. Liu, John T. Yim, and Gabriel F. Benavides 
NASA Glenn Research Center 

 
Electric propulsion being developed for CubeSats and other small satellites with the taught 

expectation that high risk is generally acceptable are resulting in devices that do not meet a minimum 
expectation of engineering rigor for many missions, including appropriate qualification testing. This 
paper proffers a preliminary set of recommended guidelines and best practices in dialogue with the 
smallsat community to strike a better balance between engineering rigor and cost effectiveness for the 
qualification of EP technologies under consideration for NASA small spacecraft.  

Introduction and Scope 
Improved capability and miniaturization of spacecraft systems, along with common use of EELV (Evolved 

Expendable Launch Vehicle) secondary payload adapters (ESPA) to sell off excess launch capacity, provides 
increased opportunity to accomplish scientific, commercial, and military space missions at a fraction of historic 
costs. As such, ESPA-class and smaller satellites (< 500 kg) are rapidly growing in both utility and popularity. 
However, they still lack the high delta-v propulsive capabilities currently available in the larger state-of-the-art 
(SOA) all-electric spacecraft buses. Although recent years have experienced a relative explosion of smallsat 
electric propulsion (EP) concepts and varying levels of development, the number of mature and qualified 
devices remains low. [1-8] 

At first glance, the lack of suitable EP devices is unexpected given a relatively high frequency of recent EP 
small spacecraft investments by NASA (National Aeronautics and Space Administration), the DoD (Department 
of Defense), and industry. However, these investments are largely associated with the surge in development of 
nano-satellites (i.e., CubeSats), which began in 1999 with the creation of the CubeSat specification through a 
joint venture between California Polytechnic State University (Cal Poly) and Stanford University. The CubeSat 
specification provides a structured approach to small satellite development and launch, which promotes a low-
cost, hands-on learning experience towards designing, manufacturing, testing, and flying spacecraft. CubeSat 
programs throughout the world are proving fruitful in cultivating the next generation of aerospace engineers, 
just as university programs such as Formula SAE have for decades successfully cultivated engineers for the 
automotive industry. 

Unfortunately, the promise and appeal of small satellites — namely lower cost and quicker turnaround — 
are somewhat tempered by significantly higher risks and failure rates associated with minimal expectations for 
qualification of devices targeting the CubeSat market. A higher level of risk may be perfectly acceptable for 
some purposes, including student learning opportunities, high-volume commercial concepts, and new 
technology demonstrations. However, for a growing subset of small spacecraft missions — where the scientific 
data or commercial service from the payload is the primary objective and the small spacecraft is not readily 
replaceable — a rebalancing of acceptable risk versus cost and schedule is in order. This includes small 
spacecraft in consideration as secondary payloads on NASA deep-space exploration and science missions — 
which are typically classified as Small Category 3, Risk Classification D (cat3/classD) missions, NASA’s lowest 
cost and highest risk categories [9-11] — but still have a much lower acceptable risk profile than university 
CubeSats or other spacecraft flown on NASA’s CubeSat Launch Initiative. In other words, EP and other systems 
largely being developed for CubeSats and other small satellites with the taught expectation that high risk is 
generally acceptable is resulting in many devices which do not meet a minimum expectation of engineering 
rigor for missions, including appropriate qualification testing.

A number of reviews of spacecraft failures, including ones focused on CubeSats and small spacecraft, have 

been conducted in recent years and shed some light on potential causes and areas of failure. Propulsion systems 
and electrical systems, both inherently tied to EP, are two of the highest failure areas noted. [12-13] In addition, 
failures of the propulsion system typically lead to a hard failure — that is, a major degradation of spacecraft 
functionality that often lead to loss of mission — contrasted with soft failures (e.g., failure of a reaction wheel 
or gyro) that do not directly lead to loss of mission. [14] Thus it is imperative that new small spacecraft EP 
technologies and hardware undergo sufficient qualification activities to reduce these failures. Additional 
investigation of secondary payload and small spacecraft failures have noted that these failures have been 
disproportionately experienced as “infant mortality” failures and encountered by relatively inexperienced 
developers. [15-16] Speculation for these differences point out lack of best practices in design and qualification, 
inadequate testing, and inherent small mass / volume constraints among potential driving causes. 

The main question addressed in this paper, then, is what minimum set of qualification activities are 
necessary for a new EP device developed with an eye for NASA cat3/classD missions (hereafter referred to as 
“Class-D” missions), such that a minimum body of evidence exists to assess a technology’s readiness and 
appropriateness for a given mission. The guidelines outlined later in the paper are recommendations to avoid 
recurring issues and common pitfalls based on past experience in qualifying electric propulsion subsystems for 
spaceflight. It should be emphasized that these recommendations are merely a minimum floor — that is, if not 
met, then there may be red flags that indicate inadequate technology maturity and readiness level. Beyond 
these recommendations, further requirements and delta qualifications will almost certainly be levied by the 
mission and will need to be accounted for. In addition, it would be expected that other lower-level engineering 
development testing and analyses have already been performed prior to implementing the activities 
recommended here. 

NASA evaluators for secondary payloads with EP have two main areas of concern. First and foremost is to 
minimize the risk of impacting the primary exploration or science mission via a propulsion loss of control, 
plume impingement or contamination, and end-of-life disposal (with respect to any orbital debris and/or 
planetary protection requirements). The second goal — which is also shared by the secondary payload mission 
primary investigator (PI) and spacecraft bus vendor / integrator — is to have a reasonable expectation of 
accomplishing the mission objectives of the secondary payload. This entails ensuring sufficient device life and 
performance while minimizing negative interactions with the rest of the small spacecraft. On the other hand, in 
the case of a technology push, rather than mission pull as described above, these guidelines provide a minimum 
level of expectation for EP technology developers to appropriately plan for subsystem qualification for a given 
NASA Class-D mission. 

This paper, with a preliminary set of recommended guidelines and best practices, is intended to initiate a 
dialogue with the smallsat community regarding smallsat EP qualification. Future updates of this paper intends 
to incorporate feedback from other NASA centers, government agencies, industry, and academia to maximize 
utility for the community. 

 
General Qualification Process Overview and Recommendations 

Qualification Entrance Criteria 

An EP subsystem will need to achieve a certain level of development maturity before it should even consider 
undergoing a qualification process. Perhaps one of the most important yet all too often neglected aspect is the 
identification of customers and the establishment of well-formed requirements. Qualification of spaceflight 
hardware is not a one-time event or process, nor does it produce a generic and broadly applicable result. 
Qualification is a very tailored and specific verification of a particular set of requirements provided by an 
identified customer and defined mission. The customers may be a combination of the spacecraft bus integrator, 
the mission principal investigator team, and/or the NASA directorate the mission is provisioned under. The 
requirements will be driven by specific mission objectives, concept of operations, spacecraft integration needs, 
and environmental constraints. Without a given mission and customer, the requirements will necessarily be 
vague or rough estimate placeholders, and portions will likely be insufficient or inapplicable for future 
missions. 

That being said, it is understood for EP subsystem developers and vendors that a certain level of technology 
push is required before it can be considered for a mission pull that can provide specific requirements. A market

survey is strongly recommended to understand the situations and scenarios for which the particular EP 
technology is competitive as a propulsive option, as well as to understand the resulting basic needs (e.g., 
performance, reliability and life, integration, environments, etc.) that need to be addressed. Corresponding 
design reference missions (DRM) can be formulated to generate a set of target requirements to meet to advance 
the relative technology readiness level (TRL) of the given device. [17] However, it should be made very clear 
that even if the hardware is “qualified” to those generic requirements, it is to be expected a fair amount of re-
qualification or delta qualification activities may be required to meet later customer- and mission-specific 
requirements. This holds even for EP subsystems that have successfully flown for one spacecraft or mission; a 
delta qualification process will still in all likelihood be necessary for a different mission. 

 
Recommendation 1: Establishment of customer- and mission-specific requirements is key to advance and 

qualify smallsat EP subsystems. Qualification of hardware to a generic set of requirements will likely require 
substantial additional delta qualification activities for an actual manifested mission. 

 
It is expected prior to commencing subsystem qualification activities that a sufficient level of design 

refinement and development has been already met. This includes meeting basic spaceflight hardware 
guidelines regarding approved and prohibited materials selection, process development, fasteners and welding 
implementation, range safety considerations, and electrical, electronic, and electromechanical (EEE) parts 
derating to name just a few. [18-20] These documents are based on decades of spaceflight hardware experience, 
and common mistakes and potential pitfalls can be averted early in the process by incorporating these practices 
during the transition from a laboratory unit into a more flight-like design and packaging. It should be noted that 
the various standards are just a starting point, and a thorough and careful evaluation of the EP subsystem —
typically with a critical design review (CDR) presented to a panel of experienced personnel including systems 
and safety engineers, mission or spacecraft customers, and other impartial third parties — should be performed 
to ensure the system design is sound for spaceflight and likely to pass the subsequent qualification activities. 
The propulsion technology should also be at TRL 5 (as applied to micro-propulsion systems) to be ready for 
qualification activities. [17]  

One perhaps common pitfall is assuming a successful demonstration flight (i.e., “skipping” the TRL 6 gate to 
reach TRL 7) assures the hardware is qualified for all future flights. While successful operation in space is 
certainly attractive from an advertising standpoint and may even partially buy down some risks, there is no 
guarantee that one successful flight will lead to another. A one-off demonstration, with limited telemetry on 
thruster condition, performance, and life, offers little assurance that the hardware will also be successful for a 
different mission. The purpose of a proper qualification program is to rigorously push the hardware beyond 
the expected imposed limits such that probability of mission success is maximized, even under adverse 
conditions. 

 
Recommendation 2: Ensure the EP subsystem is at a sufficient level of maturity and that all common and basic 

aspects of spaceflight hardware design (e.g., materials and processes, EEE parts, etc.) have been considered and 
accounted for before commencing qualification activities. 

 
Along with prior development and design reviews, successful qualification practices have typically 

implemented some form of qualification planning, reporting, and review processes. Formal deliverable 
documentation of a plan, data package, and reports along with establishment of a standing and independent 
review board to oversee these activities are not strictly necessary within the confines of Class-D missions. [11] 
However, even if informal, these are basic concepts (i.e., have a plan, document methods and results, and invite 
others to review) that go a long way towards ensuring no major gaps or misses are inherent in the qualification 
process. A high-level qualification plan can perhaps be merged into the overall project plan documentation or 
set aside as an informal document. The actual qualification test setup, data collection methods, and processed 
data should be documented in some form as general good test procedures. Reviews of the qualification plan 
and of the qualification status at key milestones can help ensure the qualification process is fundamentally 
sound and provide confidence that the EP subsystem will be able to meet the mission requirements. Some level 
of independent review of the subsystem qualification should be performed to ensure the qualification 
approach, procedures, and results are satisfactory. It is strongly recommended that the mission customers be 
included as reviewers to ensure their needs are being addressed adequately, but valuable insight can be

obtained from additional reviews from the spacecraft bus integrator, EP subject matter experts, and other 
independent peers. 

 
Recommendation 3: Though perhaps not required as formal deliverables, a qualification plan, a qualification 

data package and reports, and independent reviewers are fundamental good practices to include to ensure no gaps 
or misses are present in the overall qualification approach and interpretation of results. 

 
In addition to documentation specific to the qualification process itself, additional documentation is 

typically required of spaceflight hardware — for example, contamination control plan, parts / materials / 
processes, safety, reliability analysis, etc. It is recommended that the mission customer determine which 
content and documents need to be explicitly generated, but general guidance regarding NASA Class-D missions 
is that documentation can be merged and tailored to reduce documentation and configuration management 
burden. [11] While it is acknowledged that documentation can become an onerous process, the practice ensures 
at least some coverage of the multitude of aspects that can potentially endanger a spaceflight mission if not 
considered. 

 
Qualification Philosophy 

Before the details of the specifics of qualification activities can be delved into, a few items regarding an 
overall qualification approach philosophy for the mission and technology should be discussed. For example, 
what qualification strategy will be acceptable for the mission? Typically, two primary approaches have been 
pursued at NASA: prototype vs protoflight qualification hardware and test levels. In NASA parlance, prototype 
is flight-like hardware (but will not be flown) that is tested to full qualification levels and durations while 
protoflight is hardware intended for actual flight and tested to typically qualification levels, but for reduced 
durations. [20-24] As will be a recurring theme throughout this paper, there can be much tailoring involved 
depending on specific mission needs, technology concerns, and cost / schedule constraints. Examples include 
performing qualification tests on engineering models instead of flight production prototypes, or qualifying 
protoflight hardware with reduced levels instead of, or in addition to, reduced durations. While the full 
prototype approach provides the least amount of risk, protoflight approaches may not incur a significant jump 
in the risk posture. [25] It should be noted that this is not a blanket acceptance of protoflight over prototype, 
but provided proper risk management is in place, protoflight qualification may be an acceptable approach for 
many smallsat EP subsystems. For prototype qualification, design differences or late changes between the 
qualification hardware and flight hardware should be avoided. Numerous failures, close call mishaps, and 
lessons learned have resulted from inadequate qualification and verification of late design changes. As much as 
possible, a test like you fly (TLYF) philosophy should be followed, and qualification should be performed on as 
close to flight-like hardware as possible. 

 
Recommendation 4: In general, a protoflight qualification approach may be acceptable for Class-D smallsat 

EP hardware, given proper attention is provided to risk management. For a prototype approach with dedicated 
qualification hardware, it should mimic the flight hardware as much as possible to adhere to TLYF and minimize 
the risk of late design changes. 

 
Qualification can also occur at various levels of assembly ranging from components to subassemblies to 

subsystems up to the spacecraft level. In general, qualification at higher levels of assembly reduce risks of flight 
as all interfaces and integration concerns are included in the qualification activity. However, qualification at 
lower levels of assembly reduce development risk that could be caught much earlier in the process with less 
cost and schedule impact. Qualification can and often does occur across multiple levels of assembly to reduce 
both sets of risk. This paper will not go into a recommendation of qualification activities at lower levels of 
assembly but will instead focus on the minimum floor of qualification for the EP subsystem, which is defined 
here to be generally comprised of the thruster head, power processing unit (PPU) and other electronics, and 
the propellant feed system. 

Qualification activities at the integrated assembly level (i.e., how it will be delivered to the customer) is 
recommended. For tightly integrated EP subsystems, the whole subsystem should be qualified as a unit as the 
impacts of mechanical, thermal, electrical, and other interfaces should not be ignored. For more loosely coupled 
EP subsystems (e.g., if power electronics and feed system are well separated from the thruster and each other), 
then some qualification activities could occur at a sub-assembly level without much additional incurred risk.

Different qualification activities can and should be performed at different levels of assembly as appropriate. 
For example, system performance should always be measured with an end-to-end EP subsystem setup. 
Environmental testing could be performed at the sub-assembly level if the various sub-assemblies are not 
tightly integrated to each other mechanically or thermally. Further specific details will be provided in the 
following sections. In general, the overarching philosophy behind test setups should be a TLYF approach, where 
the setup of the hardware is as close to expected flight integrated and environment configuration as possible. 

 
Recommendation 5: Qualification tests should be conducted at minimum at the integrated assembly level it 

will be delivered to the customer. Tests at earlier levels of assembly will help to reduce the risk of issues and failures 
during qualification of integrated assemblies. 

 
Another aspect of TLYF is the qualification test flow. Typically, a flight unit will first undergo acceptance 

testing, then encounter the most stressing mechanical environments during launch, then experience stressing 
thermal environments after deployment, then need to provide mission performance and life. Other qualification 
tests are usually most applicable after environmental testing while some destructive tests (for a prototype 
approach) are of course applied at the very end. Various inspections, functional checks, and/or performance 
mapping may be performed in between various qualification test entries to ensure the EP subsystem passed 
those steps and to troubleshoot any potential issues that may have arisen.

Recommendation 6: Qualification test flow should follow a flight-like sequence where possible. Inspection 

and functional checks performed between qualification test activities provide feedback and confirmation for each 
step. 

 
Finally, the qualification method for each requirement needs to be determined. Qualification of non-trivial 

requirements are typically addressed via test or analysis approaches. Direct testing to verify a given 
requirement is typically preferred, but in certain cases tests may be impractical (due to limitations of ground 
vacuum facilities) or otherwise resource-constrained, making analysis a more attractive solution. The caveat 
with analyses (and even some tests) is that they can be subject to a number of assumptions and uncertainties 
that are not appropriately addressed. Analyses will also typically require some anchoring or validation to some 
level of experimental data to ensure confidence in the results. Specifics of where test versus analysis may be 
appropriate for smallsat EP qualification are provided in the later sections. 

Qualification by similarity or heritage is in essence making the argument that hardware qualified for another 
flight mission can effectively transfer its qualifications to a different flight mission. Or qualification by similarity 
may be argued for applying the generic qualification from a technology-push versus the likely requalification 
needed to meet the well-defined requirements obtained from a mission-pull. However, it cannot be stated 
strongly enough that there are many nuances involved with the underlying reasoning for qualification by 
similarity [23,24] and these points need to be clearly and adequately met or addressed; otherwise, qualification 
by similarity is not recommended, even for Class-D missions. Any claims of heritage need to be assessed against 
several categories, and even minor deviations from prior design, build, and application only result in partial 
heritage while any major changes essentially lead to no claimable heritage. [26]

Electric Propulsion System-Specific Qualification Recommendations 

Performance 

Performance of the EP subsystem is one of the more salient and evaluated features of technology 
development and qualification, yet there are aspects of system performance and its verification that are often 
glossed over. One of the first items to highlight is that it is system-level performance that missions and 
spacecraft bus vendors are interested in. While it can be very useful to understand the thruster-only or power 
electronics-only performance, it is often insufficient to extrapolate these lower-level values in assuming end 
system performance. This is particularly true regarding efficiency metrics, where the impacts of the power 
electronics and feed system losses on the system-level efficiency are significant. The interfaces and interactions 
between the various subassemblies can lead to additional performance losses and potentially unexpected 
effects. In particular, for many smallsat applications where tight volumetric packaging and mass limitations are 
an issue, these interactions (whether electrical, thermal, fluid, or other) can be exacerbated. At the other 
extreme for relatively separated systems, long harnessing or propellant piping can also introduce unexpected 
interactions via changes in electrical inductance or impedance and fluid flow time constants that impact system 
responsiveness and performance. For all of these reasons, as well as others stated above, it is strongly 
recommended that performance be qualified at the EP subsystem level. A proper fully integrated subsystem 
test will also fulfill a functional end-to-end checkout of the system. 

 
Recommendation 8: Performance of the EP subsystem should be qualified at a subsystem level — with flight-

like integration of the thruster, power electronics, and propellant feed system — to account for potential 
interactions affecting system-level performance. 

 
A rich heritage of EP performance measurement techniques, primarily of the thrust and mass flow rate 

which are then used to calculate the specific impulse (Isp) and efficiency, have been accrued over the years by 
the community. The best practices for these performance measurements have been recently distilled into a 
series of recommendation publications. [27,28] It is acknowledged that these recommendations are focused 



 

7 of 12 

more on the pendulum-style thrust stands and for gaseous propellant flow meters, so some tailoring will be 
required for EP technologies where these methods are not appropriate. However, the focus on proper 
calibration along with consideration and mitigation of sources of error is generally applicable to any approach 
and should be assessed if the direct details of the referenced standards do not apply. 

In addition, it is strongly recommended that thrust and propellant use be measured via direct means instead 
of indirect methods or analysis. Estimations of thrust via measurements made in the plume downstream of the 
thruster are typically inadequate for qualification purposes. If a strong and robust correlation between direct 
thrust measurements and beam parameters is demonstrated and verified, perhaps that may be deemed suitable 
for acceptance testing or later delta-qualification via similarity, but again the recommendation here is that 
performance parameters should be measured by direct means for qualification. 

 
Recommendation 9: Direct measurements of thrust and propellant use should be made for qualification of 

performance parameters. The published EP community recommended practices for thrust and propellant flow rate 
should be adhered to where applicable. Aspects of calibration and proper consideration of sources and corrections 
of error need to be handled rigorously for credible measurements. 

 
Depending on the nature of the technology and its intended application, the specific measures of 

performance may vary. For example, for pulsed thruster technologies, measurement of the impulse bit will be 
of high priority. Variations of steady-state thrust and Isp or the impulse bit over different time scales may also 
be of particular interest. These may include pulse repeatability or discharge instabilities at short time scales, 
changes with thermal conditions over intermediate time scales, and degradation with thruster wear over longer 
time scales. For many technologies and missions, determination of the thrust vector, swirl or roll torque, and 
their transient and steady-state behaviors may also be key parameters to quantify. The measurement methods 
for some of these parameters are still not well established and are an active area of research and development. 
The qualification effort will still need to determine how to best demonstrate performance over these 
parameters as dictated by the mission requirement needs. 

In addition, there are a number of known ground facility effects on EP device performance. Vacuum 
chambers provide limitations on true space-like conditions through a finite background pressure and 
conducting surface interactions. These factors, and perhaps even thermal or gravitational considerations, may 
affect prediction of in-space operation and performance. It is recommended that these factors be considered 
prior to qualification and are well characterized and accounted for, designed out, or otherwise mitigated during 
qualification. 

 
Recommendation 10: All other performance parameters and known or suspected factors that may influence 

them should be characterized during the qualification process as deemed pertinent for the technology and/or 
mission for predictions of in-space operation and over time. These may include, but are not limited to: thrust 
stability, impulse bit repeatability, thrust vector, thermal conditions, facility backpressure, and facility electrical 
interactions. 

 
Reliability and Life 

System reliability and life capability are difficult parameters to fully characterize and qualify. Since there 
are always known and unknown uncertainties inherent in systems affecting failure outcomes, the ideal proper 
approach would be to embark on a large sample size statistical assessment of life and reliability. However, that 
is almost always infeasible in the small-scale, low-volume, high-expense realm of spaceflight hardware. Electric 
propulsion compounds this further by typically requiring duration or throughput requirements that are 
impractical to fully test even once, let alone multiple times. Thus, life and reliability qualification will always 
require a compromise of some nature. 

A failure modes, effects, and analysis (FMEA) or its variations is a common approach in starting to address 
system failures. At its core, the FMEA attempts to explore potential failure mechanisms and life-limiting 
parameters, understand their likelihood and consequences, and generate mitigation options for addressing 
them. Even an informal FMEA (again, third party reviews and feedback are always strongly encouraged to 
prevent blind spots) can help anticipate a number of failure modes and address them via design, operation, or 
alternative mitigation means. It is expected that most of the anticipated failure modes would have been 
characterized and designed out or minimized during the development phase. Life qualification efforts will then 



 

8 of 12 

ensure the flight design can meet its requirements. Again as a reminder, the life requirements can vary 
significantly from mission to mission, and delta qualification will likely be necessary for different mission 
requirements. 

For the thruster, a life or an extended wear test is the most direct approach to addressing these 
requirements. However, as stated above, a single test will not fully demonstrate life, but only provide a single 
data point in the life and reliability distribution. The true strength in a life or wear test is to discover 
unanticipated failure mechanisms or wear rates and to characterize how the failures occur. Assessment of life 
by analysis or modeling alone will not capture these previously unknown effects. Since uncovering new failures 
is undesirable at a qualification stage, preliminary life or wear tests should be done prior to qualification during 
development to understand the primary failure modes and to establish analysis models to project life under 
different conditions. Depending on the configuration and outcome of the development wear testing, 
qualification efforts may require additional life or wear testing or may proceed primarily on analysis results 
(anchored by the prior testing) at this point. Life qualification by test alone is limited by number of data points 
as stated above, but also due to facility interactions such as backsputter that can compound the results of the 
life test. As with other testing, adhering to a TLYF approach as close as possible will produce the most relevant 
results. Further discussion and recommendations on EP thruster life qualification are available in published 
literature. [29] Though the referenced publication is targeted more towards NASA Class A or B missions, most 
of the general guidelines and philosophy behind life qualification are applicable to any level, and ultimately the 
customer and mission will drive the specifics of what is necessary and sufficient. 

Unless the mission required life is substantially below the expected thruster life, a protoflight approach will 
generally be infeasible. A dedicated flight-like qualification prototype unit is the most straightforward, but life 
testing on an engineering unit (not quite flight-like) may still be applicable provided any design differences 
between the engineering unit and flight design have negligible impact on the failure modes. Any design changes 
that have a potential impact on the life or failure mechanisms should have a follow-on life or wear testing to 
ensure the flight design can meet the mission needs. Similarly, qualification for a different mission with new 
life requirements — perhaps with total throughput or cycles, but also considering different throttling profiles 
and mission imposed stresses — will likely necessitate a revision of the life analysis and potentially require a 
delta life or wear test as well. As discussed above, should qualification by similarity from one mission to another 
be pursued, the assessment should ensure all the various nuances involved with similarity arguments are 
sufficiently addressed to the customer. 

 
Recommendation 11: A life or extended wear test should be performed to identify previously unanticipated 

failure modes and to characterize the behavior of the failures for the thruster. Life qualification by combined test 
and analysis is recommended, following the published general guidelines where applicable. 

For PPUs and other electronics, generation of a FMEA and implementation of proper design and selection 
of components is generally suitable for ensuring life and reliability. During the design and development phase, 
selection of radiation-hardened components properly derated for both steady-state and transient conditions 
will contribute the most to electronics life and reliability. Tests while integrated with the rest of the EP 
subsystem, such as during performance qualification, can cover the stressing corners of the operational 
envelope to confirm successful operation across all expected conditions. Specific voltage / temperature margin 
testing can also be performed to specifically assess operation of the electronics at worst-case voltages (with a 
nominal margin) at high and low test temperatures. A burn-in test, also sometimes combined with or partially 
covered by a thermal-vacuum test, is recommended to capture infant mortality issues arising from material or 
workmanship defects. Further qualification by analysis can be performed via a reliability assessment of the 
electronic components and thermal cycle fatigue analysis of critical solder joints as determined necessary by 
the customer. [30]  

Recommendation 12: Power electronics reliability is largely driven by proper component selection, but a 
burn-in test is recommended to catch infant mortality issues. 

Life qualification needs for propellant feed systems can vary dramatically depending on the nature of the 
propellant and feed system. Component-level cyclic life testing of valve actuations may be the most common 
item of concern for gaseous and liquid propellant feed systems. EP technologies utilizing a phase change 
(sublimation or vaporization) of a propellant may need to consider specific thermal and pressure cycling tests. 
Use of solid or liquid propellants may also need to consider tank expulsion testing (e.g., for liquid acquisition 



 

9 of 12 

or propellant management devices, diaphragm or piston tanks, etc.) to ensure total throughput can be reliably 
met. Typical qualification for propellant feed systems and the integrated EP subsystem will also require leak 
testing for all wetted surfaces and proof pressure testing for storage and other sealable sections of the feed 
system. 

Recommendation 13: Life and reliability qualification of propellant feed systems typically should include 
cyclic, leak, and proof pressure testing. 

Interfaces and Interactions 

For flight integration, a number of interfaces between the EP subsystem and the spacecraft need to be 
properly assessed and verified. The thermal interface is an often neglected aspect of spacecraft integration, yet 
can have significant impacts on accommodation and operation. Typically a spacecraft will impose a 
temperature and/or waste heat interface requirement that will need to be verified. The waste heat from PPU 
operation, thermal soak back from thruster operation, or heating for propellant management will need to be 
assessed. If heat rejection to the spacecraft exceeds limits, there will be subsequent impacts to the EP subsystem 
to otherwise handle excess heat or accommodate additional implementation of thermal control schemes. 
Qualification of thermal interface requirements can be verified by analysis and models, where the models are 
anchored to thermal vacuum testing. Implementing flight-like thermal interfaces during thermal vacuum 
testing is recommended to better capture these potential issues. 

Electrical interfaces for EP subsystems are another area of integration concern. The grounding architecture 
of the EP subsystem electrical circuit relative to the spacecraft should be implemented during ground testing 
as much as possible. Any non-flight-like electrical interactions between the thruster, plume, and ground facility 
— which may not be completely avoidable in most configurations — may introduce errors in the 
characterization of system operation, performance, and interactions.  

For the PPU, the input electrical interfaces should be verified to accommodate the spacecraft power buses 
required for operation and their respective voltage ranges, which are often unregulated. Various operational 
response parameters such as maximum and no-load power draws, in-rush currents, and fault handling (e.g., 
under-voltage droop) also need to be verified. For EP subsystems with integrated control software, the 
corresponding interfaces should be fully developed and verified. Algorithms for basic operation (e.g., ignition, 
pulsing, throttling, etc.) need to be tested to ensure repeatable and reliable operation. Any fault detection, 
mitigation steps, system safing, and recovery sequences should be thoroughly vetted. 

 
Recommendation 14: Flight-like thermal, electrical, and control / software interfaces should be implemented 

and monitored during qualification of the EP subsystem to prevent unanticipated integration issues. 
 
Electromagnetic interference (EMI) and compatibility (EMC) tests are standard for most electrical and 

electronic devices. With smallsats, the power level of EP devices will generally be low and perhaps the EMI 
noise will also correspondingly be low, but it is likely the communication systems will also be low power with 
lower link margins available as well. It will need to be determined at a system or spacecraft-level what tests 
will be necessary for which devices. It would be expected that the PPU and other power electronics for the EP 
system will be tested as standard for spaceflight electrical and electronic hardware; however, EMI/EMC testing 
at the EP subsystem-level may need to be specially addressed as that will require unique facilities that can 
provide sufficient pumping speed for thruster operation while not interfering with EMI/EMC measurements. 
[31] The requirements may also need to be specifically evaluated as the EMI noise from EP device operation in 
these special ground facilities can often exceed the standard MIL-STD-461 baselines. This has been observed 
with steady-state operation EP technologies, but special consideration should also be given to pulsed and 
microwave or radio-frequency EP technologies as their electromagnetic noise characteristics can be 
significantly different than steady-state direct current devices. 

 
Recommendation 15: EMI/EMC qualification testing is recommended at a minimum for the PPU and other 

power electronics as is typical for any spaceflight electrical and electronic hardware. Testing at the EP subsystem-
level may be necessary depending on the mission, spacecraft, and nature of the technology. 

 
Though not typically considered a direct part of EP subsystem qualification, characterization of plume 

interactions is a critical aspect of spacecraft integration and mission evaluation. One primary concern is plume 



 

10 of 12 

impingement on spacecraft surfaces leading to either erosive wear or deposition contamination. The deposition 
can be of the propellant itself (particularly condensable or conductive propellants), eroded thruster materials, 
or eroded spacecraft materials. Another item of concern is spacecraft charging and how the plume environment 
affects and is affected by thruster and spacecraft surfaces at various potentials. Finally, measurements and 
characterization of the plume can also provide indirect estimates of thruster performance parameters, thrust 
vector, and any impingement-imparted momentum fluxes. 

Use of witness plates and other contamination or erosion sensors (e.g., quartz crystal microbalances) can 
provide data on plume impacts with specific materials, surfaces, and geometries. Plume modeling analyses will 
likely require measures of the plasma parameters themselves. With respect to sputtering and deposition, 
characterization of the fluxes, energies, and incidence angles for each ion species will be important to project 
plume interactions to future spacecraft and mission designs. For spacecraft charging, the various plasma 
currents, potentials, and temperatures will need to be known to estimate how the plume interacts electrically 
with the spacecraft and/or ground facility. Various standards for EP plume plasma parameter measurements 
are available to guide best practices in making these measurements. [32] 

As noted above for other parameters, facility effects will also impact the plume behavior and characteristics, 
and care should be taken to translate ground measurements to in-space predictions. Facility backsputter 
contamination can affect erosion and deposition measurements. Charge exchange collisions can be significantly 
enhanced in ground vacuum chambers, and facility electrical interactions can also strongly affect the nature of 
the plume and the resultant current paths for ions and electrons. Depending on the technology, studies to vary 
the facility pressure or to vary the electrical environment may be needed to generate confidence for in-space 
operation. 

 
Recommendation 16: The plumes of new EP devices should be sufficiently characterized to understand and 

predict spacecraft interactions for system-level integration. Specifics of plume mapping details and materials for 
interaction will need to be derived from the mission and spacecraft customers. 

 
Environments 

Structural and thermal environments are some of the primary concerns for spaceflight hardware 
qualification. The specific environments, along with their levels and extremes, can vary significantly between 
different missions, operations, interfaces, spacecraft, and launch vehicles. Attempting to apply a worst-case 
envelope for all potential scenarios is typically too difficult to meet or even derive. The best approach, as stated 
before, is to obtain specific requirements from a specific mission customer with a known launch vehicle and 
spacecraft bus. Attempting to “qualify” to a generic set of requirements will likely result in unusable results for 
a future mission, necessitating delta-qualification. One pitfall is targeting or modifying a design to meet the 
generic load levels, which is then found insufficient for later realized load levels, necessitating a costly and 
consequential redesign. However, environmental testing to a set of generic levels will be useful at a 
development-level to identify weak points in the hardware design and at least establish an initial level of 
confidence in the hardware’s ability to potentially meet future environments and loads. Testing early and often 
will also help provide inputs and correlation for the structural and thermal models of the hardware. It is worth 
repeating that structural and thermal testing without defined mission-specific environments and loads 
requirements will be insufficient from a qualification perspective. It should also be noted that a prime objective 
of most environmental testing is to anchor the corresponding analysis models, which then need to ensure 
positive margins. Testing alone may be insufficient qualification for some environmental concerns. 

There are a number of potential structural environments that need to be assessed depending on the launch 
vehicle, spacecraft interfaces, and the load path to the EP subsystem. Typical dynamic environments to consider 
are sinusoidal, acoustic, and random vibrations generated from the launch event and shock spectra generated 
from separation events. Sine vibration testing covers low-frequency vibrations generated by launch. Not all 
launch vehicles will have severe sine vibration environments, and this test may be omitted for such missions. 
Acoustic and random vibrations are generated by the interactions of the launch vehicle as it transits through 
the atmosphere. Typically, only one or the other is tested for a given unit or subsystem — whichever one is 
expected to provoke a stronger response for the hardware. Hardware with a large area-to-mass ratio are more 
susceptible to acoustic environments while hardware with a low area-to-mass ratio are impacted more from 
random vibration loads. Most smallsat EP subsystems would be expected to fall under the latter, but this should 
be determined on a case-by-case basis. Shock loads encompass the high-frequency vibrations typically 
generated by the various pyrotechnic devices used to separate stages, fairings, spacecraft, or other deployment 



 

11 of 12 

mechanisms. In addition to the above dynamic loads, there will also be quasi-static loads and an imposed 
pressure profile from the launch environment that may need to be included in the verification. The qualification 
of hardware for launch environments requires a combination of test and analysis, the latter typically with finite-
element analysis software. Outside of launch environments, proper verification of deployment mechanisms 
operation and process is another concern if present.  

Testing for random vibration environments is typically one of the more acknowledged concerns for 
spacecraft qualification, and there is always present the question of what level of load spectra to apply for 
qualification. Despite all of the warnings and caveats discussed earlier in this section, in many instances 
mission-specific load levels are not available for qualification of a technology. There are a few general guidelines 
perhaps available in this case, none of them completely satisfactory, but testing early and often will help identify 
potential weak points in the design to reduce the amount of any anticipated rework during true qualification 
(or delta qualification) activities. Before even loads are applied, a modal survey or sinusoidal sweep can help 
partially validate the dynamic model of the system through identification of resonant frequencies and modes. 
Workmanship levels of vibration provide a minimum screening test to identify any flaws in the design or 
assembly. The Goddard Space Flight Center’s General Environmental Verification Standard (GEVS) also 
provides a set of load spectra at higher levels that can be used without any other information available, though 
they may still be insufficient for specific missions. [33] Mass acceleration curves (MAC) for various launch 
vehicles may provide a better sense of overall load levels, especially for lightweight components and 
assemblies.  

Apart from the primary structural and thermal environments, there may be additional environmental 
concerns. These may include, for example, humidity and contamination environments. Many EP technologies 
and devices have materials, components, or propellants that are susceptible to degradation due to water vapor 
absorption or condensation, or from other chemically-reactive contaminants. Corrosion, also including aspects 
of galvanic and stress corrosion cracking, is another potential issue particularly for seaside launch 
environments. Smallsat devices, due to their smaller nature, may also be especially susceptible to physical (e.g., 
dust particles, debris, etc.) contamination clogging small orifices and passageways. Conductive contamination, 
perhaps from facility backsputter deposition during protoflight or acceptance testing, can degrade electrical 
isolation. Proper contamination control or cleaning processes (both internal and external) may need to be 
considered for these EP subsystems. 

Smallsat missions may encounter longer than anticipated delays to launch as their participation as a 
rideshare is subject to delays from higher-priority launch payloads and issues. As a result, sufficient storage life 
capability may be important to demonstrate. In addition to contamination concerns, propellant leakage or 
stability may also be a point of concern. As a result of these considerable impacts, proper storage, handling, and 
conditioning environments will need to be considered and either tested against or planned for. Qualification 
tests or analyses may be required to ensure these other environmental concerns are not a significant issue. 

 
Conclusions 

This paper proffers a preliminary set of recommended guidelines and best practices in dialogue with the 
smallsat community to strike a better balance between engineering rigor and cost effectiveness for the 
qualification of EP technologies under consideration for NASA small spacecraft. Feedback from the smallsat 
community is highly encouraged to support future updates of this paper.
