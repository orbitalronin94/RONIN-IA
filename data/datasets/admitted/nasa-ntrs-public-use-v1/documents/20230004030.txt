 PASSIVE CONTROLS OF TIP CLEARANCE FLOW IN A TRANSONIC COMPRESSOR 
FOR STALL MARGIN IMPROVEMENT: COMMON FLOW PHYSICS 



 



 



Chunill Hah 
NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 



 



 



ABSTRACT 
Two passive tip clearance control devices for stall margin 



improvement in a transonic compressor are compared in detail. 



The primary objective of the current study is to examine the 



overall performance in terms of stall margin increase and any 



associated performance penalties near design operation with 



axial casing grooves (ACGs) and circumferential casing grooves 



(CCGs). The underlying flow physics of these passive flow 



control devices are examined and compared to identify any 



relative advantages in the development of optimized casing 



treatment designs. 



To understand the underlying flow mechanisms of ACGs and 



CCGs, available measured data and both steady and unsteady 



flow simulations from Large Eddy Simulations are used. The 



measured data illustrate changes in the overall time-averaged 



performance and provide very useful snap shots of the flow fields 



at specific times. The simulated steady (CCGs) and unsteady 



(ACGs) flow fields from LES show changes in the entire flow 



structure, which reveal the underlying flow physics. The current 



LES captures measured changes in the flow field due to the 



applied casing treatments well. Detailed comparisons of the 



calculated flow fields with and without CCGs or ACGs show that 



flow blockage near the casing is reduced by both treatments for 



near-stall operation. This reduction of blockage generation 



extends the compressor stall margin. Therefore, the root fluid 



mechanism of stall margin increase by these casing treatments 



must be the underlying method of reduction of blockage 



generation. 



 Tip leakage flow occurs due to higher pressure on the 



pressure side of the blade than on the suction side at the blade 



tip. The tip leakage flow moves counter to and collides with the 



incoming main flow, creating a tip leakage vortex (TLV). The 



formation of the TLV due to this collision is the main source of 



flow blockage generation. The unsteady flow fields from LES 



near the casing with ACGs show that flow blockage in the tip 



region is periodically reduced when the blade passes under the 



ACGs. Detailed examination of the flows near the casing 



indicates that effective tip leakage flow below the casing is 



reduced by about 26% as some of the tip leakage flow moves into 



the ACGs. Most of the flow into the current ACGs is reinjected 



into the main passage upstream of the leading edge. The reduced 



effective tip leakage flow colliding with the incoming main flow 



produces a much smaller TLV with ACGs. This is the main 



mechanism for the blockage reduction with ACGs. 



With CCGs, part of the tip leakage flow moves into the rear 



portion of the grooves and is reinjected into the main flow near 



the front side of the grooves. The radially reinjected flow from 



CCGs blocks upstream movement of tip leakage flow below the 



casing, resulting in roughly 16% less effective tip clearance flow 



below the casing. Near stall operation, a much smaller TLV is 



formed with ACGs as compared to CCGs due to a larger 



reduction of effective tip leakage flow. With the smaller blockage, 



the overall incoming axial velocity is smaller for the given flow 



rate and larger pressure rise is obtained with increased inlet flow 



angle. 



 The increase in stall margin by ACGs and CCGs is due to 



the reduction of blockage generation. The reduction of the 



blockage generation is due to the decrease of the effective tip 



leakage flow under the casing by ACGs and CCGs. 



It has been believed that casing treatments always incur 



small additional losses near the design condition due to the 



mixing of the flow exiting the grooves and the main flow, as well 



as to an increased wetted area due to the grooves. Near design 



operation, tip leakage flow typically initiates at about 25-30% 



axial chord downstream of the leading edge. ACGs or CCGs 



located near the leading edge barely affect tip leakage flow 



development. Since the current ACGs are located near the 



leading edge, the tip leakage flow development is not affected by 



the ACGs. An additional efficiency penalty is incurred by mixing 



losses near the ACGs. Measurements and LES indicate 1.1% and 



1.4% efficiency penalties due to ACGs at design condition. 



CCGs near the trailing edge pull the TLV toward the blade 



suction side at the design condition, eliminating double leakage 



flow in the current transonic compressor and resulting in higher 



efficiency. The measured efficiency penalty due to the CCGs is 



within the measurement uncertainty and LES shows the same 



result. An integrated arrangement of ACGs and one or two rows 



of CCGs toward the trailing edge could eliminate any efficiency 



penalty near the design condition with a significant increase of 



stall margin.

1. INTRODUCTION 



Various compressor casing treatments have been 



investigated over the past 50 years. The primary objective of 



compressor casing treatments is to increase stall margin without 



reducing efficiency near design operation. Compressor casing 



treatments affect the flow near casing, primarily the tip leakage 



flow. They also, however, affect the flow field over the entire 



span by altering the flow blockage in the passage. Some simple 



forms of casing treatment, like circumferential casing grooves, 



have been successfully installed in production engines. With the 



trend of increasing blade loading and decreasing core 



compressor size, more aggressive compressor casing treatments 



are being pursued for real engine applications. Integrated designs 



of compressor blades and casing treatments could be used to 



achieve higher overall pressure ratios, increased stall margins 



and greater efficiency over the entire operating range in small-



core engine environments. 



Circumferential casing grooves (CCGs) and axial casing 



grooves (ACGs) have been applied to both subsonic and 



transonic compressors. Most fan/compressor casing treatments 



installed in real engines are based on these two types of grooves. 



Although CCGs and ACGs can substantially extend stall margin 



(up to 30-40%) when applied properly, the fundamental flow 



mechanisms are not clearly understood. Consequently, the 



design criteria are still based on loosely connected experimental 



data. 



Earlier studies attempted to understand the flow physics 



behind stall margin increase with casing treatments through 



experimental and analytical approaches (for example, Moore et 



al. [1], Prince et al. [2], Takata and Fujita [3], Paulon and 



Dehoudt [4], Smith and Cumpsty [5], Lee and Greitzer [6], 



Crook et al. [7], Muller et al. [8, 9], Houghton and Day. [10], 



Sakuma et al. [11], and Mileshin et al. [12]). Prince et al. [2] 



reported performance characteristics with several configurations 



of ACGs in a subsonic compressor. The experimental 



investigation of ACGs and CCGs by Takata and Fujita [3] 



implied that tangential momentum from the groove jets to the 



mainstream plays an important role in delaying stall inception. 



While these studies reported significant changes and 



improvements in the entire compressor flow field with ACGs or 



CCGs, details of the flow mechanism behind the changes in stall 



margin and compressor efficiency have not been fully explained. 



Detailed information about the steady/unsteady flow structure 



near the casing has not been available. Crook et al. [7] performed 



a CFD study that concluded that the removal of blockage from 



the main stream and the reduction of the core vortex blockage 



are the main mechanisms of stall margin increase due to casing 



grooves. The momentum balance analysis of Shabbir and 



Adamczyk [13] concluded that radial transport of axial 



momentum in and out of the grooves is the main mechanism. 



Maher et al. [14] reported a very detailed investigation of 



advanced ACGs in a modern single-stage high-pressure 



compressor. The ACG geometry was optimized with unsteady 



Reynolds-averaged Navier Stokes (URANS) simulation. Their 



study indicates that their ACGs increase pressure ratio and stall 



margin across all rotor speeds and operating conditions. Even a 



small increase in efficiency at the design operation was reported 



with their ACGs. 



Although many numerical studies have reported that the 



current generation of Reynolds-averaged Navier Stokes (RANS) 



CFD codes can correctly calculate the stall margin extension by 



CCGs, some recent studies (for example Mileshin et al., [12]) 



report that the effects of CCGs on compressor stall margin are 



not correctly predicted by some RANS-based CFD codes. We 



must understand the fundamental mechanisms of various casing 



treatments to successfully apply any numerical tools to the 



optimization of compressor casing treatments. 



NASA initiated a research program with Johns Hopkins 



University (JHU) to study compressor tip clearance flow and 



casing treatments in JHU’s optically index matched compressor 



test facility. Chen et al. [15] reported the detailed flow structure 



near the casing and inside ACGs using stereo particle image 



velocimetry (SPIV). Hah [16], using detailed Large Eddy 



Simulation (LES), concluded that the stall margin increase with 



ACGs is due to the periodic reduction of flow blockage 



generation when the rotor blade passes under an axial groove. He 



observed that the jet flow exiting an ACG is not the main 



mechanism for stall delay, although it affects overall 



performance by modifying flow incidence at the rotor inlet. All 



previous experimental and analytical studies indicate that 



effective application of compressor casing treatments reduces 



blockage generation near stall, which results in delayed stall 



inception and increase of stall margin. 



One of the lessons from the joint NASA/JHU program is 



that both advanced flow measurement and CFD are 



complementary and should be applied together to investigate 



complex flow phenomena in modern compressors. Advanced 



measurement techniques like SPIV can provide an accurate state 



of the flow in a certain area at a given moment. However, CFD 



can give a picture of the entire unsteady flow field if executed 



properly. 



Previous experimental and numerical studies on various 



casing treatments have shown that decreased flow blockage near 



the casing delays stall inception in modern tip-loaded 



compressors. However, the flow mechanisms behind the 



decrease of the flow blockage for different casing treatments 



have not been explained. The present work aims to uncover the 



flow mechanisms behind flow blockage reduction with 



successful ACGs and CCGs. A clear understanding of the 



fundamental flow mechanism could guide any design 



optimization of advanced casing treatments.

3. NUMERICAL PROCEDURE 
Large eddy simulation was used in the present study to 



explain the flow mechanism associated with CCGs and ACGs. 



Flow fields with CCGs and ACGs were calculated in a single 



stage configuration. The actual stage has 16 rotor blades and 29 



stator blades. For LES, the number of stator blades was scaled to 



32 to save computational resources. Upstream influence of the 



stator on the rotor performance in the current compressor is 



believed to be minor since a relatively large space exists between 



the rotor and the stator. LES was applied to capture unsteady 



flow mixing of the tip leakage flow, flow from CCGs or ACGs, 



and the incoming main flow as accurately as possible. 



Unsteady flow mixing and blockage generation from 



URANS calculations depend on turbulence closure. With 



spatially filtered Navier-Stokes equations, the subgrid-scale 



stress tensor term must be modeled for closure of the governing 



equations. A Smagorinsky-type eddy-viscosity model was used 



for the subgrid stress tensor, with the standard dynamic model 



by Germano et al. [17]. Wall resolutions in the streamwise, 



pitchwise, and spanwise directions are within the ranges Dx+ < 



20, Dy+ <5.0, and Dz+ < 20, respectively. Details of the 



numerical procedures are given by Hah [16]. 



The computational grid for a single blade passage consists 



of 394 nodes in the blade-to-blade direction, 280 in the spanwise 



direction, and 440 in the streamwise direction. For the CCG 



simulation, each circumferential groove is gridded with 60 and 



30 nodes in the radial and axial directions. For the ACG 











 



 4 



simulation, each axial groove was represented with 200 and 30 



nodes in axial and radial directions. A sliding mesh arrangement 



was used to transfer flow properties between the rotor and the 



stationary ACGs. A buffer zone was created between the rotor 



grid and the ACGs to transfer flow properties between the two 



different grid topologies. The inflow boundary is located 6 



average blade spans upstream of the rotor’s leading edge and the 



outflow boundary three blade spans downstream of the trailing 



edge. Since the present investigation focuses flow fields before 



stall, one rotor passage and two stator passages with periodic 



boundary condition were calculated. All the computations were 



performed with NASA’s supercomputing system. 



 



4. OVERALL FLOW STRUCTURE 
Calculated rotor performance characteristics with CCGs and 



ACGs are compared in Fig. 4 along with measured data. 



Uncertainties of the measurements are 0.2% for mass flow 



coefficient, 0.6% for total pressure ratio, and 1.5% for isentropic 



efficiency. For the smooth casing, a slightly higher rise in 



pressure is calculated than the measured value. The measured 



stall point with the smooth casing agrees well with the value 



calculated by LES. In the simulation, the stall mass flow rate was 



determined by increasing the exit reference pressure by small 



increments until a stable converged solution could not be 



obtained. The measurements show that CCGs increase the 



compressor stall margin by almost 7.3%. At the stall point with 



the smooth casing (marked as “A” in Fig. 4), the pressure rise 



with CCGs is about 0.8% higher than without CCGs. When 



ACGs are installed, the measurements show an increase in stall 



margin of over 23% and a 4% higher pressure rise at the smooth 



wall stall condition A. The simulation calculates a slightly higher 



pressure rise than the measurements at the smooth-casing stall 



point for both CCGs and ACGs. Changes in measured adiabatic 



efficiency with the smooth-wall casing are fairly well calculated 



with LES. 



The measurements show a slight decrease in the adiabatic 



compressor efficiency with CCGs near the design condition. 



However, the difference is within the measurement uncertainty. 



The LES calculates almost the same adiabatic efficiency at the 



design condition with the smooth casing and with CCGs. The 



measurements show the maximum efficiency drops by 1.1% 



when ACGs are installed. A slightly higher efficiency penalty 



(-1.4%) was calculated by LES. 



Hah [18] reported a detailed investigation of the flow field 



with CCGs in the current compressor. Detailed flow mechanisms 



for the stall margin increase and change in aerodynamic 



efficiency at the design condition were presented in the report. 



In the current paper, flow mechanisms behind the large increase 



in the stall margin and the pressure rise with the ACGs are first 



identified with the LES simulations. Then relative differences in 



flow mechanisms between ACGs and CCGs are compared to 



identify any relative advantages for future design applications. 



Calculated flow fields with the smooth casing, with CCGs and 



ACGs near the stall point with the smooth wall (Condition “A” 



in Fig. 4) are first examined in detail to identify the flow physics 



detailing how the stall margin is affected by the two passive flow 



control devices. Then, calculated flow fields at the design 



condition (Condition “B” in Fig. 4) are examined in detail to 



explain how any efficiency penalty near the design condition is 



generated with CGGs and ACGs. 



 



5. EFFECTS OF CCGS AND ACGS ON FLOW 
BLOCKAGE AND STALL MARGIN 
Figure 5 compares calculated instantaneous velocity vectors 



at the rotor tip near stall with the smooth casing, CCGs, and 



ACGs. Various previous studies on compressor casing 



treatments have shown that flow blockage near the casing is 



reduced when a successful casing treatment is applied. The 



results in Fig. 5 show that the passage is almost blocked near the 



pressure side of the blade with the smooth casing. The flow is 



about to spill over into the next blade passage. With CCGs and 



ACGs, the velocity vectors show that flow blockage does not 



reach to the pressure side of the blade and significant space is 



available for extra flow. With the smooth wall, the tip leakage 



vortex stands almost on the leading-edge plane. With CCGs and 



ACGs, the tip leakage vortex stays well inside the blade passage 



and the mass flow rate can be further reduced before stall 



inception. 



Mueller et al. [9] reported a detailed flow measurement on 



the current compressor with smooth casing and CCGs using 



stereo particle image velocimetry. Figure 6 compares measured 



relative Mach number distributions from that study for the 



smooth casing at 95% span and for CCGs at 92.5% span. For the 



smooth casing at 95% span, two-dimensional particle image 



velocimetry (PIV) was used. For the CCGs at 92.5% span, stereo 



PIV was used to measure all three velocity components. In the 



latter case, the laser sheet was restricted to lie at 92.5% span due 



to the casing geometry. Details of the system setup, the particles 



and measurement uncertainties are given by Voges et al. [19]. 



Flow blockage at these locations is indicated by isolines of Mach 



number (Ma) = 0.8. Figure 6 clearly shows that flow blockage is 



significantly reduced when CCGs are installed on the casing. 



Calculated relative Mach number distributions for the same 



cases as in Fig. 6, as well as with ACGs at 92.5% span, are 



compared in Fig. 7. The measured flow blockages for the smooth 



casing and with CCGs are well reproduced by LES (again, 



indicated by isolines of Ma = 0.8) in Fig. 7. The LES calculates 



an even greater reduction in flow blockage with ACGs than with 



CCGs. Previous studies on compressor casing treatments (for 



example, Shabbir and Adamczyk [13], Hah [16]) have shown 



that flow blockage generation near the rotor tip is reduced when 



a successful casing treatment is applied. Therefore, the driving 



flow mechanism for compressor casing treatment effectiveness 



is a flow mechanism that changes the generation of flow 



blockage near the rotor tip.

6. CHANGES IN FLOW BLOCKAGE GENERATION 



NEAR THE CASING WITH CCGS AND ACGS 
In the following, the simulated flow fields will be inspected 



at selected locations. Two of these locations are illustrated in Fig. 



8, a radially inward view of the compressor blade row. The lines 



denoted 1 and 2 in Fig. 8 represent meridional planes extending 



in the streamwise and radial directions. Plane 1 is located at 33% 



axial chord downstream from the leading edge where the tip 



leakage vortex has already developed near stall (condition A in 



Fig. 4). As shown in Fig. 8, the blade tip and front side of the 



second CCG intersect at this location. Also, the overlap of the 



ACG and the blade ends at this location. Plane 2 is located at the 



mid-chord of the blade tip. As shown in Fig. 8, the blade tip and 



the third CCG intersect near this location. At the design 



condition, the tip leakage vortex start to develop between planes 



1 and 2 and the first and the second CCGs have no interaction 



with the tip leakage vortex. Plane 2 is located just downstream 



of the ACGs. 



Figure 9 shows time-averaged velocity vectors and vorticity 



on Plane 1 near stall for the smooth casing. Only every third 



vector is shown for clarity. Tip leakage flow arises from the 



pressure difference across the blade tip. This tip leakage flow, 



which travels against the incoming main flow, collides with the 



incoming main flow creating a TLV and subsequent flow 



blockage generation. The size of the flow blockage generation 



from this collision between the incoming main flow and the tip 



leakage flow depends directly on the size of the tip leakage flow 



and its axial momentum before it collides with the incoming 



main flow. The center of the tip leakage vortex is located away 



from the blade suction side and radially below the rotor tip 



section. Instantaneous flow structures from PIV measurements 



and LES simulations (Hah [20]) have shown that the tip leakage 



vortex is not fixed in space and oscillates constantly, resulting in 



further blockage generation. 



 



 



FIGURE 8: LOCATIONS OF MERIDIONAL PLANES FOR 



FLOW FIELD INVESTIGATION 



 



When CCGs are employed, the flow structure in the tip 



region changes drastically. Figure 10 shows time-averaged 



velocity vectors and vorticity on Plane 1 with CCGs. Above the 



rotor blade tip section, part of the tip leakage flow moves up into 



the first groove and is later injected back into the main flow. This 



reduces the total tip leakage flow below the casing before it 



collides with the incoming main flow and deflects the tip leakage 



flow towards the hub. Consequently, a much smaller TLV with 



less flow blockage forms closer to the blade suction side and 



closer to the casing compared to the smooth casing case. The 



radially inward jet emerging from the first groove blocks tip 



leakage flow below the casing from going further upstream. This 



interaction reduces the overall tip leakage flow below the casing. 



This smaller tip leakage flow interacts with the incoming main 



flow, creating a smaller area of low momentum fluid and less 



blockage generation.

7. CHANGE IN MASS FLOW THROUGH TIP GAP DUE 



TO CCGS AND ACGS 
Axial distributions of calculated time-averaged tip leakage 



mass flow rates below the casing near stall (condition “A” in Fig. 



4) are compared in Fig. 16. The axial locations of the CCGs and 



ACGs are also indicated in Fig. 16. The total tip leakage mass 



flow below the casing is reduced by 16% with CCGs and by 



26% with ACGs before colliding with the incoming main flow. 



With CCGs, the reduction of the tip gap mass flow rate occurs in 



the area directly below the CCGs. Figure 16 shows that reduction 



of the tip gap mass flow rate occurs below each of the six CCGs. 



For the ACGs, a large reduction in tip gap mass flow occurs near 



the leading edge where the ACG overlaps with the blade (33% 



of axial chord). With ACGs, the overall level of tip leakage flow 



is lower across the blade passage except near the trailing edge. 



Copenhaver et al. [21] showed that in a swept transonic axial 



compressor the stall margin can increase by about 10% when the 



tip gap is decreased by 0.27% of the tip axial chord. Reduction 



of tip gap mass flow rate by CCGs or ACGs influences the 



compressor stall margin in a way similar to reducing tip gap size 



with a smooth casing.

Velocity vectors in Fig. 10 indicate a strong radial velocity 



component in the flow reinjected from the CCGs into the main 



passage, blocking the tip gap flow from moving further 



upstream. This effect contributes to the reduction of tip gap mass 



flow rate near the CCGs. On the other hand, with ACGs, a large 



portion of the tip leakage flow enters the rear section of the 



ACGs and is carried to the front of the ACGs before being 



reinjected into the main-stream well ahead the blade passage, as 



shown in Fig. 12. With ACGs, low momentum tip leakage flow 



moves out of the blade passage, which results in a larger 



reduction of flow blockage compared to CCGs. 



The flow field near the compressor casing with CCGs or 



ACGs becomes very complex as reinjected flow interacts with 



the flow below the casing, and various flow mechanisms are 



involved. However, the primary driver of the decreased blockage 



generation in the rotor tip region with CCGs or ACGs is the 



reduction of total tip leakage mass flow rate under the casing 



before it collides with the incoming main flow. The primary flow 



mechanism for the stall margin increase is the same for these two 



passive flow control devices. 



 



8. EFFECTS OF CCGS ON EFFICIENCY AT DESIGN 
CONDITION 
The primary objective of compressor casing treatments is to 



extend stall margin without incurring any efficiency penalty at 



high flow rates near the design condition. It has been thought that 



all compressor casing treatments incur additional aerodynamic 



losses near design operation. However, some recent 



measurements in high-speed axial compressors show that certain 



CCG designs can even increase compressor efficiency by 1% to 



2% at the design flow condition (for example, Mueller et al [8]), 



Mileshin et al [12]). 



Both the measurements and LES simulations reported in this 



paper indicate a small adiabatic efficiency penalty due to CCGs 



for the current compressor at the design condition (-0.5% 



and -0.3%). Mueller et al. [9] reported a 1.1% efficiency drop 











 



 11 



with the current ACGs at the design condition. LES simulation 



indicates a similar efficiency penalty for the current ACGs 



(1.4%). The measured efficiency penalty with CCGs is relatively 



small and appears to be within the measurement uncertainty. 



However, the measured efficiency penalty with the ACGs is well 



outside of measurement uncertainty. 



Figure 17 compares measured and simulated time-averaged 



static pressure distributions on the casing for the smooth casing 



case. LES calculates the detailed flow structure very well at the 



design condition. The TLV starts to develop near 25% chord, and 



the static pressure in the blade passage starts to increase near 



40% of the axial chord. Therefore, the first two CCGs do not 



directly affect the TLV development at the design condition. The 



overlap of the current ACGs with the blade passage ends at 33% 



of the axial blade chord at the tip. Consequently, the tip leakage 



vortex at the design condition does not interact at all with the 



ACGs. 



Figure 18 compares computed time-averaged contours of 



radial velocity at design condition with CCGs and ACGs at the 



entrance to the grooves. For the CCGs, areas with large radial 



velocity are located near the third, and fourth grooves. Similar to 



the near stall condition in Fig. 11, significant radial movement 



of tip leakage flow occurs only near the blade surfaces with 



CCGs. The large radial velocity at the front part of these grooves 



means that the reinjected flow from these grooves blocks 



upstream movement of tip leakage flow below the casing, like at 



the near stall condition. Also like at the near stall condition, the 



flow blockage generation is decreased and the tip leakage vortex 



is pulled toward to the blade suction side by the CCGs. For the 



ACGs, very little radial flow is seen in Fig. 18, which indicates 



ACGs add additional friction losses by the small amount of flow 



going through the ACGs. 



Calculated time-averaged tip leakage mass flow rates below 



the casing (before colliding with the incoming flow) at the design 



condition are compared in Fig. 19. The total tip leakage mass 



flow rate is about the same with the smooth wall and ACGs. The 



total tip leakage mass flow rate is reduced by about 10% with 



CCGs. The tip leakage mass flow rate decreases near CCG 



grooves located toward the blade trailing edge. The reduction of 



the tip leakage flow with the CCGs results in less blockage 



generation and pulling of the tip leakage vortex toward the blade 



suction side. 



Figure 20 compares computed entropy contours at the rotor 



tip section at the design condition with smooth casing, CCGs, 



and ACGs. Additional entropy generation due to double leakage 



tip clearance flow with the smooth casing is seen in Fig. 20. With 



CCGs, the TLV is pulled toward the suction side of the blade, 



eliminating the double leakage tip clearance flow and the 



associated additional loss. Flow mixing between reinjected flow 



from the CCGs and the main passage flow creates additional 



loss. Depending on the blade loading at the design condition, the 



removal of double leakage tip clearance flow can compensate for 



any additional mixing losses between the main flow and flow 



from the CCGs, possibly resulting in an efficiency gain at the 



design condition due to the use of CCGs. With the ACGs, the 



development of entropy is very similar to that with CCGs up to 



about 60% of axial chord. However, the tip leakage vortex spills 



over into the adjacent blade passage resulting in additional loss 



with a double leakage tip vortex.

9. CONCLUSION 
The flow mechanisms underlying the stall margin increase 



caused by two passive control devices (CCGs and ACGs) were 



studied in detail with measured data and LES simulations. The 



following are major findings: 



1. Flow mechanism for stall margin increase with CCGs: 



In the vicinity of the blade tip surfaces, part of the tip 



leakage flow moves into the grooves and is later 



reinjected into the main passage. This reinjection 



occurs mostly near the front part of the grooves. The 



reinjected flow blocks upstream movement of tip 



leakage flow below the casing, reducing the overall tip 



leakage mass flow rate and total axial momentum 



below the casing before it collides with the incoming 



main flow. For the current compressor, the overall tip 



leakage mass flow rate below the casing is reduced by 



16% compared to the smooth casing case. This 



reduction of tip gap mass flow has an effect on the 



stall margin similar to that from a reduction in tip gap 



size. The reduction of overall tip gap mass flow below 



the casing is the primary mechanism by which CCGs 



decrease blockage generation and increase compressor 



stall margin. 



2. Flow mechanism for stall margin increase with ACGs: 



Near the rear part of ACGs, where the ACGs overlap 



with the blade passage, part of the tip leakage flow 



moves into grooves and is later reinjected back into 



the passage. The reinjection mostly occurs near the 



front of the grooves which is located well ahead of 



blade passage. This recirculation of tip leakage flow 



occurs in all ACGs due to the pressure increase along 



the blade passage. Therefore, a large portion of the tip 



leakage flow is removed from the blade passage and 



reinjected upstream. Like with CCGs, the overall tip 



leakage flow below the casing is reduced by 26% in 



the current compressor. This is the main mechanism 



for the reduction in blockage generation and the stall 



margin increase. The reduction of flow blockage 



generation with ACGs is much greater than that with 



CCGs. With the large reduction of flow blockage, the 



effective blade passage area increases, resulting in 



lower axial velocity at the inlet across the entire blade 



span. This is why total pressure rise is higher with 



ACGs than with the smooth wall or with CCGs. 



• The flow mechanisms behind the stall margin increase 



are the same for CCGs and ACGs. Flow blockage near 



the compressor casing is mostly generated when the 



tip leakage flow collides with the incoming main flow 



near the casing. Both CCGs and ACGs reduce the tip 



leakage flow below the casing before it collides with 



the main flow, which is the main flow mechanism of 



the stall margin increase. 



• High blade loading at the rotor tip is common in 



modern compressor designs, and double tip leakage 



flow is commonly observed. The tip leakage vortex 



initiates downstream of the blade leading edge (at 



25-30% axial chord) at the design condition for the 



current compressor. CCGs located further downstream 



pull the TLV toward the suction side of the blade. This 



eliminates double leakage tip clearance flow and the 



associated efficiency loss. For some compressors this 



removal of loss generation by the double leakage tip 



clearance flow can be larger than the inherent 



additional mixing loss by CCGs. The current ACGs 



have very limited influence on the development of the 



tip leakage vortex at design condition since they 



overhang only the front part of the blade passage. The 



current ACGs incur an efficiency penalty of around 



1% at the near-design condition for the current 



compressor.
