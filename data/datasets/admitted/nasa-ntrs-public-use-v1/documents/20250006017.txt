Icing Performance Degradation of an eVTOL 



Propeller at the NASA Glenn Icing Research 



Tunnel 



Paul H. von Hardenberg1 and Curtis A. Flack2 
NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, 44135, United States 



In March 2023, a 10-day test entry was conducted at the NASA Glenn Icing Research 



Tunnel (IRT) to study ice accretion on an eVTOL propeller. The primary objective was to 



collect ice accretion data to support the development of NASA’s latest ice accretion solver, 



GlennICE. Various parametric sweeps of cloud and operating conditions were performed to 



evaluate the sensitivity of the parameters on the resulting ice accretion, the results of which 



have been published previously. A subsequent 5-day test entry was carried out in January 



2024, focusing on gathering experimental data related to ice shedding and exploring 



conditional icing scaling methods for propellers. Throughout both test campaigns, propeller 



performance data was recorded in the form of torque rise over time. The purpose of this paper 



is to present a subset of the performance data acquired during these tests and provide a 



discussion of the key findings.

II. Introduction 



A wide range of electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft concepts have been proposed for 



Advanced Air Mobility (AAM) operations due to their potential to have lower noise, reduced operating costs, and 



decreased emissions when compared to conventional VTOL aircraft, such as helicopters. Despite the diversity in 



eVTOL designs, there are certain characteristics which are shared by many. For example, eVTOL concepts typically 



have distributed electric propulsion systems where thrust generation is distributed across several electrically 



powered rotors. To reduce their acoustic signature, eVTOL rotors generally operate at much slower tip speeds and 



have higher solidities than conventional helicopter rotors [1]. In addition, many eVTOL concepts have vectored 



thrust capabilities where their rotors act as propellers during the cruise phase of flight. An example of an eVTOL 



aircraft with vectored thrust capabilities is the NASA tiltwing reference vehicle shown in Fig. 1 [2]. 



 



Fig. 1 Renderings of NASA’s Tiltwing Reference Vehicle in a Cruise (a) and Hover (b) Configuration 



 As with all aircraft, icing presents a significant challenge to the safe operation of eVTOL aircraft. Ice can form on 



lifting surfaces and propulsors which leads to a reduction in lift and thrust, and an increase in drag and stall speed of 



the vehicle. However, due to the novelty of eVTOL aircraft, the scope and severity of these effects is not as well 



understood as for conventional aircraft. In addition, eVTOL aircraft presents several unique challenges. For example, 



the large number of rotors and limited power capacity of eVTOL aircraft prohibits the use of conventional ice 



protection systems (IPS). As a result, most eVTOL manufacturers are currently not seeking certification for flight into 



known icing. However, they will still likely be required to demonstrate safe operation in icing for a limited duration 



in the event of an inadvertent icing encounter. Thus, the ability to accurately predict and understand the impact of 



icing on their performance is still of great importance. 



 To investigate the icing phenomenon relevant to eVTOL aircraft, NASA Glenn Research Center developed a 



general-purpose propeller test stand for conducting fundamental icing research on electrically driven propellers in the 



Icing Research Tunnel (IRT). A 10-day test entry in the IRT was completed in March 2023, with the goal of generating 



ice accretions on a non-proprietary propeller geometry under well-characterized conditions [3]. The data from this test 



is currently being used to support the development of NASA’s latest ice accretion solver, GlennICE [4-6]. Three 



carbon fiber propellers of diameters 0.610, 0.711, and 0.914 m (24, 28, and 36 in) were utilized during the test. A 



follow-on test lasting 5 days was later completed in January 2024, which focused on gathering experimental ice 



shedding data as well as evaluating conditional icing scaling techniques for propellers [7,8]. Propeller performance 



degradation in the form of torque change over time was captured during both test entries. This paper provides an 



analysis and discussion of a subset of the propeller performance data that was acquired during these tests. 



 



 











III. Test Article and Instrumentation 



A. Propeller Test Rig 



 A picture of the propeller test rig installed in the IRT test section with the 0.914 m (36 in) diameter propeller is 



shown in Fig. 2. The propeller is driven by an EMRAX 188 permanent magnet synchronous motor with a continuous 



power rating of up to 34 kW (45 hp) and a continuous torque rating of up to 52 Nm (38 lbf-ft). The motor has an IP65 



ingress protection rating and is liquid cooled to prevent overheating. The propeller hub is mounted directly to the 



motor via an adapter. To measure propeller forces and moments, the propeller rig is compatible with any of the 



Interface 6A68 6-axis load cells which can be mounted directly aft of the motor as shown in Fig. 3. Only the Interface 



6A68C load cell was available during the 2023 test. However, the 6A68C load cell was only used during the dry (non-



icing) portion of the test to acquire baseline propeller performance data due to concerns that the imbalance loads 



caused by ice shedding could exceed the structural strength of the load cell. The higher capacity, 6A68E load cell was 



later obtained and used throughout the 2024 test entry to measure forces and moments during the icing encounter. The 



motor speed is controlled by sending commands to a Cascadia Motion CM200DX Inverter. The inverter also provides 



estimates of motor power, temperature, and torque.

B. Propeller Geometry 



Three carbon fiber propellers of diameters 0.610, 0.711, and 0.914 m (24, 28, and 36 in) were custom-made using 



a non-proprietary propeller geometry to maximize data dissemination. All three propellers had a NACA 0012 airfoil 



profile. The twist and chord distributions for the three propellers are shown in Fig. 4. The propeller geometry was 



inspired by the Computationally Optimized Proprotor (COPR) design developed by NASA [9]. However, some 



deviations from the original COPR design were required for manufacturing feasibility purposes. The twist and chord 



distributions provided in Fig. 4 represent the final design after the necessary modifications were made. 



 



 



(a) Chord Distributions (b) Twist Angle Distributions 



Fig. 4 Comparisons of the propeller chord (a) and twist angle (b) distributions between the three propeller

It can be seen from Fig. 4 that the three propellers have similar normalized twist and chord distributions from a 



radial location of approximately 𝑟/𝑅 = 0.40 to 𝑟/𝑅 = 1.0. However, the use of the same hub and spinner for each set 



of propeller blades prevented geometric similarity towards the root of the blade. In the complimentary paper by Rigby 



and von Hardenberg, the effect of not geometrically scaling the spinner with propeller size was explored using 



GlennICE and found to have a negligible effect on the predicted ice accretion [6]. 



A 4-bladed, variable pitch hub was chosen for this test entry to provide greater flexibility in propeller operating 



conditions. A pitch-adjustment tool was provided by the propeller manufacturer to change the collective pitch of the 



blades. For both test entries, the target propeller pitch was 18° higher than the design twist distributions shown in Fig. 



4b. After evaluating the laser scans of the propeller blades obtained during the test, it was determined that the blade 



pitch angle could deviate as much as one degree from the target pitch setting. Future tests will utilize a calibrated 



inclinometer to provide more consistent pitch settings. 



IV. Test Procedure 



A. Spinner Drag Characterization 



 To accurately determine the thrust generated by the propeller from the load cell measurements, it is important to 



account for additional forces that may influence the net load measured by the load cell. In this case, the drag force 



acting on the spinner was one such force that contributed to the net axial load measured by the load cell. To account 



for this, the spinner drag was characterized by conducting a sweep of tunnel speed with the spinner installed on the 



test stand without the propeller blades. During the tunnel speed sweep, the motor was exercised at a speed of 1000 



RPM. Once the drag on the spinner was characterized, the thrust produced by the propeller could be determined by 



subtracting the spinner drag force from the net axial load measured by the load cell. The results of the spinner drag 



characterization are shown in Fig. 5.

Predictions 



 Recall from Section III that an estimate of the propeller torque could be acquired from two independent sources: 



1) from the load cell measured moment about the propeller axis of rotation and 2) from the inverter estimate of motor 



torque based on the motor rotational speed and power. As such, the propeller performance charts include the calculated 



torque coefficient and resulting efficiency using both sources of propeller torque. As can be seen from Fig. 6c, the 



torque measured by the load cell is very similar to the torque estimate provided by the inverter, albeit slightly lower 



at the higher advance ratios. Given the general good agreement between the load cell and inverter estimate of motor 



torque, it was decided to utilize the inverter estimate of torque for the remainder of testing. This approach offers a 



significant advantage over using the load cell, as the inverter estimate of motor torque is not affected by thermal drift, 



creep, and hysteresis. As a result, the inverter estimate of torque was found to be much more consistent and repeatable 



than the torque measured by the load cell. In addition, significant time was saved by not having to thermal soak the 



load cell prior to each run. As such, only the inverter estimate of motor torque is presented herein.

B. Icing Performance Characterization 



 During a typical icing run, the propeller was operated at the target RPM and tunnel speed for several minutes until 



the freestream total air temperature, 𝑡∞,0, reached a steady state at the target temperature for that run. After achieving 



steady state conditions, the propeller was operated for 30 seconds before introducing the icing cloud to acquire baseline 



performance data. The icing spray would continue for the target duration of that run or until a major shedding event 



resulted in vibrations that exceeded their predefined limit. If a major shedding event occurred, the tunnel operators 



would immediately turn off the cloud and begin the shutdown procedure. A linear curve fit was then applied to the 



performance data from spray-on until spray-off, or until the 1st shedding event (if applicable), whichever occurred 



first. Figure 8 shows a typical time history plot showing the rise in propeller torque for a run which includes a shedding 



event around the 7-minute mark.

B. Effect of LWC 



Figure 10a shows the effect of increasing the Liquid Water Content (LWC) from 0.55 to 1.20 g/m3 on the propeller 



torque for runs RA3683 and RA3684, respectively. The spray time for the 1.20 g/m3 run was reduced to match the 



accumulation parameter, 𝐴𝑐, of the 0.55 g/m3 condition. All other conditions were identical as shown in Table 4. Based 



on the analytical calculations of the freezing potential, 𝑛0
∗ , it was believed that the temperature was sufficiently cold 



to produce rime ice along the entire span of the propeller for both the 0.55 and 1.20 g/m3 condition. However, analysis 



of the ice shapes following the test revealed that the freezing fractions for the 1.20 g/m3 case were slightly less than 



one. Based on this observation, and the results of subsequent temperature sweeps, it is believed that the freezing 



fractions achieved during testing were approximately 20% lower than the analytically predicted freezing potentials 



reported in Table 4. As a result, the freezing fraction for run RA3684 may have been closer to 𝑛𝑐 = 1.19*0.8 = 0.95 



at 𝑟 𝑅⁄ = 0.75 where 𝑛𝑐 is the corrected freezing fraction. The reason for the large discrepancy between the 



experimentally achieved freezing fractions and the analytical freezing potentials is still being investigated. 



Nonetheless, a comparison of the propeller torque requirements between these two conditions is presented below. 



 As expected, the 1.20 g/m3 condition led to a more rapid increase in torque than the 0.55 g/m3 condition due to the 



faster rate of ice accretion on the propeller. However, by plotting the change in torque as a function of the product of 



the stagnation collection efficiency and accumulation parameter (𝛽0𝐴𝑐) as calculated at 𝑟 𝑅⁄ = 0.75, comparisons of 



the propeller torque can be made at similar stagnation ice thicknesses. This result is shown in Fig. 10b. Note that, in 



Fig. 10b, the propeller torque is presented in non-dimensional form as the torque coefficient divided by solidity 



(𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄ ). As seen from Fig. 10b, while the change in 𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄ for the 0.55 and 1.20 g/m3 LWC conditions are similar, the 



1.20 g/m3 condition still shows a slightly higher 𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄ for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐. This is likely due to the 1.20 g/m3 condition 



having a freezing fraction slightly less than one as explained in the previous paragraph, resulting in more glaze-like 



features along the stagnation line of the propeller. It is likely that even better agreement could have been achieved had 



the temperature for the 1.20 g/m3 condition been reduced further to achieve a freezing fraction of one. The ice shapes 



obtained at the 𝑟 𝑅⁄ = 0.75 location for these runs are provided in Fig. 10b. Additional ice shapes for runs RA3683 



and RA3684 at other radial locations are presented in Ref. 3.

C. Effect of MVD 



 Figure 12a shows the effect of increasing the MVD from 15 to 80 µm on the propeller torque for runs RA3711 and 



RA3712, respectively. All other conditions for runs RA3711 and RA3712 were identical as shown in Table 6. A 



freestream total temperature of -15°C was chosen to achieve a freezing fraction of 𝑛0 = 1 for both conditions, which 



is consistent with experimental observations and analysis of the ice shapes. As a result of increasing the MVD from 



15 to 80 µm, the 𝛽0 at 𝑟 𝑅⁄ = 0.75 increased from 0.88 to 0.99. 



 As seen from Fig. 12a, the higher MVD condition resulted in a slightly faster rise in propeller torque. This is likely 



due to the higher collection efficiency of the 80 µm case, which results in a faster rate of ice accumulation (from the 



product 𝛽0𝐴𝑐) as well as increased impingement limits on the propeller. To make comparisons of the two MVD cases 



at similar stagnation ice thicknesses, the non-dimensional change in torque is plotted as a function of 𝛽0𝐴𝑐, as shown 



in Fig. 12b. As can be seen from Fig. 12b, the higher MVD condition requires more torque even at similar values of 



𝛽0𝐴𝑐. The difference in 𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄ between the 15 and 80 µm MVD condition for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐 can likely be attributed to 



the difference in impingement limits at these two conditions, as evidenced by the ice shapes at 𝑟 𝑅⁄ = 0.75 provided 



in Fig. 12b. This effect is especially noticeable on the pressure side of the airfoil, where the main ice shape of the 80 



µm condition extends significantly further aft on the airfoil. Additional ice shapes for runs RA3711 and RA3712 at

D. Effect of Temperature 



 The effect of air temperature on the propeller torque requirements were investigated at freestream total air 



temperatures ranging from -3 to -18°C. Air temperature is an important parameter in icing as it has a strong influence 



on the freezing fraction and, consequently, the resulting type and shape of the ice accretion. Figure 13a shows the 



change in propeller torque vs time at freestream total temperatures of -3, -6, -9, -15, and -18°C corresponding to runs 



RA3700, RA3692, RA3696, RA3684, and RA3697, respectively. Note that the spray times varied significantly during 



these runs in order to achieve other test objectives unrelated to propeller performance. Except for temperature and 



spray time, all other conditions were identical as shown in Table 7. 



 As seen from Fig. 13a, the propeller torque increases more rapidly as the total air temperature increases from -18 



to -6°C. This is likely caused by the change in the ice morphology during the transition from rime to glaze ice. 



However, when the total air temperature increases further to -3°C, the trend reverses and the propeller torque 



requirements are significantly less than the preceding -6°C case. These results are summarized in Fig. 14 which 



presents the linear curve-fit slopes of the non-dimensional change in torque as a function of the corrected freezing 



fraction at 𝑟 𝑅⁄ = 0.75. Recall that the corrected freezing fractions are 20% lower than the analytical freezing potentials 



provided in Table 7. Note that Fig. 14 includes repeat cases, and a 2nd order polynomial curve fit has been applied to 



the data through the averages of the repeats for a given freezing fraction. Due to the vastly different values of 𝐴𝑐, 



comparing the ice shapes is impractical and thus are omitted from Fig. 13b for these conditions. In addition, ice shapes 



are unavailable for some of the conditions due to ice shedding.

 The results suggest that the required propeller torque in icing is highly dependent on 𝑛0 and, therefore, is highly 



dependent on temperature for conditions when 𝑛0 < 1. When 𝑛0 = 1 is maintained, the ice morphology and resulting 



aerodynamic impact are largely independent of temperature over the temperatures considered. Additionally, under 



rime conditions, the impinging droplets freeze instantly upon impact, resulting in an ice shape that more closely 



follows the original contour of the airfoil. As a result, the performance impact is generally less severe than for glaze 



conditions. For 𝑛0 < 1, the propeller torque requirements initially increase with increasing temperature (decreasing 



𝑛0), and then decreases with increasing temperature below a certain value of 𝑛0. For this study, the peak torque 



requirements were found to occur somewhere between a 𝑛𝑐 of 0.40 and 0.60. More data is needed to fully understand 



why this trend occurs and whether similar trends are present under different propeller operating regimes.

VI. Conclusion 



 An experimental investigation of an eVTOL propeller under simulated icing conditions was conducted at the 



NASA Glenn Icing Research Tunnel. Propeller performance degradation, in the form of torque rise over time, was 



recorded under various cloud and operating conditions. The effect of LWC, MVD, temperature, tip speed, and 



propeller size on the resulting change in torque were investigated and found to align well with the expected trends. 



Several repeat conditions were conducted which found the repeatability of the torque data to be excellent. 



 To aid in the interpretation of the results, the key icing similarity parameters (𝑛0, 𝛽0, 𝐴𝑐, and 𝑊𝑒𝐿) have been 



calculated at the blade 𝑟 𝑅⁄ = 0.75 location. The change in torque was also presented non-dimensionally as the torque 



coefficient divided by solidity (𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄ ) and plotted against the product of the stagnation collection efficiency and 



accumulation parameter (𝛽0𝐴𝑐), as calculated at the 𝑟 𝑅⁄ = 0.75 location. 



 Over the range of conditions tested, it was found that the change in 𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄ under rime conditions (𝑛0 = 1) was 



largely independent of LWC, tip speed, and propeller size for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐 and advance ratio, provided that the 



distribution of 𝑛0 and 𝛽0 are the same or similar. This observation is consistent with the measured ice shapes, which 



revealed that good ice shape similarity was achieved under these conditions. When independently varying 𝛽0 at rime 



conditions, it was found that the higher 𝛽0 condition required more torque for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐, likely due to the negative 



aerodynamic impact of the increased impingement limits. 



 For glaze conditions (𝑛0 < 1), the change in 𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄ was also found to be largely independent of LWC and propeller 



size for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐 and advance ratio over the range of conditions tested, provided that the distributions of 𝑛0, 𝛽0, 



and 𝑊𝑒𝐿 were the same or similar. However, when testing at a different tip speed (and consequently, 𝑊𝑒𝐿), it was 



𝑟 𝑅⁄ = 0.75 











found that the higher tip speed condition resulted in a slightly higher 𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄ for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐. More work is needed to 



understand whether the difference in 𝑊𝑒𝐿 is responsible for this observation or if there are other factors involved. 



 While the results are encouraging for those who wish to apply current icing scaling methods to achieve similarity 



of propeller performance in icing, the present study is limited to a small range of propeller sizes and operating 



conditions. Further work is needed over a larger range of tip speeds, advance ratios, and propeller sizes to provide 



greater confidence in the scaling methodology.
