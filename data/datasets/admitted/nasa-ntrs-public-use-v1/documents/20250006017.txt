<p>Icing Performance Degradation of an eVTOL 
</p>
<p>Propeller at the NASA Glenn Icing Research 
</p>
<p>Tunnel 
</p>
<p>Paul H. von Hardenberg1 and Curtis A. Flack2 
NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, 44135, United States 
</p>
<p>In March 2023, a 10-day test entry was conducted at the NASA Glenn Icing Research 
</p>
<p>Tunnel (IRT) to study ice accretion on an eVTOL propeller. The primary objective was to 
</p>
<p>collect ice accretion data to support the development of NASA’s latest ice accretion solver, 
</p>
<p>GlennICE. Various parametric sweeps of cloud and operating conditions were performed to 
</p>
<p>evaluate the sensitivity of the parameters on the resulting ice accretion, the results of which 
</p>
<p>have been published previously. A subsequent 5-day test entry was carried out in January 
</p>
<p>2024, focusing on gathering experimental data related to ice shedding and exploring 
</p>
<p>conditional icing scaling methods for propellers. Throughout both test campaigns, propeller 
</p>
<p>performance data was recorded in the form of torque rise over time. The purpose of this paper 
</p>
<p>is to present a subset of the performance data acquired during these tests and provide a 
</p>
<p>discussion of the key findings.    
</p>

<p>II. Introduction 
</p>
<p>A wide range of electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft concepts have been proposed for 
</p>
<p>Advanced Air Mobility (AAM) operations due to their potential to have lower noise, reduced operating costs, and 
</p>
<p>decreased emissions when compared to conventional VTOL aircraft, such as helicopters. Despite the diversity in 
</p>
<p>eVTOL designs, there are certain characteristics which are shared by many. For example, eVTOL concepts typically 
</p>
<p>have distributed electric propulsion systems where thrust generation is distributed across several electrically 
</p>
<p>powered rotors. To reduce their acoustic signature, eVTOL rotors generally operate at much slower tip speeds and 
</p>
<p>have higher solidities than conventional helicopter rotors [1]. In addition, many eVTOL concepts have vectored 
</p>
<p>thrust capabilities where their rotors act as propellers during the cruise phase of flight. An example of an eVTOL 
</p>
<p>aircraft with vectored thrust capabilities is the NASA tiltwing reference vehicle shown in Fig. 1 [2]. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fig. 1  Renderings of NASA’s Tiltwing Reference Vehicle in a Cruise (a) and Hover (b) Configuration 
</p>
<p> As with all aircraft, icing presents a significant challenge to the safe operation of eVTOL aircraft. Ice can form on 
</p>
<p>lifting surfaces and propulsors which leads to a reduction in lift and thrust, and an increase in drag and stall speed of 
</p>
<p>the vehicle. However, due to the novelty of eVTOL aircraft, the scope and severity of these effects is not as well 
</p>
<p>understood as for conventional aircraft. In addition, eVTOL aircraft presents several unique challenges. For example, 
</p>
<p>the large number of rotors and limited power capacity of eVTOL aircraft prohibits the use of conventional ice 
</p>
<p>protection systems (IPS). As a result, most eVTOL manufacturers are currently not seeking certification for flight into 
</p>
<p>known icing. However, they will still likely be required to demonstrate safe operation in icing for a limited duration 
</p>
<p>in the event of an inadvertent icing encounter. Thus, the ability to accurately predict and understand the impact of 
</p>
<p>icing on their performance is still of great importance.  
</p>
<p> To investigate the icing phenomenon relevant to eVTOL aircraft, NASA Glenn Research Center developed a 
</p>
<p>general-purpose propeller test stand for conducting fundamental icing research on electrically driven propellers in the 
</p>
<p>Icing Research Tunnel (IRT). A 10-day test entry in the IRT was completed in March 2023, with the goal of generating 
</p>
<p>ice accretions on a non-proprietary propeller geometry under well-characterized conditions [3]. The data from this test 
</p>
<p>is currently being used to support the development of NASA’s latest ice accretion solver, GlennICE [4-6]. Three 
</p>
<p>carbon fiber propellers of diameters 0.610, 0.711, and 0.914 m (24, 28, and 36 in) were utilized during the test. A 
</p>
<p>follow-on test lasting 5 days was later completed in January 2024, which focused on gathering experimental ice 
</p>
<p>shedding data as well as evaluating conditional icing scaling techniques for propellers [7,8]. Propeller performance 
</p>
<p>degradation in the form of torque change over time was captured during both test entries. This paper provides an 
</p>
<p>analysis and discussion of a subset of the propeller performance data that was acquired during these tests. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>III. Test Article and Instrumentation 
</p>
<p>A. Propeller Test Rig 
</p>
<p> A picture of the propeller test rig installed in the IRT test section with the 0.914 m (36 in) diameter propeller is 
</p>
<p>shown in Fig. 2. The propeller is driven by an EMRAX 188 permanent magnet synchronous motor with a continuous 
</p>
<p>power rating of up to 34 kW (45 hp) and a continuous torque rating of up to 52 Nm (38 lbf-ft). The motor has an IP65 
</p>
<p>ingress protection rating and is liquid cooled to prevent overheating. The propeller hub is mounted directly to the 
</p>
<p>motor via an adapter. To measure propeller forces and moments, the propeller rig is compatible with any of the 
</p>
<p>Interface 6A68 6-axis load cells which can be mounted directly aft of the motor as shown in Fig. 3. Only the Interface 
</p>
<p>6A68C load cell was available during the 2023 test. However, the 6A68C load cell was only used during the dry (non-
</p>
<p>icing) portion of the test to acquire baseline propeller performance data due to concerns that the imbalance loads 
</p>
<p>caused by ice shedding could exceed the structural strength of the load cell. The higher capacity, 6A68E load cell was 
</p>
<p>later obtained and used throughout the 2024 test entry to measure forces and moments during the icing encounter. The 
</p>
<p>motor speed is controlled by sending commands to a Cascadia Motion CM200DX Inverter. The inverter also provides 
</p>
<p>estimates of motor power, temperature, and torque. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>B. Propeller Geometry 
</p>
<p>Three carbon fiber propellers of diameters 0.610, 0.711, and 0.914 m (24, 28, and 36 in) were custom-made using 
</p>
<p>a non-proprietary propeller geometry to maximize data dissemination. All three propellers had a NACA 0012 airfoil 
</p>
<p>profile. The twist and chord distributions for the three propellers are shown in Fig. 4. The propeller geometry was 
</p>
<p>inspired by the Computationally Optimized Proprotor (COPR) design developed by NASA [9]. However, some 
</p>
<p>deviations from the original COPR design were required for manufacturing feasibility purposes. The twist and chord 
</p>
<p>distributions provided in Fig. 4 represent the final design after the necessary modifications were made. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>(a) Chord Distributions                                               (b) Twist Angle Distributions 
</p>
<p>Fig. 4  Comparisons of the propeller chord (a) and twist angle (b) distributions between the three propeller 
</p>
<p>sizes.  
</p>
<p>It can be seen from Fig. 4 that the three propellers have similar normalized twist and chord distributions from a 
</p>
<p>radial location of approximately 𝑟/𝑅 = 0.40 to 𝑟/𝑅 = 1.0. However, the use of the same hub and spinner for each set 
</p>
<p>of propeller blades prevented geometric similarity towards the root of the blade. In the complimentary paper by Rigby 
</p>
<p>and von Hardenberg, the effect of not geometrically scaling the spinner with propeller size was explored using 
</p>
<p>GlennICE and found to have a negligible effect on the predicted ice accretion [6]. 
</p>
<p>A 4-bladed, variable pitch hub was chosen for this test entry to provide greater flexibility in propeller operating 
</p>
<p>conditions. A pitch-adjustment tool was provided by the propeller manufacturer to change the collective pitch of the 
</p>
<p>blades. For both test entries, the target propeller pitch was 18° higher than the design twist distributions shown in Fig. 
</p>
<p>4b. After evaluating the laser scans of the propeller blades obtained during the test, it was determined that the blade 
</p>
<p>pitch angle could deviate as much as one degree from the target pitch setting. Future tests will utilize a calibrated 
</p>
<p>inclinometer to provide more consistent pitch settings.  
</p>
<p>IV. Test Procedure 
</p>
<p>A. Spinner Drag Characterization 
</p>
<p> To accurately determine the thrust generated by the propeller from the load cell measurements, it is important to 
</p>
<p>account for additional forces that may influence the net load measured by the load cell. In this case, the drag force 
</p>
<p>acting on the spinner was one such force that contributed to the net axial load measured by the load cell. To account 
</p>
<p>for this, the spinner drag was characterized by conducting a sweep of tunnel speed with the spinner installed on the 
</p>
<p>test stand without the propeller blades. During the tunnel speed sweep, the motor was exercised at a speed of 1000 
</p>
<p>RPM. Once the drag on the spinner was characterized, the thrust produced by the propeller could be determined by 
</p>
<p>subtracting the spinner drag force from the net axial load measured by the load cell. The results of the spinner drag 
</p>
<p>characterization are shown in Fig. 5.   </p>

<p>Predictions 
</p>
<p> Recall from Section III that an estimate of the propeller torque could be acquired from two independent sources: 
</p>
<p>1) from the load cell measured moment about the propeller axis of rotation and 2) from the inverter estimate of motor 
</p>
<p>torque based on the motor rotational speed and power. As such, the propeller performance charts include the calculated 
</p>
<p>torque coefficient and resulting efficiency using both sources of propeller torque. As can be seen from Fig. 6c, the 
</p>
<p>torque measured by the load cell is very similar to the torque estimate provided by the inverter, albeit slightly lower 
</p>
<p>at the higher advance ratios. Given the general good agreement between the load cell and inverter estimate of motor 
</p>
<p>torque, it was decided to utilize the inverter estimate of torque for the remainder of testing. This approach offers a 
</p>
<p>significant advantage over using the load cell, as the inverter estimate of motor torque is not affected by thermal drift, 
</p>
<p>creep, and hysteresis. As a result, the inverter estimate of torque was found to be much more consistent and repeatable 
</p>
<p>than the torque measured by the load cell. In addition, significant time was saved by not having to thermal soak the 
</p>
<p>load cell prior to each run. As such, only the inverter estimate of motor torque is presented herein. 
</p>

<p>B. Icing Performance Characterization 
</p>
<p> During a typical icing run, the propeller was operated at the target RPM and tunnel speed for several minutes until 
</p>
<p>the freestream total air temperature, 𝑡∞,0, reached a steady state at the target temperature for that run. After achieving 
</p>
<p>steady state conditions, the propeller was operated for 30 seconds before introducing the icing cloud to acquire baseline 
</p>
<p>performance data. The icing spray would continue for the target duration of that run or until a major shedding event 
</p>
<p>resulted in vibrations that exceeded their predefined limit. If a major shedding event occurred, the tunnel operators 
</p>
<p>would immediately turn off the cloud and begin the shutdown procedure. A linear curve fit was then applied to the 
</p>
<p>performance data from spray-on until spray-off, or until the 1st shedding event (if applicable), whichever occurred 
</p>
<p>first. Figure 8 shows a typical time history plot showing the rise in propeller torque for a run which includes a shedding 
</p>
<p>event around the 7-minute mark. </p>
<p></p>

<p>V. Test Data 
</p>
<p>A. Repeatability 
</p>
<p> Several repeat cases were performed to understand what level of variation could be expected in the propeller torque 
</p>
<p>measurements due to normal run to run variations in the cloud conditions. Figure 9 shows the propeller torque 
</p>

<p>B. Effect of LWC 
</p>
<p>Figure 10a shows the effect of increasing the Liquid Water Content (LWC) from 0.55 to 1.20 g/m3 on the propeller 
</p>
<p>torque for runs RA3683 and RA3684, respectively. The spray time for the 1.20 g/m3 run was reduced to match the 
</p>
<p>accumulation parameter, 𝐴𝑐, of the 0.55 g/m3 condition. All other conditions were identical as shown in Table 4. Based 
</p>
<p>on the analytical calculations of the freezing potential, 𝑛0
∗ , it was believed that the temperature was sufficiently cold 
</p>
<p>to produce rime ice along the entire span of the propeller for both the 0.55 and 1.20 g/m3 condition. However, analysis 
</p>
<p>of the ice shapes following the test revealed that the freezing fractions for the 1.20 g/m3 case were slightly less than 
</p>
<p>one. Based on this observation, and the results of subsequent temperature sweeps, it is believed that the freezing 
</p>
<p>fractions achieved during testing were approximately 20% lower than the analytically predicted freezing potentials 
</p>
<p>reported in Table 4. As a result, the freezing fraction for run RA3684 may have been closer to 𝑛𝑐 = 1.19*0.8 = 0.95 
</p>
<p>at 𝑟 𝑅⁄  = 0.75 where 𝑛𝑐 is the corrected freezing fraction. The reason for the large discrepancy between the 
</p>
<p>experimentally achieved freezing fractions and the analytical freezing potentials is still being investigated. 
</p>
<p>Nonetheless, a comparison of the propeller torque requirements between these two conditions is presented below.  
</p>
<p> As expected, the 1.20 g/m3 condition led to a more rapid increase in torque than the 0.55 g/m3 condition due to the 
</p>
<p>faster rate of ice accretion on the propeller. However, by plotting the change in torque as a function of the product of 
</p>
<p>the stagnation collection efficiency and accumulation parameter (𝛽0𝐴𝑐) as calculated at 𝑟 𝑅⁄  = 0.75, comparisons of 
</p>
<p>the propeller torque can be made at similar stagnation ice thicknesses. This result is shown in Fig. 10b. Note that, in 
</p>
<p>Fig. 10b, the propeller torque is presented in non-dimensional form as the torque coefficient divided by solidity 
</p>

</p>
<p>1.20 g/m3 condition still shows a slightly higher 𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄  for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐. This is likely due to the 1.20 g/m3 condition 
</p>
<p>having a freezing fraction slightly less than one as explained in the previous paragraph, resulting in more glaze-like 
</p>
<p>features along the stagnation line of the propeller. It is likely that even better agreement could have been achieved had 
</p>
<p>the temperature for the 1.20 g/m3 condition been reduced further to achieve a freezing fraction of one. The ice shapes

</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>conditions. For 𝑛0 &lt; 1, the propeller torque requirements initially increase with increasing temperature (decreasing 
</p>

</p>
<p>requirements were found to occur somewhere between a 𝑛𝑐 of 0.40 and 0.60. More data is needed to fully understand 
</p>

</p>
<p>identical as seen in Table 9.

<p> The results are encouraging for those who wish to gain insights into iced propeller performance degradation using 
</p>
<p>scaled models. However, the data collected during the present study is limited, as it encompasses only a small range

</p>

</p>
<p>VI. Conclusion 
</p>
<p> An experimental investigation of an eVTOL propeller under simulated icing conditions was conducted at the 
</p>
<p>NASA Glenn Icing Research Tunnel. Propeller performance degradation, in the form of torque rise over time, was 
</p>
<p>recorded under various cloud and operating conditions. The effect of LWC, MVD, temperature, tip speed, and 
</p>
<p>propeller size on the resulting change in torque were investigated and found to align well with the expected trends. 
</p>
<p>Several repeat conditions were conducted which found the repeatability of the torque data to be excellent. 
</p>
<p> To aid in the interpretation of the results, the key icing similarity parameters (𝑛0, 𝛽0, 𝐴𝑐, and 𝑊𝑒𝐿) have been 
</p>
<p>calculated at the blade 𝑟 𝑅⁄  = 0.75 location. The change in torque was also presented non-dimensionally as the torque 
</p>
<p>coefficient divided by solidity (𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄ ) and plotted against the product of the stagnation collection efficiency and 
</p>
<p>accumulation parameter (𝛽0𝐴𝑐), as calculated at the 𝑟 𝑅⁄  = 0.75 location.   
</p>
<p> Over the range of conditions tested, it was found that the change in 𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄  under rime conditions (𝑛0 = 1) was 
</p>
<p>largely independent of LWC, tip speed, and propeller size for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐 and advance ratio, provided that the 
</p>
<p>distribution of 𝑛0 and 𝛽0 are the same or similar. This observation is consistent with the measured ice shapes, which 
</p>
<p>revealed that good ice shape similarity was achieved under these conditions. When independently varying 𝛽0 at rime 
</p>
<p>conditions, it was found that the higher 𝛽0 condition required more torque for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐, likely due to the negative 
</p>
<p>aerodynamic impact of the increased impingement limits. 
</p>
<p> For glaze conditions (𝑛0 &lt; 1), the change in 𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄  was also found to be largely independent of LWC and propeller 
</p>
<p>size for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐 and advance ratio over the range of conditions tested, provided that the distributions of 𝑛0, 𝛽0, 
</p>
<p>and 𝑊𝑒𝐿 were the same or similar. However, when testing at a different tip speed (and consequently, 𝑊𝑒𝐿), it was 
</p>
<p>𝑟 𝑅⁄ = 0.75 </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>found that the higher tip speed condition resulted in a slightly higher 𝐶𝑄 𝜎⁄  for a given 𝛽0𝐴𝑐. More work is needed to 
</p>
<p>understand whether the difference in 𝑊𝑒𝐿 is responsible for this observation or if there are other factors involved.  
</p>
<p> While the results are encouraging for those who wish to apply current icing scaling methods to achieve similarity 
</p>
<p>of propeller performance in icing, the present study is limited to a small range of propeller sizes and operating 
</p>
<p>conditions. Further work is needed over a larger range of tip speeds, advance ratios, and propeller sizes to provide 
</p>
<p>greater confidence in the scaling methodology.  
</p>
