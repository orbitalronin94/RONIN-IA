<p>NASA’s PACE Ocean Color Instrument Thermal 
</p>
<p>Design Evolution: from Goddard’s Instrument Design 
</p>
<p>Lab Through Flight Development 
Kan Yang1, Deepak Patel2, and Wes Ousley3 
</p>
<p>NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Greenbelt, MD, 20771 
</p>
<p>NASA’s Plankton, Aerosol, Cloud, ocean Ecosystem (PACE) mission, set to launch in 2024, 
</p>
<p>seeks to provide data continuity for the ocean color, aerosol and cloud measurements acquired 
</p>
<p>by NASA’s on-orbit Earth Science observatories since the 1990s. It will accomplish this 
</p>
<p>through its Ocean Color Instrument (OCI), an optical spectrometer being developed for 
</p>
<p>hyper-spectral measurements in the ultraviolet-to-near-infrared band between 340 nm and 
</p>
<p>2260 nm. Although OCI’s instrument architecture will provide greater insight and resolution 
</p>
<p>than its predecessors in this wavelength range, the engineering required to achieve this also 
</p>
<p>poses a greater challenge. In thermal engineering, this translates to a more complex thermal 
</p>
<p>control approach to address high heat dissipations, stringent stabilities, the volume of heat 
</p>
<p>that requires transport, and changing thermal environments due to tilting of the entire 
</p>
<p>instrument ±20° twice per orbit. This current work explores how the PACE OCI instrument 
</p>
<p>design has evolved from its initial conception in NASA Goddard’s Instrument Design 
</p>
<p>Laboratory (IDL) to the current iteration of its flight design. The IDL studies explored three 
</p>
<p>separate instrument configurations and two spatial resolutions per configuration, which were 
</p>
<p>then down selected to a single instrument type and spatial resolution for flight instrument 
</p>
<p>development. OCI subsequently went through major project milestones, including 
</p>
<p>Preliminary Design Review (PDR), Critical Design Review (CDR), Pre-Environmental Review 
</p>
<p>(PER) and Pre-Ship Review (PSR), with significant design updates along the way. This paper 
</p>
<p>aims to provide a comprehensive account of OCI’s thermal control architecture evolution and 
</p>
<p>the engineering drivers that have shaped it, with the goal of identifying trends spanning the 
</p>
<p>full instrument development timeline to inform and advance future instrument thermal 
</p>
<p>designs.  
</p>

<p>I. Introduction  
</p>
<p>HE Plankton, Aerosol, Cloud, ocean Ecosystem (PACE) Mission is NASA’s next major observatory to monitor 
</p>
<p>the health of Earth’s living ocean and climate and ensure data continuity on critical metrics for ocean color, 
</p>
<p>aerosols, and clouds. It builds on both the repository of scientific data and prior design heritage from instruments such 
</p>
<p>as the Sea-Viewing Wide Field-of-View Sensor (SeaWiFS) 1 on the OrbView-2 Satellite, the Moderate Resolution 
</p>
<p>Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) 2 on the Terra and Aqua satellites, and the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer 
</p>
<p>Suite (VIIRS) 3 on Suomi NPP, to answer such essential questions as: what are the long-term changes in aerosol and 
</p>
<p>cloud properties and how are they correlated with variations in climate? How is the concentration and composition of 
</p>
<p>organisms in our ocean ecosystems changing, and how do they affect our food webs? What materials are exchanged 
</p>
<p>between the land and ocean, and how do they affect life on our coasts as well as peoples’ health and welfare?  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The Ocean Color Instrument (OCI) is the primary instrument on PACE. OCI is an advanced optical spectrometer 
</p>
<p>that will enable higher resolution measurement of Earth’s radiance spanning the near-infrared to ultraviolet portions 
</p>
<p>of the electromagnetic spectrum. OCI uses gratings for spectral light dispersion and conducts hyper-spectral 
</p>
<p>measurements via separate Ultraviolet-to-Visible (UVVIS, 340-605 nm) and Visible-to-Near-Infrared (VISNIR, 600 
</p>
<p>– 890 nm) Focal Plane Assemblies (FPA). The “blue” UVVIS channel enables measurements of aerosols and 
</p>
<p>microscopic marine algae, while the “red” VISNIR channel allows for studies of phytoplankton health and coastal 
</p>
<p>T </p>
<p></p>
</div>

</p>
<p>International Conference on Environmental Systems 
 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>biology. Spectrally filtered Short Wave Infrared (SWIR) detectors in discrete bands between 940 nm to 2260 nm allow 
</p>
<p>for determination of the clarity of the atmosphere over the ocean and provide information on the distribution and 
</p>
<p>characteristics of clouds, enabling measurements of the near-surface concentration of chlorophyll as well as the 
</p>
<p>understanding of the balance of energy that enters and leaves the climate system, helping to drive down uncertainties 
</p>
<p>in models to predict future climate change. The following sections will focus on the evolution of the engineering 
</p>
<p>design on the OCI instrument since 2015, with special focus on thermal design, and its eventual integration and testing 
</p>
<p>on its journey to flight.   
</p>
<p>II. Ocean Color Instrument Design Trades in the Instrument Design Laboratory 
</p>
<p>Early concept studies of the OCI instrument were undertaken at NASA Goddard Space Flight Center’s (GSFC) 
</p>
<p>Instrument Design Laboratory (IDL). Through separate week-long sessions each employing a team of engineers in a 
</p>
<p>concurrent and collaborative rapid design process, the IDL performed trade studies on three OCI instrument 
</p>
<p>configurations: a Multi-Band Scanner (MBS), a Hyper-Spectral Scanner (HSS), and a Hyper-Spectral Pushbroom 
</p>
<p>(HSP). A comparison between the three separate data acquisition methods that defined the design of each instrument 
</p>
<p>configuration is shown in Figure 1; these are discussed in greater detail in the following sub-sections. Note that for 
</p>
<p>these studies, the Main Electronics Box (MEB) was assumed to reside on the spacecraft and have its thermal control 
</p>
<p>managed separately from the instrument via its own dedicated radiator. Therefore, its dissipations and radiator sizes 
</p>
<p>were not considered in these early design comparisons.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
Figure 1. OCI Instrument Design Configurations Considered in the IDL. 
</p>
<p>A. Multi-Band Scanner (MBS) 
</p>
<p>The Multi-Band Scanner was a whisk-broom design with a Rotating Telescope Assembly (RTA), which 
</p>
<p>continuously rotated a scan mirror at 0.4 Hz through a cross-track scan to acquire an image on the ground. The rotating 
</p>
<p>telescope assembly transformed the 112° cross-track field of view into an image that emerged from the side of the 
</p>
<p>RTA housing at a large angle off the rotation axis. The Half Angle Mirror (HAM) spun at half the RTA rate and was 
</p>
<p>located inside the Outboard Spin Bearing, opposite the RTA’s drive motor. The center image in Figure 2 shows the 
</p>
<p>optical path at the heart of the MBS configuration, where the signal from the instrument’s nadir view is transported 
</p>
<p>via telescope lenses in the spinning RTA to the centrally located HAM, then distributed to three separate downstream 
</p>
<p>channels: a UVVIS channel sensitive between 350 and 580 nm, a VISNIR channel sensitive between 610 and 940 nm, 
</p>
<p>and a SWIR channel sensitive between 1240 and 2250 nm, each with separate detector assemblies.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Two cases were studied: one design which produced 250 m ground sample distance pixels at nadir, and one which 
</p>
<p>produced 500 m. As seen from Figure 2, the RTA was centrally located within the instrument housing, and as it rotated 
</p>
<p>it scanned through views of the Earth via the opened Earth Aperture Door, the Sun via the Solar Aperture, and cold 
</p>
<p>space via the Deep Space Aperture. Protruding from the anti-ram side of the instrument was a housing which protected 
</p>
<p>all three detector assemblies and their respective optics from the harsh space environment. The UVVIS and VISNIR 
</p>
<p>channel detectors were required to be held at -23°C ± 0.1°C to achieve ample radiometric signal, and dissipate 0.6 W </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>4 
</p>
<p>International Conference on Environmental Systems 
 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>and 0.3 W respectively in the 250 m case, and 0.42 and 0.21 W respectively in the 500 m case. The SWIR channel 
</p>
<p>had a temperature requirement of -93°C ±0.1°C, and dissipated 0.39 W with 250 m and 0.3 W with 500 m. The SWIR 
</p>
<p>and visible channels were read through a single Front End Electronics (FEE) box close by, which output 8.2 W of heat 
</p>
<p>at 250 m, and 4.1 W at 500 m.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Thermal control of the MBS configuration was fairly simple and 
</p>
<p>was achieved with passive heat transport to three dedicated radiators 
</p>
<p>with a view to deep space. The SWIR detector was heat-strapped to an 
</p>
<p>ethane heat pipe and its heat was transported to a SWIR radiator; the 
</p>
<p>UVVIS and VISNIR channel detectors were both heat-strapped to a 
</p>
<p>single ammonia heat pipe which transported to a dedicated visible 
</p>
<p>radiator; heat from the FEE and RTA motors were pulled via dual 
</p>
<p>ammonia heat pipes to an “Instrument Enclosure” radiator. An earth 
</p>
<p>shield covering the SWIR and visible radiators allowed them to 
</p>
<p>passively cool to desired sink temperatures. Precision control on 
</p>
<p>detectors was achieved by cold biasing the detector assemblies and 
</p>
<p>using trim heaters to achieve ± 0.1°C stability. This entire assembly 
</p>
<p>was mounted onto a cruciform flexure at one end and a bipod at the 
</p>
<p>other, connecting it to its instrument baseplate. A tilt motor mounted 
</p>
<p>on the cruciform side allowed the instrument to pitch ±20° to avoid 
</p>
<p>glint.  
</p>
<p>B. Hyper-Spectral Scanner (HSS) 
</p>
<p>Similar to the MBS, the Hyper-Spectral Scanner design also 
</p>
<p>employed an RTA, rotating the scan mirror at 18 Hz to acquire the 
</p>
<p>image through a large 120° cross-track Field of View (FOV). The 
</p>
<p>image that emerged from the RTA was transferred via the optical path 
</p>
<p>through the HAM and split into three major wavelength bands: the 
</p>
<p>UVVIS channel between 350 and 555 nm, VISNIR channel between 583 and 865 nm, and six SWIR channels between 
</p>
<p>940 and 2250 nm. The rotating telescope moved the image of the science pixels across the ground to cover the full 
</p>
<p>field of regard without any gaps in coverage per scan. Then, the detector used Time Delay Integration (TDI)4 to 
</p>
<p>transfer the charge from pixel-to-pixel at the same rate as the rotating telescope, allowing the same scene to be imaged 
</p>
<p>on the detector for an extended time and building up enough signal to meet signal-to-noise ratio.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>  Two cases were studied: one design which produced 350m ground sample distance pixels at nadir, and one which 
</p>
<p>produced 500m. Figure 3 speaks to the instrument design: the RTA sat at the heart of the instrument, spun by a motor 
</p>
<p>which contains a counter-rotating component for momentum compensation. The HAM and its rotation mechanism 
</p>
<p>resided diametrically opposite of the RTA motor, and the UVVIS and VISNIR channel detector subassemblies sat 
</p>
<p>immediately downstream of the HAM. Both UVVIS and VISNIR channel detectors were required to be held at -20°C 
</p>
<p>±0.1°C and dissipate 0.5 W each. Below the rotating telescope, the six SWIR detectors were required to be held at  
</p>
<p>-93°C ± 0.1°C and dissipate a total of about 0.2 W. The FEEs were within close proximity to the detectors; in the 350 
</p>
<p>m case there is one FEE for the SWIR detectors dissipating 18 W and another FEE for the visible detectors dissipating 
</p>
<p>22.3 W, while for the 500 m case these electronics were consolidated into one FEE box dissipating 25.5 W. The 
</p>
<p>smaller dissipation in the 500m case was a result of a smaller detector used in the 500 m configuration, reducing the 
</p>
<p>necessary number of FEE cards for readout. The optical telescope, detectors, calibration assembly, and FEEs all sat 
</p>
<p>atop a mechanism which allowed pivoting of the instrument ±20°, supported by the instrument deck structure. Three 
</p>
<p>bipod assembles tied the instrument deck to the spacecraft.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>For thermal management of the system, two large radiators were part of the pivoting assembly: the detector radiator 
</p>
<p>and the FEE radiator. The SWIR detector assemblies each siphoned their heat via Ethane Constant Conductance Heat 
</p>
<p>Pipes (CCHP) to a cold plate actively controlled to colder than –93°C by thermoelectric coolers (TEC). Those TECs 
</p>
<p>then rejected their waste heat directly to the detector radiator. The visible detectors each had their own respective 
</p>
<p>high-purity Aluminum heat straps to reject their heat to the same white paint-coated radiator. The FEEs transported 
</p>
<p>their heat through ammonia CCHPs to a dedicated FEE radiator, which itself had embedded ammonia heat pipe 
</p>

<p>C. Hyper-Spectral Pushbroom (HSP) 
</p>
<p>The Hyper-Spectral Pushbroom design expanded on the Copernicus 
</p>
<p>program’s Sentinel-3 Ocean and Land Colour Instrument (OLCI)5,6 and 
</p>
<p>the European Space Agency’s Medium Resolution Imaging 
</p>
<p>Spectrometer (MERIS)7 ocean color instrument heritage, taking data 
</p>
<p>across 490 spectral pixels between 342.5 nm and 955 nm, with the 
</p>
<p>additional inclusion of two near-infrared atmospheric correction bands 
</p>
<p>between 820 and 940 nm and five SWIR bands between 1240 and 2250 
</p>
<p>nm. In the IDL, it was studied in both 260m and 500m spatial resolution 
</p>
<p>configurations. As a “push-broom” design, it relied on simultaneous 
</p>
<p>measurements across a large 104° instantaneous cross-track field of 
</p>
<p>view via the use of six cameras in the 260 m configuration, each 
</p>
<p>covering 17.33° FOV, and five cameras in the 500m configuration, each 
</p>
<p>covering 20.8° FOV. While this eliminated the use of a scan mechanism 
</p>
<p>in the design, it increased the optical and detector readout complexity of 
</p>
<p>the system.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>As seen from Figure 4, the HSP design consisted of a mechanical 
</p>
<p>cradle assembly which attaches to bi-pods with an integrated tilt 
</p>
<p>mechanism on either end, allowing the instrument itself to pitch ±20°. 
</p>
<p>The cradle assembly contained the calibration wheel with the entrance 
</p>
<p>baffle for the nadir-facing Earth Science signal and the solar calibration baffle, as well as the six FEE boxes. The FEEs 
</p>
<p>were required to be kept below 40°C operational temperatures; there were three visible readout FEEs dissipating 5.8 
</p>
<p>W each at 260 m and 4.2 W at 500 m, and three SWIR readout FEEs dissipating 3 W each at 260 m and 2.5 W each 
</p>
<p>at 500 m. The OB was mounted atop the cradle structure via flexures, and six cameras (five for 500 m) are aligned 
</p>
<p>precisely on top of the optical bench. Within each camera resided one SWIR detector, one visible detector which 
</p>
<p>covered the full range of UVVIS and VISNIR measurements, and the respective optical elements to shape the image 
</p>
<p>and direct the incoming signal. Each SWIR detector was required to be held at &lt; -123°C ± 0.1°C and dissipated 0.055 
</p>
<p>W in the 260 m case and 0.045 W in the 500 m case. The operational temperature requirement for each visible detector 
</p>
<p>was &lt; -25°C ±0.1°C and dissipated 1.0 W in the 260 m case and 0.58 W in the 500 m case. An MLI-wrapped 
</p>
<p>mechanical enclosure protected this assembly, and external features on this enclosure included the OB, FEE, and 
</p>
<p>SWIR radiators. The SWIR radiator itself had an earth shield with Silver Teflon exterior and Vapor-Deposited 
</p>
<p>Aluminum (VDA) interior to passively cool to the temperatures required for providing a heat sink to the SWIR 
</p>
<p>detectors.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The thermal control scheme developed for the HSP configuration relied primarily on passive heat management. 
</p>
<p>Manganin wire and long harness lengths were used to minimize the conductive parasitics to the SWIR detector within 
</p>
<p>each camera, and all of the SWIR detectors spread across camera assemblies were tied to an ethane heat pipe, which 
</p>
<p>transferred the dissipations and parasitic heat to the dedicated SWIR radiator. Three parallel ammonia heat pipes 
</p>
<p>pulled heat from the OB and the visible detectors to the OB radiator. Three other parallel ammonia heat pipes 
</p>
<p>connected the cradle assembly and the FEEs to the FEE radiator. The radiators were 0.1 in. (0.254 cm) thick Aluminum 
</p>

</p>
<p>with an AZ93 white paint coating on the space-facing side and MLI on the back side, and contained Proportional-
</p>
<p>Integral-Derivative (PID) control heaters for tight thermal stability control. There were thermostatically controlled 
</p>
<p>survival heaters for the OB, the FEE radiator, and visible detectors. In the 500 m design, the only significant difference 
</p>
<p>was that the OB and FEE radiators have been combined into one common radiator.

<p>E. Selection of Flight Configuration and Evolution of Instrument Architecture to System Requirements 
</p>
<p>Review 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The previous section detailed a comparison of thermal designs, which showed significant differences between all 
</p>
<p>configurations considered as well as the strengths and shortcomings of each. Although engineering concerns weighed 
</p>
<p>heavily in determining the design to proceed 
</p>
<p>forward with development for flight, the 
</p>
<p>quality of science data that could be achieved 
</p>
<p>was the ultimate factor in choosing for the 
</p>
<p>flight configuration.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>From radiometric analysis of each 
</p>
<p>configuration, HSP, MBS, and HSS were all 
</p>
<p>able to achieve sufficient science signal at 
</p>
<p>their detector. However, the HSP concept, 
</p>
<p>being a pushbroom, was susceptible to image 
</p>
<p>striping, increasing the temporal and spatial 
</p>
<p>variability and reducing the accuracy of the 
</p>
<p>science products. A pushbroom concept also 
</p>
<p>required complex pitch and yaw maneuvers 
</p>
<p>to perform lunar calibration, increasing the 
</p>
<p>scientific and technical risk of this monthly 
</p>
<p>calibration. The MBS concept did not meet its Level 1 science objectives either, as the resolution achievable in its 
</p>
<p>data product was 10 nm, versus a 5 nm required resolution for hyper-spectral capability. Its technical implementation 
</p>
<p>was also very difficult, requiring custom detector development. HSS allowed all Level 1 science objectives to be 
</p>
<p>achieved while showing the greatest technical feasibility and lowest risk, and was therefore selected as the best option 
</p>
<p>to proceed forward for flight design.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Advancing towards Mission Concept Review (MCR), a further trade study was performed on the ground sample 
</p>
<p>distance range for HSS, between 500 m and 1000 m spatial resolution. As OCI was cost-constrained, the 1000 m 
</p>
<p>resolution presented a lower cost estimate while still achieving all Level 1 science objectives, and was chosen over 
</p>
<p>the 500 m spatial resolution design to advance to System Requirements Review (SRR). A Solar Calibration Assembly 
</p>
<p>(SCA) containing solar diffusers was also added to monitor the short-term changes in detector response, along with 
</p>
<p>dark blackbody targets at the point of rotation where the telescope views the inside of the RTA housing, to allow for 
</p>
<p>dark current calibration.  
</p>

</p>
<p>into PDR, CDR and Flight TV design. The flight thermal design is generalized in the block diagram shown in Figure 
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>subsections.

<p> The following sections attempt to capture some 
</p>
<p>of the thermal changes of the instrument as it 
</p>
<p>passed through major instrument development 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>A. OCI at Preliminary Design Review (PDR) 
</p>
<p>At PDR, OCI was more complex from a systems perspective than a thermal perspective. The complexity of OCI 
</p>
<p>at PDR mainly derived from the measurement of critical science wavelengths, while isolating grounding issues from 
</p>
<p>electrical noise sensitive components within the DAU and FPA subsystems. A view of OCI at PDR is shown in Figure 
</p>
<p>7. Between SRR (review gate pre-PDR) and PDR, the thermal changes to instrument included: 
</p>
<p>1. The star tracker being moved to OCI 
</p>
<p>2. The ±20° Tilt actuation being moved out of OCI and becoming part of the 
</p>
<p>Spacecraft entity 
</p>
<p>3. EMI gaskets being introduced at the FPA/LHP interface 
</p>
<p>4. The ICDU and MCE being moved from the spacecraft (S/C) to the 
</p>
<p>instrument 
</p>
<p>5. The stability requirements being introduced which resulted in the addition 
</p>
<p>of a Heater Control Card to the ICDU.  
</p>
<p>  
</p>
<p> The star tracker moving to OCI was a change to provide a better 
</p>
<p>understanding of OCI pointing in relation to the OCI primary mirror bore 
</p>
<p>sight, as all components would be held by the primary structure. With the 
</p>
<p>star tracker on the S/C, the uncertainty would have been too large to accurately calculate whether OCI was pointing 
</p>
<p>directly at Earth. This was verified by performing the Structural, Thermal and Optical (STOp) analysis, performed 
</p>
<p>through following process: 1) the stress analyst provided a meshed model, with data on nodes and location with some 
</p>
<p>reference point to the thermal analyst; 2) the thermal engineer then overlayed the meshed mechanical model over the 
</p>
<p>thermal model; 3) the thermal analyst mapped temperatures to each structural node; 4) the stress analyst received 
</p>
<p>inputs from Optical engineer on the light path of the optical system; 5) the stress analyst combined the structural, 
</p>
<p>thermal and Optical model to produce line of sight error value as well as any point-to-point reference misalignment, 
</p>
<p>such as the star trackers to OCI bore-sight.  
</p>
<p>  
</p>
<p> The tilt actuation moved from being a part of the instrument to external to the instrument, as a spacecraft-mounted 
</p>
<p>mechanism that cradled OCI. This was driven due to the challenges of having a flexible thermal system to maintain 
</p>
<p>FPA temperatures, which were part of the Optical Module that was originally being tilted within OCI. The choice of 
</p>
<p>having the entire instrument tilt simplified the thermal and mechanical design of the instrument, but did add weight to 
</p>
<p>the observatory as the new tilt interface would be required to carry the mass of the instrument. EMI gaskets were 
</p>
<p>added between the FPA and LHP evaporator interface in support of shielding the FPA CCDs from radiative electrical 
</p>
<p>noise. This approach decreased the radiative susceptibility of the FPAs, but also added an interface that resulted in a 
</p>
<p>temperature delta; this delta was one that thermal had to account for in maintaining CCD temperatures. The ICDU 
</p>
<p>and MCE were moved to the instrument for simplification of the instrument build, but unfortunately added two new 
</p>
<p>thermal control zones to the instrument for operational temperature maintenance. Lastly, one of the major impacts to 
</p>

<p>thermal was the introduction of new stability requirements. These stability 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>impacted the ICDU total power consumption. 
</p>

</p>
<p>Between SRR and PDR, another major addition to the OCI Thermal Control 
</p>
<p>System (TCS) was LHPs. Loop heat pipes provide a means for transporting heat from 
</p>
<p>source to radiator/condenser through capillary action. Multiple benefits of LHPs 
</p>
<p>proved highly useful on OCI, including transport of 
</p>
<p>heat over long distances and lack of sensitivity to 
</p>
<p>orientation. These LHPs were introduced to provide the 
</p>
<p>least technical impact to mission while also providing 
</p>
<p>adequate thermal control for the FPAs. Given the long (~30 in. / 76 cm) distance of 
</p>
<p>FPAs away from a direct view to space and a need for precise temperature control and 
</p>
<p>mass/power budget, it became evident through thermal trade studies that LHPs would 
</p>
<p>be the best option. Constant conductance heat pipes would have required 60W 
</p>
<p>additional heater power, and would have been extremely sensitive to orientation, as OCI 
</p>
<p>has to go through ±20° tilt operation during ground testing. Thermal straps would have 
</p>
<p>required considerable mass increase (~25 kg) as well as the 60W heater power hit. Though LHPs represented a 
</p>
<p>significant increase to schedule and budget, these considerations reinforced the need for LHPs to provide the best 
</p>
<p>thermal performance for the FPAs. For OCI, Thermacore was chosen as the vendor for the LHPs, with a very robust 
</p>
<p>design to withstand OCI tilt actuation, provide thermal stability and withstand large temperature gradients (up to 
</p>
<p>100°C between radiator and FPA, during survival mode).  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Moving towards CDR, the instrument incorporated the thermal changes described above. In addition, there was an 
</p>
<p>Engineering Test Unit (ETU) built of the loop heat pipe and FPA Thermal Straps. These ETUs were identified as risk 
</p>
<p>reduction units to verify whether the expected temperature drop, given the dissipation, environment and radiator sizing 
</p>
<p>would provide FPA CCDs with temperature control per requirements; see Figure 8 for the schematic of the LHPs and 
</p>
<p>thermal straps. Testing of ETUs did provide insight into temperature drop across the LHPs from condenser to 
</p>
<p>evaporator as well as the temperature vs. power performance curve for this specific LHP design. Figure 9 shows the 
</p>
<p>setup of the ETU LHP inside the chamber. Although this was not a flight-like or ground-test-like setup, the importance 
</p>
<p>of this test was to ensure there was no gravity influence on the transport lines and that the reservoir was in a non-
</p>
<p>adverse orientation, and both these requirements were met through this setup. Results from this test showed that, to 
</p>
<p>maintain the FPA CCDs at -25°C (for worst case hot) and -35°C (worst case cold), the radiator sizing would have to 
</p>
<p>increase by approximately 15%.

</p>
<p>critical hardware. Leading up to the CDR, the main focus for the thermal team 
</p>
<p>was to ensure that the long lead items were put on order. Heaters and blanketing 
</p>
<p>were the major part of thermal subsystem procurement, with the LHP being the 
</p>
<p>other major procurement. Since the design for LHP did not require any change in 
</p>
<p>power or temperature and only required a slight mechanical design change, it was 
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>and there were some gaps widened to allow for thermal expansion while maintaining the purpose of the features. Also, 
</p>
<p>there was welding of new transport lines to accommodate the shorter length for flight design. These changes were 
</p>
<p>fluidly executed by the LHP vendor, Thermacore.

<p>this allowed for choosing makeup heaters that did not adversely impact performance. For OCI, there was a 7 W and a 
</p>
<p>10 W startup heater. After LHP testing, it was observed that at operational temperatures keeping the 7 W startup heater 
</p>
<p>provided adequate LHP operation, whereas 10 W resulted in slightly warmer CCD temperatures. Overall, the Flight 
</p>
<p>LHP testing was a very successful campaign that verified that the LHPs will be able to maintain the CCD temperatures 
</p>
<p>throughout the protoflight range as well as operational range when exposed to worst case environments. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Late in the design process, the SDA Pulse Calibration 
</p>
<p>Assembly (SPCA) was added as a passive optical pulse 
</p>
<p>generator that was used to calibrate the SDA. From ETU 
</p>
<p>testing of the SDA, it was realized that the detector and 
</p>
<p>FEE response were too slow to match the speed of the 
</p>
<p>rotating telescope, putting Level 1 requirements associated 
</p>
<p>with the SWIR data products at risk. Using the sun, the 
</p>
<p>SPCA created an impulse response used to trend and 
</p>
<p>calibrate the SDA signal over the life of the mission, 
</p>
<p>providing an onboard signal to allow for software 
</p>
<p>correction and on-orbit calibration to achieve requirements.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Once the instrument was fully integrated and 
</p>
<p>underwent EMI and Vibration/Acoustics testing, it was then exposed to worst-case on-orbit thermal environments. 
</p>
<p>OCI TV testing was a sixty-day test campaign composed of calibration activities, protoflight level testing, bakeout, 
</p>
<p>survival and operational thermal balances. In order to achieve the goals of the test, a setup was constructed to provide 
</p>
<p>radiative views and similar sink temperatures that each of the following key components would experience on orbit: 
</p>
<p>DAU, MCE, ICDU, FPA/SDA and the Main Optical Bench (MOB). A schematic of the setup is shown in Figure 13.  
</p>
<p>Lessons learned from this test are captured in Section IV.  
</p>
<p>IV. Instrument Testing Lessons Learned 
</p>
<p>There were two major lessons learned from this effort; this included: 
</p>
<p>1. MOB Heater Control Approach: During OCI TV test nominal operation temperatures, the science and 
</p>
<p>systems team had observed a cyclical behavior within the signals at 940nm. The MOB HAM heater, along 
</p>
<p>with all of the heaters on the MOB structure were designed with a bang-bang (ON/OFF) software control 
</p>
<p>approach. Even after lowering the deadband, it was realized that the physical limitation of the heater card 
</p>
<p>response had been achieved and no further deadband adjustments would have improved the steady-state 
</p>
<p>oscillations. See Figure 14 for the heater vs. signal response plot. Additionally see Figure 15 for the multiple 
</p>
<p>attempts that were made to achieve a smaller deadband for the MOB HAM Heater, but were not successful. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

</p>

<p> 
</p>
<p>Lesson Learned: always utilize a heater control approach that provides thermal stability (±0.5°C over an 
</p>
<p>orbit) for an optical system. For OCI, the requirement on the MOB is ±2.5°C over an orbit. Although this 
</p>
<p>requirement was met analytically and through testing during transient cases, it was observed that this may 
</p>
<p>have been too wide of a deadband range.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Lesson Learned: the MOB was constructed from 
</p>
<p>Aluminum 6061. Using composite or carbon-
</p>
<p>fiber would have provided much better optical 
</p>
<p>stability due to its much lower coefficient of 
</p>
<p>thermal expansion. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>2. LHP Reservoir Shutdown and Operational 
</p>
<p>heater sizing: During transition to the OCI 
</p>
<p>survival test phase, it was observed that the 
</p>
<p>UVVIS LHP had gone into a self-start situation. 
</p>
<p>Self-start is when LHP achieves the right type of 
</p>
<p>temperature gradients as well as an external 
</p>
<p>influence on system that causes an unexpected 
</p>
<p>start while in shutdown mode. During the transition to survival, the radiator was at -100°C, the reservoir was 
</p>
<p>cycling at 8°C to 15°C and the FPA was at 20°C (due to transitioning from Bakeout temperature of 40°C). 
</p>
<p>As soon as UVVIS LHP self-start occurred, the cold fluid (-100°C) rushed into the reservoir (8°C) and 
</p>
<p>resulted in the FPA CCD temperature to drop at ~3.5°C/min, whereas the requirement was 0.5°C/min. At 
</p>
<p>this point the shutdown heater (~9W) was not sufficient to overcome the self-start. The main driver to this 
</p>
<p>was found to be the additional pressure head from gravity induced on the vertical run of liquid line. Due to 
</p>
<p>the reservoir being lower than the exit of the liquid line from radiator, this resulted in the LHP having to deal 
</p>
<p>with this additional pressure head. On orbit, if a self-start occurs then it is likely going to cause a much less 
</p>
<p>severe temperature drop on FPA CCDs.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Lesson learned: discuss with the instrument/observatory systems team on how the LHP will be utilized for 
</p>
<p>ground testing as well as on orbit operations. This included phases such as survival, cold startup, and any 
</p>
<p>idiosyncrasies to be aware of during protoflight testing. This also helped in sizing the operational heater, as 
</p>
<p>the steady state need was no more than 2W, while the startup needed a full 9.5W of heater power on the 
</p>
<p>reservoir. 
</p>
<p>V. Conclusion 
</p>
<p>This paper addressed the overall design process of the OCI instrument on PACE, from trade studies performed 
</p>
<p>within the Instrument Design Laboratory at GSFC all the way to modifications for flight implementation, and lessons 
</p>
<p>learned from flight integration. Figure 16 gives a visual overview of the design changes from MCR to CDR. Although 
</p>
<p>there was a lot of variation and flux in engineering resources between the early designs of OCI, latter iterations, 
</p>
<p>especially from SRR onwards, showed only marginal changes in detector dissipations, radiator sizes, and heater 
</p>
<p>powers. For OCI, the greatest thermal impact occurred with the addition of the instrument electronics boxes to the 
</p>
<p>instrument itself, rather than being mounted to the spacecraft as in earlier design iterations. This drove much of the 
</p>
<p>initial increase in instrument dissipations and radiator sizes from the IDL HSS 500m design to the SRR and PDR 
</p>
<p>design.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>With the solidification of the design from SRR to the as-built TV configuration, operational heater powers only 
</p>
<p>decreased by 15%, and total radiator area only increased by 9% over the five-year design period between those two 
</p>
<p>bookend milestones. This speaks to the accuracy in which early design estimates manage to capture the thermal design 
</p>
<p>needs of the instrument, and the confidence in which the thermal designer can provide order-of-magnitude estimates 
</p>
<p>early-on so long as sufficient design margins are held. As usual, the commonly used 30% over Current Best Estimate 
</p>
<p>values from SRR was sufficiently conservative to encompass as-built flight values. Through this comprehensive look 
</p>
<p>at OCI’s thermal control architecture evolution, the engineering drivers that have shaped it, and the design
