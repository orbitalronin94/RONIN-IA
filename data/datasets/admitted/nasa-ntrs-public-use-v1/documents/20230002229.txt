<div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>SSRG Fatigue Study</p>
<p>Six subjects performed repetitive dynamic-gripping interspersed with isometric grip-holds under three conditions</p>
<p>Barehanded</p>
<p>Wearing pressurized Phase VI EVA SSG</p>
<p>Wearing pressurized SSRG</p>
<p>Results showed that the SSRG successfully reduced the neuromuscular effort needed to close the fingers of the space suit glove in more than half of subjects during two types of tasks</p>
<p>However, the SSRG required more neuromuscular effort to extend the fingers compared to a conventional SSG in many subjects</p>
<p>Subjects using SSRG stated they felt less fatigued during short periods of intense work compared to the SSG</p>
<p></p>
<p>Results show the potential of the SSRG as a grasp-assist device that can improve astronaut performance and reduce injury risk by reducing effort</p>
<p>Madden et al., ICES 2017</p>

<div class="slide-content"><p>Portable Dynamometry</p>
<p>Joint strength assessments serve an important role in the medical rehabilitation domain, granting clinicians better insight into the progress of their patients</p>
<p>Similar assessments could be utilized by exercise physiologists, strength and conditioning specialists, and flight doctors to help maintain astronauts’ muscle function during missions</p>
<p>Isokinetic dynamometry is an accepted method of joint strength assessment in clinical settings, and NASA uses it as a tool to assess strength changes from pre- to post-flight</p>
<p>However, currently no practical means of obtaining individual joint strength data exists during spaceflight, precluding the ability to track changes in strength during missions</p>
<p>We developed a portable knee version to fill this gap</p>
<p>Comparison testing has shown that the Portable Knee Dynamometer has analogous repeatability to the state-of-the-art ground system and, overall, the peak knee torque measured by the PKD agrees very well for isometric and isokinetic contractions.</p>
<p>Device has been evaluated for proof-of-concept in microgravity</p>
<div class="embedded" id="slide17_rId2"></div>
<div class="embedded" id="slide17_rId3"></div>
<div class="embedded" id="slide17_rId4"></div>

<div class="slide-content"><p>Additional Developments</p>
<p>Sensorimotor Countermeasures Device</p>
<p>Sensorimotor system adapts to microgravity</p>
<p>Useless vestibular inputs</p>
<p>Main proprioceptive stimuli from treadmill</p>
<p>Future long-duration transit vehicles might not have a treadmill, so alternative sensorimotor training will be necessary</p>
<p>We are developing a Balance Board with pulldown actuation via a harness</p>
<p>The latest prototype has been evaluated for microgravity feasibility during a parabolic flight suite</p>
<p>Working toward an ISS technology demonstration unit</p>
<div class="embedded" id="slide18_rId2"></div>
<div class="embedded" id="slide18_rId3"></div>
<div class="embedded" id="slide18_rId4"></div>
</div>
<div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Additional Developments</p>
<p>Upper Extremity Offload Device for Reduced Gravity Analog</p>
<p>Analog system capable of actively offloading the weight of a person (e.g. wearing spacesuit) or hardware to simulate alternate gravitational environments</p>
<p>Extremities still feel the pull of 1g, which can be fatiguing and/or uncomfortable, and provides an imperfect analog which could misinform suit design, etc.</p>
<p>We have developed a passive arm offload device to simulate the desired gravitational environment on the limbs</p>
<p>Now investigating making it “smart” with actuation and sensing</p>
<div class="embedded" id="slide19_rId2"></div>
