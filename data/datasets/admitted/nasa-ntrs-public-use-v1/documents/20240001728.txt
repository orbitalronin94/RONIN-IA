Summary 
Additive manufacturing opens new paths for introducing oxide particles into superalloys, to 



potentially provide oxide dispersion strengthening (ODS) at high temperatures. This provides 
opportunities to study how such dispersions can influence failure modes for a given superalloy, after 
nearly identical material processing paths. The objective of this study was to compare monotonic failure 
modes for additively manufactured superalloys with versus without an introduced oxide dispersion. The 
superalloy for each case was produced by laser powder bed fusion additive manufacturing. Specimens 
were subsequently given consistent thermal treatment paths and then tested in tensile and creep tests at 
varied temperatures. Failure modes were then compared. Additively manufactured NiCrAl without ODS 
had randomly oriented equiaxed grains while this alloy with 0.25 wt% Y2O3 ODS additions had elongated 
[001] oriented grains in the build direction. Material made without ODS (“No-ODS”) was nearly
completely single phase gamma, while ODS additions resulted in gamma, chromium carbide, and
Y-Al-Cr oxide phases, with carbide and oxide phases contents estimated at no more than 13 and 1 vol%,
respectively. Compared to No-ODS, material made with ODS had higher tensile strengths at room,
760 and 1093 °C. The elongation of ODS material was lower than No-ODS at room temperature, but
higher at 760 °C. At 1093 °C ODS material had higher elongation than No-ODS when tested in the build
direction, however elongation was lower for ODS material in the transverse direction at 1093 °C. Creep
rupture strain, life, and area reduction at 760 and 1093 °C were all much higher for ODS material
compared to No-ODS; 760 °C rupture life was nearly 100 times longer for ODS material. The presence of
Y in the ODS material significantly improved oxidation resistance.



Introduction 
Oxide dispersion strengthening (ODS) has been proven to improve a superalloy’s creep resistance 



(Ref. 1). A progression of ODS superalloys have been produced through mechanical alloying followed by 
thermo-mechanical processing (Refs. 2 to 4). In mechanical alloying, powders of alloys and oxides are 
repeatedly ground together by harder milling media in attritors or rolling mills. Powder fracturing and 
cold welding occurs to combine the materials and form the alloys. Subsequent consolidation under 
pressure at high temperatures can be followed by multiple hot working and heat treatment steps to 
produce superalloys with a uniform dispersion of oxide strengtheners, often with grains elongated in the

rolling direction to provide superior creep rupture resistance in this direction at high temperatures. Several 
commercial ODS superalloys have evolved through this processing (Ref. 4). Experimental alloys have 
continued down this path in more recent years, some with potential for turbine engine applications 
(Ref. 5). 



Additive manufacturing opens new paths for introducing oxide particles into superalloys to 
potentially provide oxide dispersion strengthening. A variety of deposition approaches can be used to 
build materials layer by layer (Refs. 6 and 7). In powder bed melting, a layer of powder is applied at 
controlled thickness onto a surface or bed, which is then carefully melted. This localized melting can be 
accomplished by rastering a laser beam or an electron beam across the layer of powder in carefully 
controlled patterns. This repetitive sequence is usually performed in inert gas or in a vacuum to prevent 
environmental contamination. Such additive manufacturing processes produce different surface 
roughness, near-surface porosity, cracking, residual stresses, and microstructures from those encountered 
in conventional cast and wrought processing. Subsequent processing such as sintering, hot isostatic 
pressing, heat treatment, and surface machining can be used to address these issues for intended 
applications. Applications of these advanced additive manufacturing processes to some nickel-based 
superalloys have progressed rapidly (Refs. 6 and 7). In certain cited cases, additive manufacturing 
processes, subsequent hot isostatic pressurizations, heat treatments, and surface finishes have evolved 
from coupons to components (Ref. 8). 



Additive manufacturing provides new opportunities to study how such oxide dispersions can 
influence microstructure and failure modes for a given superalloy. In one approach, attritor milling has 
been employed to introduce yttrium oxide (Y2O3) powder particles into atomized powder of a model 
superalloy (Ref. 9). In another approach, acoustic mixing has been used to introduce Y2O3 into atomized 
powder of multiple principal element superalloys (Refs. 10 and 11). 



The objective of this study was to compare monotonic failure modes for an additively manufactured 
superalloy with and without ODS. Monotonic mechanical properties and failure modes were assessed for 
a simple NiCrAl model superalloy with and without an oxide dispersion, produced by laser powder bed 
fusion. Mechanical tests were performed for initial screening of NiCrAl monotonic tensile and creep 
rupture failure modes. The additively manufactured microstructures and prevalent failure modes for the 
ODS alloy were then considered in contrast to those for comparably processed material with the added 
oxide dispersion. 



Materials and Procedures 
Ni-20Cr-5Al and Ni-20Cr argon-atomized powder of comparable diameter were mechanically 



alloyed using attritor milling with Y2O3 powder to attain powder having an average composition of 
Ni-20Cr-2Al-0.6Y2O3. The milled powder was selectively screened to have particle diameters between 
40 and 100 μm. Argon-atomized Ni-20Cr-2Al powder at 5 to 50 μm diameter was obtained from Powder 
Alloy Corporation, Loveland, Ohio for additively manufacturing material without the yttria particles. 
Selective laser melting was used to fabricate mechanical test blanks of each material at Quadrus 
Corporation, Huntsville, Alabama using a Concept Laser M1 laser powder bed fusion system. This 
system used a build plate of superalloy 718 to build 25 μm-thick layers in a controlled argon atmosphere 
containing no greater than 0.1 percent O2. Four build sets were produced with consistent build parameters 
and configurations. Hot isostatic pressurization and solution heat treatment at 1185 °C were applied to 
No-ODS and ODS specimen blanks oriented in the build direction (B) and transverse to this direction (T), 
by Bodycote Corporation, Cincinnati, Ohio. 



NASA/TM-20240001728 2











Microstructures were examined from specimens prepared parallel and transverse to the build’s 
upward Z direction. Optical and scanning electron microscopy were employed to examine grain and 
precipitate microstructures, and x-ray diffraction was used to assess grain texture. Samples were polished 
using SiC grit polishing paper followed by a 0.05 μm colloidal silica finish. To enhance optical imaging 
of grain boundaries, a waterless Kalling’s etchant consisting of 100 mL hydrochloric acid, 100 mL 
methanol, and 5 g cupric chloride was applied with a swab. Line intercept methods were used for 
measuring average grain size. 



Tensile tests were performed by Metcut Research Associates, Cincinnati, Ohio per ASTM E21-17. 
This utilized an axial extensometer and resistance heating furnace in tests at 25, 760, and 1093 °C. The 
tests were performed at a displacement rate producing a strain rate of approximately 0.5 percent per min 
up to 2 percent strain. Tests were then continued at a faster displacement rate giving a strain rate of 
approximately 5 percent per min to fracture. Creep rupture tests were also performed by Metcut Research 
Associates, per ASTM E139-11. A lever arm system applied axial load with an axial extensometer and 
resistance heating furnace at test temperatures of 760 and 1093 °C. 



Microstructures of metallographically prepared specimen sections were evaluated using a Hitachi 
4700 field emission scanning electron microscope operated at 15 kV and 15 μA with an integrated Oxford 
energy dispersive spectroscopy system. Fracture surfaces were examined using optical microscopy and 
scanning electron microscopy (Jeol 6400) on fracture surfaces, as well as their metallographic sections 
prepared parallel to the loading direction. X-ray diffraction was performed with a PANalytical Empirean 
Series 2 system using Cu Ka radiation to assess crystallographic texture of specimen cross sections. X-ray 
diffraction was performed D8 Advance diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation to identify phases, first from 
specimen sections and then from phases electrochemically extracted from specimen sections. Electron 
backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was performed on a Tescan MAIA3 field emission scanning electron 
microscope at 20 kV accelerating voltage with an integrated Oxford energy dispersive spectroscopy 
system. Aztec software was used to collect and analyze the EBSD data. The total area scanned varied 
slightly due to adjustments in collection time, but on average was 0.9 by 0.7 mm, with a step size of 
0.8 µm. 



Results 
Composition/Microstructure 



Average composition in weight percent as measured by inductively coupled plasma optical emission 
spectrometry and LECO combustion is listed in Table I. Typical grain and phase structures normal to the 
test specimens’ loading direction are shown in Figure 1. The typical grain structures varied between ODS 
and No-ODS materials in terms of preferred orientation and size. No-ODS materials had equiaxed grains 
measuring 29.1 and 27.1 μm in average width for specimens loaded parallel and transverse to the build 
direction, respectively, each with random texture. ODS material had elongated grains in the building 
direction oriented in the [001] crystallographic direction. Directional grains are clearly evident in ODS 
material, resulting in highly variant linear intercept measurements for specimens loaded transverse to the 
build direction. Grain width here averaged 22.0 μm. For ODS specimens loaded parallel to the building 
direction, the sectioned grains had a more consistent linear intercept distance, averaging 21.1 μm. 



Pole figures generated using x-ray diffraction and electron backscatter diffraction are compared for 
No-ODS and ODS specimens in Figure 2 and Figure 3. No-ODS specimens had random texture for 
specimens loaded in the build direction and transverse to the build direction, when analyzed by EBSD and 
XRD. In contrast, ODS specimens had preferred texture for specimens loaded in the build direction and 
transverse to the build direction, when analyzed by EBSD and XRD. There is excellent correspondence

between the EBSD and XRD pole figure features. Slight differences between the pole figures from EBSD 
and XRD can likely be attributed to sample area. The EBSD data were collected from a smaller area than

Phase contents also differed between No-ODS and ODS materials. No-ODS was made up of a single 
gamma phase. ODS material also had another major phase, along with the gamma phase and oxide 
dispersion. ODS material had both an oxide phase and a chromium carbide phase present. These phases 
are discriminated in the x-ray dot maps of Figure 4, which were generated by energy dispersive 
spectroscopy on metallographically polished, unetched specimens prepared parallel and transverse to the 
building direction. Average compositions of particles over 1 μm in diameter for each phase are included 
in Table II. The major second phase in ODS material indicated by “Cr” in Figure 4 contained a very high 
Cr level, along with O and C. The oxide dispersion indicated by “O” in Figure 4 was rich in O, Al, Y, 
with lower Cr and Ni. X-ray diffraction indicated the Cr rich second phase was M7C3 carbides. 
ThermoCalc (Ref. 12) was employed to assess potential carbide phases during equilibrium cooling for 
each material, using the average compositions measured by ICP. ThermoCalc projected at least 10 times 
more M7C3 carbide phase was likely for the ODS material than for No-ODS material, due to the much 
higher C level for ODS, as depicted in Figure 5. Image analyses indicated the carbides took up 13 percent 
of the area in typical images of unetched specimen sections. Due to their extremely fine size range, it was 
not possible to rigorously measure the area percent of the oxide dispersion with optical or scanning 
electron microscope images. Based on the acquired images as presented here (Figure 1), the oxide 
dispersion content was estimated to be below 1 area percent. 



Failure Responses 
Tensile Failure Response 



Typical surfaces of tensile fractures at room temperature for specimens loaded parallel and transverse 
to the building direction are shown in Figure 6. Ultimate tensile strength and elongation are listed in the 
top right corner for each image of the overall fracture, and tabulated in Table III. Ultimate tensile strength 
was significantly higher for the ODS materials loaded in the build and in the transverse directions in 
comparison to No-ODS. Elongation was reduced to half of No-ODS values. No-ODS had ductile cup-
cone failures, with microvoid coalescence (MVC) failure across grains. ODS failures had also had ductile 
failures with smaller shear rims, for the build (B) direction and transverse (T) direction. 



Typical surfaces of tensile fractures at 760 °C for specimens loaded parallel and transverse to the 
building direction are compared in Figure 7. Ultimate tensile strength was again significantly higher for 
the ODS materials loaded in the build and in the transverse directions. Elongation of ODS at 760 °C was 
significantly increased in comparison to No-ODS values. In the build direction, the ratio of elongations 
for ODS/No-ODS was 19, while in the transverse direction this ratio was 4. Corresponding increases of

reduction in area were present, as listed in Table III. This is readily apparent in the images of these 
fracture surfaces, all shown at the same magnification. No-ODS failures had nonplanar intergranular (IG) 
failures both internally and near the surface at low strains. ODS failures varied with the loading direction: 
specimens loaded in the build direction had MVC, while those loaded transverse to this direction had 
curious textured failures. 



Longitudinal sections metallographically prepared on a plane parallel to the loading direction of these 
tensile fractures at 760 °C are shown in Figure 8. These sections indicated the No-ODS material failures 
initiated from surface and internal grain boundaries. The curious ODS textured failures occurred along the 
boundaries between elongated grains. While some evidence of these failures was observed in specimens 
loaded in the build direction, they were most influential for transverse (T) specimens. 



Typical surfaces of tensile fractures at 1093 °C for specimens loaded parallel and transverse to the 
building direction are compared in Figure 9. Ultimate tensile strength for the ODS materials was about 
twice that of No-ODS materials, loaded parallel or transverse to the build direction. When loaded parallel 
to the building direction, elongation of ODS was about 5 times that of No-ODS. Yet when loaded 
transverse to the building direction, ODS material had lower elongation than No-ODS. No-ODS had less 
dominant intergranular cracking at 1093 °C than for 760 °C. ODS failures in the building direction were 
from MVC, but the transverse direction again had the dominant unidirectional textured failures. 



Cross sections metallographically prepared parallel to the vertical loading direction of tensile 
fractures at 1093 °C were evaluated to further examine these trends. Typical images are shown in 
Figure 10. No-ODS failures were confirmed to initiate from intergranular failures throughout the 
specimen cross section. ODS failures in the building direction were ductile from MVC, but transverse 
specimens were again dominated by the failures of boundaries between elongated grains. 



Creep Rupture Response 



Typical surfaces of creep rupture fractures at 760 °C for specimens loaded parallel and transverse to 
the building direction are shown in Figure 11. Applied stress, rupture life, and creep rupture strain are 
listed in the top right corner of each image at lowest magnification of the overall fracture surface, and 
tabulated in Table III. Rupture life was nearly 100 times higher for the ODS material loaded in the build 
direction and transverse direction in comparison to No-ODS. Creep rupture strain was similarly increased 
for ODS over No-ODS materials loaded in the build direction. This gave an analogous difference in 
reduction in area for the two materials, also evident in Figure 11. However, for loading transverse to the 
build direction ODS creep rupture strain was only 2 times that of No-ODS. 



The fractures surfaces showed that creep rupture at 760 °C of No-ODS specimens occurred by 
intergranular failure for the build (B) direction and transverse (T) direction. ODS ruptures were highly 
divergent. Specimens loaded in the build direction failed by microvoid coalescence, while specimens 
loaded transverse to the build direction had the textured failures. 



Cross sections metallographically prepared parallel to the vertical loading direction of creep rupture 
fractures at 760 °C were also evaluated. Typical images are shown in Figure 12. Creep ruptures of No-
ODS specimens occurred with failures of individual grain boundaries normal to the load. ODS ruptures 
had cavities at grain boundaries forming and joining to form cracks. This appeared accentuated for 
specimens loaded transverse to the building direction, enhanced at the boundaries between elongated grains. 



Typical surfaces of creep rupture fractures at 1093 °C for specimens loaded parallel and transverse to 
the building direction are compared in Figure 13. Rupture life was far higher for the ODS material loaded 
in the build direction and transverse direction in comparison to No-ODS. This necessitated running the 
ODS specimens at a far higher stress level of 21 MPa than the 11 MPa applied to No-ODS specimens. At 
these stresses, rupture life for specimens loaded in the build direction of ODS material was still about 5

times that of No-ODS material. This was not the case for ODS material loaded in the direction transverse 
to building, where rupture life at 21 MPa for ODS was lower than life at 11 MPa for No-ODS. Rupture 
strain was moderately reduced for ODS material in comparison to No-ODS material loaded parallel to the 
build direction. The reduction was greater for specimens loaded transverse to the build direction. 



The fracture surfaces of Figure 13 and cross sections of their metallographically prepared sections in 
Figure 14 show that No-ODS material suffered excessive oxidation at the exposed surface during the 
creep rupture tests. This produced a surface layer of radial oxide crystals having a typical composition of 
30 O-30 Cr-38 Ni-2.4 Al in weight percent, as measured by energy dispersive spectroscopy in the 
scanning electron microscope. Simultaneous intergranular cavitation and failure is evident throughout the 
cross section. The ODS material showed far less of this oxidation at the surface. ODS specimens also 
displayed intergranular cavitation and failure throughout their cross sections. 



Discussion 
Processing-Microstructures 



Oxide Dispersion: A fine dispersion of oxide particles was identified in the ODS material. These 
particles were present throughout the microstructure, both within grains and at grain boundaries. It is 
illuminating that the oxide particles did not contain only yttrium and oxygen as in the input yttria powder. 
Their composition, as estimated using relatively large particles for sufficient excitation volume, also 
contained Al, Cr, and Ni, as listed in Table II. This suggests the input yttria powder particles were melted 
during additive manufacturing (Ref. 10), or formed through interdiffusion (Ref. 13) with the surrounding 
NiCrAl matrix to produce the measured oxide particles. Peak metal temperatures generated by the laser 
could not be estimated at this time, but these could help determine which alternative path prevailed. The 
melting path could be consistent with the general presence of oxide particles both within grains and near 
grain boundaries. Their general presence also suggests that these particles were not overly effective at 
pinning growing grain boundaries during processing of the tested material. 



Grain Structures: After the use of attritor milling to add in the oxide dispersion, comparable additive 
manufacturing, hot isostatic pressurization, and heat treatment of No-ODS and ODS materials produced 
surprisingly different grain structures. Unlike the equiaxed grains and random texture of No-ODS 
material, ODS material had grains near [001] orientation preferentially growing in the building direction. 
More refined analyses of this grain texture in ODS materials are necessary, and this is currently 
underway. [001] texture in the building direction has been reported for many other additively manufacture 
superalloys (Refs. 6 and 7), and this could also be influenced by the scan line orientations during additive 
manufacturing. Further evaluations are underway to assess this potential relationship. 



Carbides: The major second phase of M7C3 carbides present in ODS material was not expected. The 
presence of this second phase in significant quantity of 13 area percent complicated assessments of 
changes specifically due to the oxide dispersion. The carbon contamination apparently came from attritor 
milling, as has been previously reported for some steels (Ref. 14). In the present study, stearic acid 
containing C was used as a lubricant during attritor milling, and the level of this addition could be 
reduced in future trials. The presence of this much carbide phase in the form of films along grain 
boundaries has been shown in the past to reduce ductility in many superalloys (Ref. 15). However, 
carbide films could not be located along the ODS grain boundaries. Instead, the observed carbides were 
discrete particles located both along grain boundaries and within grains. Average size of the carbides was 
larger for those at the grain boundaries than for those within grains. Other methods are currently being 
screened to mix in the present oxide dispersion while not introducing such high carbon and carbide levels, 
to help understand the effects of these carbides.

Failure Responses 



The predominance of microvoid coalescence within grains in tensile test failures at room temperature 
indicated grain interiors were weaker than grain boundaries in these tests for both No-ODS and ODS 
specimens oriented in the build direction and transverse to the build direction. In these failures, the 
reduced grain width and preferential orientation of grains identified for ODS material apparently played a 
role, with ductility reduced to half that observed in No-ODS materials. It is not clear that the carbides in 
ODS material had detrimental influences in these failures, as carbides were not prominent on these 
fracture surfaces. Yet, one could envision scenarios where the introduced oxides and carbides of ODS 
material provided more initiation points for the microvoids. Nevertheless, these particles were not often 
identified within the microvoids on the fracture surface or in metallographic sections to confirm such 
scenarios. 



The substantial divergences of failure responses in tensile and creep rupture tests at 760 °C indicated 
grain boundaries in both No-ODS and ODS materials were more often weaker than the grains at this 
temperature. No-ODS materials failed preferentially at grain boundaries throughout the specimen volume, 
when loaded in the building direction or transverse to the building direction in tensile tests at high stresses 
and strain rates plus creep rupture tests at lower stresses and strain rates. These failures occurred at over 
10 times lower inelastic strains than observed at room temperature, indicating more limited tensile and 
creep rupture ductilities. These failures may be related to the very low carbon level in No-ODS material 
and the resulting scarcity of carbides to strengthen these boundaries. Tests of this material with higher 
carbon levels for increased carbide content would be necessary to explore this consideration. 



The failures of ODS materials in these tests at 760 °C, with striking contrasts for loading in the 
building direction versus transverse to the building direction, disclosed the benefits and limitations of this 
directional grain structure and corresponding grains/grain boundaries. Grains and grain boundaries loaded 
in the building direction could withstand very high inelastic strains, with grain interiors eventually failing 
rather than grain boundaries. However, the grain boundaries oriented and loaded transverse to the 
building direction withstood far lower inelastic strains before their failure in tensile and creep rupture 
tests. Directional grain structures parallel to the building direction have been reported for other additively 
manufacture superalloys to give directional mechanical properties (Ref. 4) including lower ductility for 
loading transverse to the elongated grains. Nevertheless, the drastic differences in ductility and failure 
modes observed here are striking. The presence of largest carbides at the grain boundaries between 
elongated grains in the building direction could help explain this. As shown for the longitudinal sections 
in Figure 8 and Figure 12, these grain boundaries preferentially failed with cracks adjacent to such large 
carbides, even for specimens loaded in the building direction. Grain boundary cohesion could be impaired 
by these large carbides. 



The failure responses for tensile and creep rupture loading at 1093 °C in some respects continued the 
trends observed at 760 °C. No-ODS materials continued to preferentially fail at grain boundaries for both 
tests. However, in long term creep rupture tests excessive oxidation attack completely covered the 
specimen surface. In this attack, ODS materials clearly performed much better than No-ODS with greatly 
reduced depths of attack. The improvements in oxidation resistance of yttria-containing superalloys have 
been well documented over many years (Ref. 16), and this trend has carried over into the present 
materials. 



The reasons for higher tensile strength observed for ODS over No-ODS material at all three of the 
testing temperatures could not be fully explained based only through evaluations of failure modes. It 
would be expected that these benefits could chiefly be related to different grain structure, carbide content, 
and the oxide dispersion. The higher creep rupture lives observed for ODS over No-ODS material at the 
two creep rupture testing temperatures can likewise be related to changed grain structures, carbide 



NASA/TM-20240001728 7











contents, and the oxide dispersion. Additional evaluations of strengthening mechanisms and creep 
deformation mechanisms would be necessary to complement their failure mechanisms in order to confirm 
which microstructural features and mechanisms were dominant for different loading conditions. As a 
weakening mechanism, long term oxidation was an influential damage mode that clearly improved for 
ODS over No-ODS materials in creep rupture tests at 1093 °C. Fully understanding the competition 
between these strengthening and damage mechanisms would require tensile and creep rupture tests to be 
interrupted before failure at lower strains. These specimens could be evaluated with electron backscatter 
diffraction evaluations in a scanning electron microscope and dislocation/stacking fault evaluations in a 
transmission electron microscope. 



Summary and Conclusions 
NiCrAl powder metallurgy superalloy was prepared without and with an oxide dispersion using 



selective laser melting of a powder bed. Despite consistent subsequent hot isostatic pressurization plus 
solution heat treatment, the introduction of the oxide dispersion caused markedly different 
microstructures and phases. Introduction of the oxide dispersion improved tensile strength by 20 to 100 
percent in tests from room temperature to 1093 °C. Tensile failure modes at elevated temperatures of 
ODS in the building direction became more ductile via microvoid coalescence. Corresponding tensile 
failure modes of ODS in the transverse direction became less ductile with enhanced failures along 
boundaries between elongated grains at high temperatures. Addition of the oxide dispersion improved 
creep rupture lives at 760 and 1093 °C. Rupture failure modes of ODS in the build direction became more 
ductile via microvoid coalescence at 760 °C, and cavitation of grain boundaries at 1093 °C. 
Corresponding creep rupture failure modes of ODS in the transverse direction appeared less ductile with 
enhanced failures along the boundaries between the elongated grains. Oxidation was noticeably reduced 
in rupture tests of ODS material. 



Several conclusions can currently be drawn from this evaluation: 
 



1. Attritor milling offers a convenient avenue for combining powder of different compositions to 
produce a new experimental composition, optionally including oxide dispersion particles for additive 
manufacturing. However, the carbon content needs to be carefully regulated. 
2. Introduction of oxide dispersions into powder before additive manufacturing, hot isostatic 
pressurization, and heat treatment can result in some unexpected changes in the microstructures of 
superalloys. 
3. Tensile loading: Introduction of an oxide dispersion as done here can improve strength and encourage 
divergent failure modes to dominate when loading in the build versus transverse directions. 



a. Build Direction—Ductile transgranular failure via microvoid coalescence. 
b. Transverse Direction—Less ductile intergranular failure along the boundaries between elongated 
grains perpendicular to the applied load. 



4. Creep rupture loading—This introduction of an oxide dispersion can improve life, though still with a 
divergence in failure modes between the build and transverse directions. 



a. Build Direction—Ductile microvoid coalescence at intermediate temperatures, grain boundary 
cavitation at high temperatures. 
b. Transverse Direction—Less ductile intergranular failure along the boundaries of elongated grains 
normal to the applied load. 



5. Introduction of the present oxide dispersion can significantly improve the oxidation resistance of a 
superalloy during long-term loading in either direction.
