Abstract 



Dust particles and occasional moisture condensations are unavoidable reality of all wind tunnels. On the 



path to pursue a goal of velocity and temperature measurements in high-speed wind tunnels we have 



created a tabletop, spectrally resolved, Rayleigh-Mie scattering setup around a small jet fed by ambient 



and air with different particle concentration to determine the accuracy of the technique. The other reality 



of a wind tunnel setup is the spurious reflection and scattering of the incident laser beam by solid surfaces 



(flare light), which contaminates the Rayleigh-Mie scattered light. This is simulated by backgrounds with 



different reflectivity towards the collection optics. Light from a CW laser is delivered via an optical fiber 



and the scattered light from a point on the laser path is spectrally resolved using a stabilized Fabry-Perot 



interferometer, followed by imaging on an EMCCD camera. A model of the combined background glare, 



Mie scattering, and the Rayleigh spectrum was fitted to the camera image using maximum likelihood 



estimation. It was observed that the present modeling approach can extract velocity and temperature with 



reasonable accuracy when the intensity of the Mie scattered light is less than or comparable to that of the 



Rayleigh scattered light; beyond that error in velocity measurement remains reasonable ±12m/s but the 



temperature measurement becomes progressively more inaccurate. Increasingly the flare light is found to 



cause a bias error in velocity. Similar trend is observed in the presence of both the flare light and the Mie 



scattered light. The setup has provided a set of data for the future improvement of the modeling procedure, 



and demonstrated that even in the case of large amount of dust particles and large flare light the present 



technique can provide measurement of velocity with reasonable accuracy. 



 



I. INTRODUCTION 



Transonic and supersonic wind tunnels lack viable tools to measure basic quantities such as velocity, temperature, 



and density, particularly when local shock waves are present. The lack of experimental data makes computational 



fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations as the sole provider of many aerodynamic information. However, CFD cannot 



fill the gap alone as the computational codes require experimental data for validation. Out of the many emerging 



optical techniques that are attempting to provide these information, molecular Rayleigh scattering is a promising 



candidate due to the relatively large signal levels, the appeal of a complete measurement of kinematic (velocity u) 



and thermodynamic properties (temperature T, and density ϱ) , and the affordability of the opto-electronics. 



Commercially available, particle-based PIV have found applications in large wind tunnels [1] , still contamination 



by the seed particles is an issue for test facilities. Additionally, particle-based techniques are unreliable in shock 



containing flows. Various molecular tagging velocimetry (MTV) techniques such as FLEET [2], use specialized 



and expensive pulsed lasers to ionize some gas molecules and follow emissions from these molecules to measure 



flow velocity. Coherent Anti-Stokes Raman Spectroscopy (CARS) based technique have been used to measure 



temperature and standard deviation of temperature fluctuations [3]. The Rayleigh-scattering based technique is 



non-intrusive and does not require injection of seed particles. Among the Rayleigh based techniques there exist 



variations, which can be broadly divided into two categories based on the means of resolving the Rayleigh 



spectrum [4]. For the spectrally resolved technique an interferometer, such as a Fabry-Perot [5-10] is used to 



completely resolve the Rayleigh spectrum. For the filtered Rayleigh scattering technique a molecular aborption 











2 
 



filter, such as the hyperfine absorption lines of iodine vapors [11, 12] is used to convert the spectrum into light 



intensity. The spectrally resolved path requires an extensive modeling of the measured spectra to extract the 



velocity and temperature information. There exist multiple challenges for wind tunnel implementation of the 



Rayleigh technique: contamination by the naturally present dust particles, scattering of incidence beam by 



windows and beam dump (flare light), vibration of the test facilities, and limitations imposed on the velocity 



component that can be measured via the available optical access ports in the facility. The motivation of the 



present study is to find means to overcome the first two difficulties. The flare light and the Mie scattered light 



adds to the burden of correct modeling. The goals of this work are to collect a set of data with different levels of 



Mie and flare light, evaluate the accuracy of the present model to extract the underlying Rayleigh-Brillouin 



spectrum and to help with the further development of the model. 



 



Note that despite the above challenges the spectrally resolved Rayleigh has a proven record of measuring 



spectrum of u/, T/, ϱ/, shear stresses such as ϱ/u/u/, and correlations such as < ϱ/- u/> , < u/- T/>, and more 



importantly two-point statistics in relatively clean flows [13, 14,15]. The present work only attempts to evaluate 



uncertainties with the time-averaged measurement of velocity and temperature, which by themselves can become 



valuable tools in high-speed wind tunnel applications.

A simplistic description of the Rayleigh spectrum in the presence of the background flare and particle (Mie) 



scattering, are schematically shown in Fig. 1. Fig 1(a) helps to recapitulate the basics of the Rayleigh spectrum: 



The electric field of the incident laser light polarizes individual gas molecules, which in turn scatter a very small 



part of that light. The moving gas molecules create a Doppler shift in the scattered light. Airflow, such as that in a 



wind tunnel, can be thought of as a combination of a bulk motion at the measured Mach number plus a 



superimposed random velocity of the individual molecules. Therefore, the Doppler-shifted scattered-light is 



spectrally broadened with a distinct peak. The separation between the peak of the incident laser line and the peak 



of the Rayleigh spectrum provides a measure of the bulk velocity. The full width at half maximum (FWHM) 



depends on the spread of molecular motion and therefore is a measure of gas temperature, and the area under the 



Rayleigh spectrum, i.e., the intensity of the scattered light is proportional to the gas density. In the presence of 



background glare from the laser beam, the Rayleigh scattered light is mixed with light at the laser frequency (Fig. 



1b) and can be easily identified once the laser frequency is known. Most of the naturally born particles and the 



condensates are small are expected to follow the bulk motion of the flow. In fact, Mie scattering from the dust 



particles clearly marks the central peak of a Rayleigh spectrum. Fig 1(c) shows the spectrum in presence of both 



background glare and particles in the air flow.

present work are to create a setup to collect such images, followed by an analysis to determine local velocity and 



temperature inside a jet, and a parametric study using progressively increasing background glare and particle 



contents to determine the accuracy of velocity and temperature measurements.

II. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 



IIa. Optical setup 



Because the Doppler shift is of the order of 109 Hz (2.66MHz per m/s of flow velocity for 90˚ scattering using 



532nm light), which is far smaller than the optical frequency (5.6x1014 Hz for 532nm light in air), there is a need 



to use a narrow linewidth laser source and a hyperfine spectroscope, such as a Fabry-Perot interferometer (FPI). 



Fig 4 shows the optical arrangement around a small free jet. There are two aspects of this setup that needs to be 



mentioned at the outset. In a wind tunnel application, an important question is how to deliver the laser. Passing 



the beam through any window, even when coated to reduce reflection, produces excessive background flare that 



may swamp the Rayleigh-Mie signal. Unlike many other optical techniques, we use a CW laser, which allows for 



transmission through optical fibers. Delivery of laser light through an optical fiber will reduce the glare. So, we 



decided to test such a delivery system for the present setup. The downside of using fibers is transmission loss 



(~40%), and a scrambling of the polarization axis, which effectively reduces the Rayleigh scattered light towards 



the collection direction by half. The second aspect is the choice between direct imaging of the probe volume 



versus the use of an optical fiber for passing the collected scattered light. Our previous work [6] used direct 



imaging of the probe volume through FPI on a camera. In the presence of dust particles, it becomes difficult to 



separate the Rayleigh and Mie scattered signal in the direct imaging technique. Therefore, the other option of 



imaging the probe volume on an optical fiber was used. The optical fiber scrambled the scattered light from 



different parts of the probe volume, and presented a uniformly illuminated circular face for imaging through FPI. 



Such images provided radial segregation between the Rayleigh scattered, Mie scattered, and the background flare 



light (Fig 2). The downside of using an optical fiber for the transmission of the scattered light is a further loss of 



photons. 



 



 The optical layout can be divided into five groups: (a) The laser source and the delivery of light to the flow, (b) 



optics to collect Rayleigh-Mie scattered light, (c) the spectroscopic arrangement, (d) optics to deliver a small part 



of the incident laser light also called as the reference light, and (e) stabilization arrangement. The 532 nm, single-



mode, incident beam of the first group was produced from a frequency-doubled, Nd:YVO4 solid state, Coherent 



Verdi laser that emitted a maximum of 10.5W power. The vendor provided a specification of 5MHz for the 



linewidth. The light was coupled to a 50micron core diameter fiber. At the output end the light was collimated by 



a 10.9mm focal length aspheric lens and passed through the air stream from a small jet. To reduce the flare light 



off the aspheric lens a hollow conical baffle was used to house the fiber end and the lens. This baffle was removed 



to increase the flare light content in the Rayleigh-Mie scattered light. 



 



In the second group scattered light from 1.35mm long probe volume was collected by a pair of 75mm diameter 



achromats with f/# 4 and 2.67, and focused on a 900micron core diameter fiber, which in turn, delivered light to 



the spectroscopic arrangement - the third group. The spectroscopic arrangement first collimated the light delivered 



by the fiber by a 50mm f/#2 achromatic lens. The collimated light was passed through air-spaced, planar, 30mm 



diameter, 90 % reflectivity FPI mirrors with a free-spectral range of 8GHz. The optical cavity of the stabilized 



Fabry-Perot, supplied by Lightmachinery, was actively controlled by three piezo-scanned support points and three 



strain gauges that provided position sensing. Once aligned, the closed-loop control maintained the parallelism of 



the two mirrors through extraneous environmental factors such as thermal drift, vibration, and noise. This was 



particularly beneficial for long-duration data collection and eliminated the need for any manual adjustment. The 



actively controlled interferometer simplified the day-to-day alignment so much that the laser can be turned on any 



day, and nicely formed fringes with little degradation of the finesse can be readily visible. Finally, the 



interferometer output was imaged through a 300mm focal length fringe-forming lens on a low-light, cooled, 



EMCCD camera. Baffles and curtains were placed around the spectroscopic setup to further reduce contamination 



from stray light. 



 



For the alignment of the spectroscopic setup and for determining the instrument function, a small part of the 



incident laser light needed to be passed through the interferometer – this made the fourth group. To achieve this 



goal, a small amount of light scattered by the fiber coupler was collected and passed to an Integrating sphere via 











7 
 



another optical fiber, marked as fiber carrying reference light in fig 4. The IS was mounted on a linear actuator, 



which allowed placement of an opening of the IS in front of the collection fiber. When the IS was placed in front 



of the collection fiber, the Rayleigh scattered light was blocked and the reference light from the incident beam 



passed through the interferometric setup. 



 



The fifth and the final group is the stabilization arrangement to correct for the slow drift of the laser frequency. 



Despite the extensive use of active temperature control of the laser head some amount of slow drift caused by the 



changes in the ambient temperature is inevitable. Such drifts create error in velocity measurement and makes 



identification of the background glare difficult. One of the mirrors in the laser cavity was mounted on PZT stacks. 



When supplied with an external DC voltage the PZT-actuated mirror allows for frequency tuning over a small 



range. To monitor the drift in frequency of the laser the reference light from the integration sphere was sent to the 



stabilization arrangement via another optical fiber. There the light was collimated and passed through a solid 



etalon and then to a CCD camera. The camera images were continuously analyzed to determine change in the 



fringe diameter. A PID based controller in a LabVIEW environment determined the DC voltage needed to supply 



to the PZT stack to correct for the drift. A 0-100V DC supply provided this voltage to the stack. At the time of 



writing this paper the stabilization arrangement was used to monitor the drift in the laser frequency and to decide 



the amount of warm up time needed before collection of the Rayleigh data.

IIb. Modelling of the Fabry-Perot fringes: 



The interferometric measurement was a two-step process. The first step involved characterizing the instrument 



function, for which the Rayleigh-scattered light was blocked, and a tiny amount of the incident laser light 



(reference light) was passed through the FPI setup via actuating the IS. This provided the reference image Iref (fig. 



5a). During the next step, the IS was moved out and the Rayleigh-scattered light was imaged through the FPI, 



providing the Rayleigh image IRal (fig. 6a). Accordingly, there were two parts to the modelling: extracting the 



instrument function IFP from the Reference image and using the instrument function to extract the Rayleigh-



Brillouin spectra SR(x, y) from the Rayleigh image. Each step was performed via nonlinear least-square curve fits 



that minimized the difference between the measured image and the modelled image for a selected set of 



parameters. Because the images were made up of a set of pixels i = 1, 2, 3, … npix, the modeling was performed 



for all such pixels, and the least-square was calculated as the norm of the differences between the measured and 



the modelled image. To determine the angle θi created by each pixel to the optical axis, the center of the fringes 



(χc, ζc), and the radius rp of the innermost fringe needed to be determined (fig 5a). Therefore, parameters extracted 



from the reference image are χc, ζc, rp and the finesse (N) of the interferometer. Notice that this combination also 



fixes the incidence angle θp (equation 12) at the fringe location, and ultimately specifies the variation of the phase 



ψ over the entire image plane for the incident light frequency. The model for the reference image included the

IIIa. Effect of increasing Mie scattering from particles: 



To vary the particle content in the jet stream air from two different sources were supplied to the jet. The shop air 



used in our experiment was found to be cleaner than the ambient due to multiple filters both at the inlet and the 



outlet of the air compressor, and an oil separator and a chiller used at the outlet. Air from this source is called as 



“Cleaner air.” A second compressor without any filter was the second source of air. Fine droplets of lubrication 



oil added to the naturally born particles and significantly increased the particle content in this “Dirtier air.” To 



further increase the particle content to the level of condensation mists in the wind tunnel couple of incense sticks 



were burnt at the inlet of the compressor without any filter. The smoke from the incense sticks farther increased 



the particle counts in this dirtiest case, also referred as “Dirtier air + Incense” air, which created a visible glare on 



the laser path passing through the jet. Figure 7 shows the particle counts from all the above operations in a 



tabular form and as a histogram. The jet velocity was changed for 0 to 290m/s and was measured by a small Pitot 



tube placed at the location of the probe volume. The total pressure measured from the Pitot tube and the total 



temperature from a Kiel probe was used in the isentropic relationship to determine the jet properties. The very 



small jet diameter made the perfect juxtaposition of the Pitot tube at the laser probe volume difficult. This may be 



the cause of the slight bias error in velocity and temperature seen in the data presented. The Pitot tube was placed 



below the laser probe volume. The jet was mounted on a vertical traverse which moved the jet between the 



location of the laser probe volume and that of the Pitot tube. 



The model for the Rayleigh image (equation 19) provided a measure of the intensity of the Rayleigh light (IR), 



particle scattered light (IPart) and that of the flare light ILas. These values were extracted for each data point (see the 



heading of Fig. 6) and were used to quantify the respective scattering intensities. The ratio of the intensity of the 



Mie scattered light to that of the Rayleigh light IPart/IRal was varied from 0.0001 to 6. For IPart/IRal <1, the expected 



range for naturally present dust particles, it was observed that the model function was able to extract velocity 



within an accuracy of ±8m/s (our data had a bias error of 6m/s), and temperature of ±5K (our data had a bias error 



of 5K). For more intense Mie scattering IPart/IRal > 1, such as that would be encountered in the presence of some 



condensation in the air stream, the uncertainty in velocity increased to ±12m/s – still a reasonable value, but the 



temperature error increased enormously. In other words, it was found that the present spectrally measured 



technique can provide good measurement of velocity in the presence of any number of particles and condensation, 



but good measurement of temperature can be obtained only up to an extent of particle contamination. Further 



improvements may be obtained via refinements of the model function such as by accounting for the turbulent 



dispersion of the particle spectrum. In addition, the particle size may also have created uncertainty in the 



measurement.

IIIb. Effect of increasing flare light: 



An efficient use of the baffles and beam stops minimized the flare light in the optical setup. Such data are referred 



as “small flare.” A white card was placed across from the jet and facing the collection optics to increase the flare 



light. Such data points are referred as “increased flare.” Finally, the baffle around the aspheric lens of the delivery 



fiber was removed to significantly increase the flare light, and the same white card was used redirect such flare 



light to the collection fiber. Such data points are referred as “high flare.” Figure 9 presents data from these three 



conditions, all obtained from the “Cleaner + ambient” condition of the particle level. The ratio of the intensity of 



the flare light to that of the Rayleigh light ILaser/IRayl was varied from 0.0005 to 3.5. Increasing the flare level is 



found to increase the bias error in velocity measurement, and a larger random error in temperature measurement.

IIIc. Effect of high Mie scattering and flare light: 



When both flare light and particle scattered lights are velocity measurements can be made within ±10m/s accuracy 



(although a couple of data points were found to have higher error), but the temperature measurements become 



very erroneous (Fig 9). An examination of a data point for which ILaser/IRayl=2.5 and ILaser/IRayl = 3.2 (Fig. 10) show

Rayleigh spectrum. More efforts are needed to extract out the Rayleigh spectrum from such a situation. 



IV. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION 



Spectrally resolved Rayleigh scattering is known to provide time-averaged measurement of velocity, temperature 



and density, and spectra of turbulent fluctuations in clean, particle free flows. How to measure the same quantities 



in large wind tunnels and other facilities where there are naturally born dust particles and flare light from stray 



scattering is a problem. The spectrally resolved technique makes a complete measurement of the optical spectrum 



of the scattered light from a probe volume inside a flow. The Mie-scattered light from the dust particles and the 



flare light from the incident laser beam are simply added at distinct frequency bands to the spectrum of Rayleigh-



Brillouin scattering. The extraction of velocity and temperature information is dependent upon sophistication of 



the signal processing methods, and the extent of contamination. To systematically evaluate the accuracy of one 



such models an optical setup was created around a small jet supplied with air with different particle loading. The 



amount of flare light was adjusted by coupling different amount of background scattered light to the collection 



optics. The setup used a narrow line-width CW laser that was passed through the jet. The scattered light from a 



probe volume was collected by a pair of lenses and was coupled to an optical fiber. The fiber passed the light to a 



Fabry-Perot interferometer for spectral analysis and final imaging by a cooled EMCCD camera. A model function 



of the fringes formed by the interferometer was used to extract time averaged velocity and temperature at the 



probe volume. The ratio of the intensity of the Mie scattered light to that of the Rayleigh light IPart/IRal was varied 











14 
 



from 0.001 to 6. For IPart/IRal <1, expected range for naturally present dust particles, it was observed that the model 



function was able to extract velocity within an accuracy of ±6m/s (our data had a bias error of 6m/s), and 



temperature of ±6K (our data had a bias error of 5K). For more intense Mie scattering IPart/IRal > 1, such as that 



would be encountered in the presence of some condensation in the air stream, the uncertainty in velocity increased 



to ±12m/s – still a reasonable value, but the temperature error increased enormously. In other words, it was found 



that the present spectrally measured technique can provide good measurement of velocity in the presence of any 



number of particles and condensation, but good measurement of temperature can be obtained only up to an extent 



of particle contamination. ….
