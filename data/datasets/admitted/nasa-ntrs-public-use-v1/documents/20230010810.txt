<p>PERFORMANCE OF AN ACTIVE VEHICLE STABILIZATION
SYSTEM DURING TOUCHDOWN IN THE PRESENCE OF
</p>
<p>PROPELLANT SLOSH DYNAMICS
</p>
<p>Jing Pei†
</p>
<p>This paper characterizes the effect of propellant slosh on the performance of an
active stabilization control system for the two-two-and-over symmetric landing
mode during touchdown. Generalized impulse and momenta theory is used to
model the instance when the landing leg strikes the ground as an inelastic collision
event. For active stabilization, a rate damping controller is used to stabilize the
attitude of the vehicle via onboard reaction control system. Simulation results are
shown comparing the performance of the control system with and without slosh
dynamics.
</p>
<p>INTRODUCTION
</p>
<p>The landing phase of any planetary mission represents one of the most crucial stages of the
mission. For the Apollo missions, the elapsed time between one of the landing legs initially striking
the lunar surface and the vehicle coming to rest was less than 2 seconds, as discussed in Ref. [1].
The landing gear design must account for local surface conditions and uncertainties in the vehicle
states during touchdown to guard against toppling. Numerous studies of touchdown stability were
published in Refs. [2–8] as a result of the work performed during the Apollo program. Analytical
approaches ranged from using simple two-dimensional inelastic collision theory to six-degree-of-
freedom simulations. Subsequently, the analytical results were verified with sub-scale and full-
scale experiments (Refs. [2–4, 7]). Despite the abundance of literature, few studies detail how to
account for propellant slosh dynamics. Recently, in Ref. [9], the authors built on the foundation
laid down in Ref. [2] and used a compound pendulum attached to a rigid body to model lander
containing sloshing propellant. A two-step procedure was used to characterize the dynamics of a
planar touchdown: 1) A theory involving generalized momenta and generalized impulses described
in Ref. [10] is used to predict the post-collision angular velocities of the vehicle and pendulum; 2)
Equations of motion for the two-degree-of-freedom, post-collision system are solved numerically
to determine whether toppling occurs. Stability boundaries corresponding to the two-and-over and
two-two-and-over landings were then compared for the models with and without slosh.
</p>
<p>The aforementioned literature focus on the passive landing scenario in which the main engine
shuts down prior to touchdown and the inherent passive stability of the vehicle takes over thereafter.
Most lander designs, like Apollo, have a relatively moderate ratio of landing footprint width Hd to

Due to its height and relatively narrow base, the design has a reduced ratio of Hd to H suggesting

requirements and prevent the vehicle from toppling. This active stabilization will require additional
software and operational complexity. An active control strategy at touchdown is likely implemented
on the Falcon 9 booster landings [12]. However, in this case, slosh is unlikely a major factor since
the propellant tanks are nearly empty at the time of touchdown.
</p>
<p>The present study extends the dynamics formulation developed in Ref. [9] and investigates how
slosh dynamics affects the performance of an actively controlled vehicle during touchdown. The
two-two-and-over symmetric landing mode is shown in Fig. 1. In this mode, one pair of legs
initially collides with the surface, followed by the collision of a second pair of legs with the surface
and rebound which may or may not lead to toppling of the lander.
</p>

</p>
<p>The paper is organized as follows. In Section II, the treatment of the collision dynamics and
equations governing the motion in between collisions from Ref. [9] is summarized. Section III
discusses the parameters of the rate damping controller used in this study for active attitude stabi-
lization. Simulation results obtained for the Altair lunar lander configuration are presented for the
active two-two-and-over landing mode with and without slosh dynamics are shown in Section IV.
Section V. provides concluding remarks.
</p>

used to mimic control system delays and thruster transients. The appropriate thruster is commanded
to full thrust whenever the rate deadband is reached. Mass depletion is assumed to be negligible.

<p>CONCLUSION
</p>
<p>In this work the impact of propellant slosh on the performance of an active stabilization control
system during touchdown was presented with the focus on the planar two-two-and-over symmetric
landing mode. Collision dynamics were modeled via Kane’s theory of generalized impulse and mo-
menta. Reducing the controller deadband and delay time or increasing the control power improved
the robustness of the closed-loop system to slosh conditions prior to touchdown.
</p>
