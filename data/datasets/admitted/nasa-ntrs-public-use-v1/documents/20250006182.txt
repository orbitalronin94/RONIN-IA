The Tradeoffs
➢ Increased complexity:



• Safety factors rely on trusting “offline” testing and analysis to ensure appropriate 
conservatism and coverage of uncertainties → set it and forget it



• Probabilistic results have an aura of precision → more responsibility on analyst to 
be rigorous and potentially modify approach on a case-by-case basis



➢Need more data to calibrate/validate the stochastic model



➢ Neither can predict unknown unknowns, but “overconservatism” of safety 
factors might save us where a precise probabilistic result may not.



• Example 1: Underconservatism in COPV liner fatigue crack growth rates post-autofrettage



• Example 2: Shuttle probabilistic analysis (anecdote)

Epistemic vs. Aleatory Uncertainty
• Types of uncertainty:



• Aleatory: “Also known as statistical, stochastic, or 
irreducible uncertainty… that in principle cannot be 
reduced by additional physical or experimental 
knowledge.” [1]



• Epistemic: Reducible uncertainty that is “due to 
simplifying model assumptions, missing physics, or 
basic lack of knowledge.” [1]



• Useful to identify uncertainty that can potentially be 



reduced with additional resources.
➢ Typically implemented as a double loop 



Monte Carlo simulation



➢ Results in interval results referred to as a “p-box”

2. Roy, Christopher J., and William L. Oberkampf. "A comprehensive framework for verification,

Example | FAA, Titanium turbine rotor disks
• Multi-year testing and characterization effort by the 



Aerospace Industries Association (AIA) Rotor Integrity Sub-
Committee (~10 member companies)



• Used POD curve to estimate candidate flaw distributions



• Selected/adjusted flaw distribution to match field experience



• Performed probabilistic analyses to guide relative risk 
reduction (e.g., through redesign) compared to an identified 
baseline “design target risk”



1. FAA Advisory Circular 33.14 – Damage Tolerance for High Energy Turbine Rotors



2. Aerospace Industries Association Rotor Integrity Sub-Committee. "The development of anomaly distributions for aircraft engine titanium disk 
alloys." Proceedings of 38th AIAA/ASME/ASCE/AHS/ASC Structures, Structural Dynamics, and Materials Conference. 1997.



We developed the distribution through a series of 



analytical modeling steps, based on behavioral 



observations, that simulate the manufacturing process of 



a titanium rotating component from ingot conversion 



through forging inspection. The simulation incorporates 



three-dimensional size information from detected and



analyzed hard alpha, inspection capability, and inspection 



find rates to determine a baseline distribution shape. The 



initial estimated distribution was adjusted based on 



certain Commercial Engine service experience of the 



industry committee members. This adjustment was 



necessary since the analytical process involved a set of 



difficult to evaluate assumptions, leading to multiple 



potential solutions.2

Example | USAF, Equivalent Initial Flaw Size (EIFS)
• Back-calculate “equivalent” initial flaws from 



observed fatigue cracks



• Resulting flaw distribution is not necessarily 
physical, as it is dependent on the specific back-
calculation method
➢ Crack growth predictions based on the derived 



distribution must use the corresponding forward 
calculation method



• Relies on statistically significant number of 
observations of damage that has progressed to a 
quantifiable size in representative hardware



See: USAF Damage Tolerant Design Handbook: Guidelines for the Analysis

Conclusion



• Probabilistic analysis can enhance models to provide 
significantly more information and potentially reduce 
overconservatism.



• Primary tradeoffs are the increased complexity of 
analysis and data requirements.



• Alternatives to probabilistic approaches for reusable 
spaceflight hardware, such as inspection intervals, should 
also be considered.
