The previous 20 to 25 years have seen a tremendous increase in space

exploration, and with that an increase in the level of logistics planning needed

to ensure mission success. For spacecraft that are designed to be periodi-

cally re-supplied, a key logistics consumable is propellant, as it constitutes the

greatest up-mass on re-supply vehicles. A trajectory design strategy is there-

fore desired that minimizes propellant usage in order to ease the demand for

propellant re-supply missions. This thesis develops such a strategy in three

stages, and uses the International Space Station (ISS) as its testbed, as no

other LEO spacecraft is more challenging from a space logistics standpoint.

First, the ISS trajectory planning problem is formulated as a con-

strained burn optimization problem assuming a deterministic atmosphere. The

cost function is total ∆v, with constraints imposed on longitude of ascending

viii



node (LAN) and semi-major axis (SMA) altitude. Analytic derivatives are

constructed for both the cost and constraints, which are necessary given the

6-week to 2-year time frames being considered. A gradient-based optimizer

is then utilized to find locally-optimal solutions to real-world ISS trajectory

planning problems.

Second, atmospheric uncertainty is addressed by constructing a proba-

bilistic model of space weather data using Gaussian Processes (GPs). Bayesian

inference is performed using the GP model to generate mean and covariance

estimates for space weather predictions, whose pedigree is assessed against

test data. The predictions are then mapped into atmospheric density via the

analytic Jacchia-Roberts density model, and the effect of space weather un-

certainty on orbital lifetime is examined.

Third, an ISS burn execution uncertainty model is developed. This

model, along with the space weather uncertainty model, are deployed in a lin-

ear covariance analysis to ascertain their combined effect on LAN and SMA al-

titude dispersions. The deterministic constraints from the original problem are

re-formulated as stochastic constraints, where now the constraint uncertainty

interval is required to fall within specified bounds. An updated optimization

framework is constructed using the original ∆v cost function along with the

stochastic constraints to solve the trajectory optimization problem under at-

mospheric uncertainty. Finally, the complete architecture is summarized for

deployment in an operational setting.

Introduction

The previous 20 to 25 years have seen a tremendous increase in the

interest of space exploration, commercialization, and militarization. This can

be attributed to several factors, including the following.

• Advancements in imaging technology that have enabled historical firsts

such as close up views of Pluto and video footage of the Perseverence

rover landing on Mars

• Efficiencies in space component manufacturing and spacecraft reusability

that continue to bring down launch and other mission costs

• Demand for global internet coverage and a look ahead to interplanetary

internet capabilities1

• Military demand for increased space-based surveillance capabilities and

the creation of the U.S. Space Force to coordinate U.S. military opera-

tions in space

1

https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-interplanetary-internet


• Scientific demand for more robust climate change models that utilize

data gathered from space-borne assets

• A continuous human presence in space on board the International Space

Station (ISS) since November, 2000

• The allure and real possibility of space tourism for routine civilians, as

demonstrated during the September, 2021 flight of Inspiration 4

• An emphasis on STEM education in schools coupled with the social

media presence of personalities such as Elon Musk

In response to and commensurate with this heightened interest has been

a steady increase in number of launches and orbiting spacecraft worldwide, as

well as the scope, complexity, and longevity of their missions. On Earth, these

aspects of any operation (civilian or military) are directly proportional to the

level of logistics planning needed to ensure its success, and in this regard space

operations are no different.

In its 2004 recommendation to the U.S. Government for addressing crit-

ical U.S. space logistics needs, the AIAA Space Logistics Technical Committee

defines space logistics as follows [19].

Space logistics is the science of planning and carrying out the move-

ment of humans and materiel2 to, from, and within space combined

2This is not a typo. Materiel is defined as military materials and equipment.

2



with the ability to maintain human and robotics operations within

space. In its most comprehensive sense, space logistics addresses

the aspects of space operations both on the Earth and in space

that deal with: (a) design and development, acquisition, storage,

movement, distribution, maintenance, evacuation, and disposition

of space materiel; (b) movement, evacuation, and hospitalization

of people in space; (c) acquisition or construction, maintenance,

operation, and disposition of facilities on the Earth and in space

to support human and robotic space operations; and (d) acquisi-

tion or furnishing of services to support human and robotics space

operations.

Focusing on the maintenance aspect of space logistics and applying it to space-

craft that are designed to be periodically serviced, as the scope, complexity,

and longevity of these missions increase, the more important it becomes to de-

termine the right composition and frequency of servicing missions. For robotic

spacecraft, this is relevant to propellant re-supply and provision of spare com-

ponents. For human spacecraft, this relevance is expanded to include food,

clothing, personal effects, tools and equipment, and any other human consum-

ables that need to be re-supplied at regular intervals. The degree to which

humans can routinely live and work in space for extended periods therefore

depends in part on how we approach spacecraft maintenance and servicing.

For spacecraft that are periodically serviced, propellant is the most

expensive consumable, as it constitutes the greatest up-mass on re-supply

vehicles. The rate and frequency at which a spacecraft consumes propellant

3



directly impacts the composition and frequency of its re-supply missions. Even

for spacecraft that use propellant for attitude control, most of a spacecraft’s

propellant is used for translation burns that adjust its orbit to meet one or more

trajectory constraints. A trajectory design strategy that minimizes propellant

usage subject to constraints is therefore desirable in order to ease the demand

for propellant re-supply missions.

To narrow the problem space, this thesis focuses on trajectory design

and optimization for spacecraft in Low-Earth Orbit (LEO). While the funda-

mental architecture developed in this work can be applied to any LEO space-

craft, this thesis utilizes the ISS as its primary testbed, as no other spacecraft

in this regime comes close in terms of scope, complexity, and longevity, and

therefore challenge from a space logistics standpoint. Indeed, the increase in

space interest beginning in the early 2000s alongside the buildout of the ISS

likely motivated works by Snead [87], which propose a space logistics infras-

tructure for public and private space enterprises, and Baker and Eichstadt [6],

which analyze cargo transport requirements for the ISS and advocate for com-

mercial cargo transport services.

The remainder of this chapter discusses the current trajectory design

process for the ISS at a high level along with its limitations. These include

limitations in the algorithms and techniques for selecting burn times and mag-

nitudes, and for predicting space weather and its effect on LEO trajectory

design. In both cases, improvements are proposed which lay the groundwork

for the thesis contributions put forth at the end of the chapter.

1.1 Trajectory Design for the ISS

This section, all of Chapter 3, and part of Chapter 6 were published

previously in [15] and are re-published here with minor additions.

Since its launch in 1998, the ISS has remained in a roughly circular,

51.6◦ orbit, between 330 km and 420 km in altitude, akin to an unending,

precessing, circle. Over a 6-month period, the ISS traces out approximately

2850 nearly identical revolutions that exhibit a near-uniform distribution in

longitude of their ascending node crossings.

The ISS orbit is maintained by executing translational burns once or

twice per month in order to meet known trajectory constraints. A constraint

occurs approximately once per month, primarily in the form of a longitude

5



1.1 Trajectory Design for the ISS

of ascending node (LAN) constraint imposed by Russian vehicle launches and

landings, or occasionally a semi-major axis (SMA) altitude constraint. There-

fore, in a 6-month period, the ISS is typically faced with 6 trajectory con-

straints, and 6 to 9 burns to meet those constraints. In the typical case where

they are all LAN constraints, 6 out of the above 2850 orbits will be constrained

to have their LAN crossings occur between prescribed lower and upper LAN

limits, as shown in Figure 1.2. These 6 orbits must be identified and their

LANs evaluated to determine if the constraints are met, i.e., if the LANs fall

within their set limits. If they do not, the burns must be adjusted to shift the

LANs within their limits, thereby meeting the constraints.

Figure 1.3 similarly shows ISS SMA altitude over a 3-month period.

An SMA altitude constraint would require the ISS to be at a particular SMA

altitude at a particular time. As before, if the constraint is not met, the burns

must be adjusted to ensure the constraint is satisfied.

This is the trajectory design problem for the ISS. Past and current

missions besides the ISS have solved their respective trajectory design problems

using a variety of tools. During the Space Shuttle program, an application

called the Orbital Maneuver Processor (OMP) [12, 39, 40] was used to target

the Shuttle’s rendezvous burns to meet trajectory constraints for missions to

the ISS. For Orion, the two-level burn targeting algorithm [59, 83] is being

used onboard to converge on feasible solutions for the Artemis 1 mission [21]

and beyond. Copernicus [71, 98] is similarly being used on the ground to

extensively analyze and optimize the Artemis 1 trajectory. Finally, MONTE

1.1 Trajectory Design for the ISS

has been used to plan trajectories for several interplanetary missions, including

Mars Science Laboratory and Cassini [27, 86].

For the ISS, the current approach to trajectory design is to target the

aforementioned burns to meet the constraints using simple rules of thumb

that approximate the change in LAN or SMA altitude per unit of burn ∆v,

or change in orbital speed. These rules are employed in a manually-driven

set of tools to arrive at burn solutions. While this approach is able to obtain

burn solutions, it does so in a non-iterative fashion that lacks automation.

Furthermore, this technique provides only coarse information of the problem

sensitivities due to its inherent first-order nature, which limits its efficiency

in finding solutions. Finally, the current set of tools are unable to directly

optimize burns, nor can they be easily integrated with third-party optimization

packages to minimize total ∆v.

A different approach to consider for the ISS is to use algorithms for

designing repeat ground track orbits (RGTOs), which are analogs to the ISS

orbit in that they both require ascending node crossings to occur over specific

Earth-fixed locations. RGTOs are used for robotic missions that require the

ground track to repeat based on a set of conditions, namely that the orbit

repeat after a certain number of revolutions in a certain number of days [92].

RGTOs are established by iterating on SMA to achieve a change in LAN

(∆λ) per revolution that satisfies the desired repeat conditions. Trajectory

design for RGTOs consists of computing a reference orbit that meets the repeat

conditions, and then planning periodic correction burns to maintain that orbit

8



1.1 Trajectory Design for the ISS

in the presence of non-Keplerian perturbations [92, 43].

While conceptually similar, RGTOs differ from the ISS orbit in a num-

ber of ways. First, as noted earlier, RGTOs are used for robotic missions

that do not have to rendezvous with other vehicles. The ISS, in contrast

is a crewed spacecraft that serves as a rendezvous target for multiple visit-

ing vehicles. Second, RGTOs target burns to meet ∆λ constraints driven by

the repeat conditions, whereas the ISS targets burns to meet absolute LAN

(λ) constraints imposed by the Russian Soyuz and Progress visiting vehicles.

Third, RGTOs typically have tight tolerances on the ground track trace, and

therefore on ∆λ, in order to meet the (usually science or other observation-

driven) mission objectives. The absolute LAN constraints imposed on the ISS,

however, can at times be met across a wide LAN range, as discussed in Chap-

ter 3. The large range available to meet such a constraint is in fact what makes

trajectory optimization possible for the ISS. In light of these differences, the

algorithms for designing RGTOs are not readily applicable to the ISS orbit.

The above difficulties therefore motivate a new approach to ISS tra-

jectory design, one that casts the ISS problem as a constrained trajectory

optimization problem that can be solved using a conventional gradient-based

optimizer. This approach requires accurate problem derivatives, which can

be approached in a number of ways. Conventional finite differencing is un-

tenable given the accuracy and performance losses incurred when computing

constraint derivatives over the 6-month to 2-year time frames typically consid-

ered in ISS trajectory planning. Using complex step numerical derivatives [60]

prevent the accuracy loss but still incur the performance loss due to requir-

Chapter 3 provides a framework that enables searching for both feasi-

iteratively converging to a solution using a gradient-based search with exact

problem derivatives. These derivatives are analytic for the cost function and

consist of variational equations for the constraints. The latter are developed

by examining a notional set of ISS trajectory events and combining both the

state and constraint partial derivatives for each event using the chain rule. An

initial estimate for each burn is developed using the rules of thumb already

being employed in the current approach. The derivatives and initial estimate

generator can then be utilized in any trajectory planning and optimization

toolset that accepts user-supplied derivatives. To this end, the derived frame-

work is implemented in a gradient-based optimizer to find local minimum ∆v

solutions, assuming fixed dates for the reboost burns. Once a local solution is

found, the burn dates are randomly varied and a new local solution is found.

This step is repeated until a candidate global minimum emerges from the local

explored for both notional and real-world ISS trajectory planning problems.

In eliminating the deficiencies noted with the current process, this

framework reduces the human time needed to generate a solution, and po-

while this approach is developed for the ISS, it can be extended to other LEO

missions with similar constraints, such as RGTOs, surface mapping missions,

and planetary science missions.

The overall architecture proposed in this work is built up in stages,

starting with the framework introduced in this section and presented fully in

Chapter 3. For simplicity, this first stage assumes a deterministic atmosphere

as part of its state dynamics, i.e., with no uncertainty in atmospheric density.

The next stage, introduced in Section 1.2 and presented in Chapter 4 develops a

probabilistic model for predicting space weather, which is a key contributor to

uncertainty in atmospheric density. The final stage, introduced in Section 1.3

and presented in Chapter 5, brings together the first and second stages to

deliver a trajectory design and optimization methodology in the presence of

atmospheric uncertainty for LEO missions with direct application to the ISS.

1.3 Trajectory Design Under Atmospheric Uncertainty

The trajectory design problem for the ISS as discussed in Section 1.1 is

to determine the translational burns needed to satisfy LAN and SMA altitude

14



1.3 Trajectory Design Under Atmospheric Uncertainty

trajectory constraints over a given time frame. The ISS operates at a low

enough altitude that the effects of atmospheric drag on the orbit must be

accounted for in the state dynamics. Given the uncertainty in predicting

atmospheric density discussed in Section 1.2, any trajectory design effort in

LEO should consider atmospheric uncertainty in the design process. Assessing

∆v costs and constraint satisfaction based on a nominal atmospheric profile

alone provides single point information, but lacks insight into the distribution

of possible trajectory outcomes, and therefore cannot provide the measure of

conservatism needed to protect for the extreme trajectory cases.

The space weather prediction framework introduced in Section 1.2 and

developed in Chapter 4 addressed this need by providing a probabilistic model

for characterizing space weather uncertainty. This uncertainty can then be

related to atmospheric uncertainty through utilization of an analytic density

model that is a function of space weather parameters.

In order to be useful, the space weather uncertainty predictions pro-

duced by this model must be incorporated into the trajectory design process

via uncertainty propagation. While several methods exist for propagating un-

certainty, two of the more common methods in orbital mechanics are Monte

Carlo (MC) simulation and Linear Covariance (Lincov) analysis [58].

MC simulation assumes a set of initial conditions x0 at t0 that are

described by a PDF p(t0, x0). N samples are drawn from this distribution,

and each sample is propagated from t0 to tf through the state dynamics, with

key parameter statistics collected at points of interest that characterize the

15



1.3 Trajectory Design Under Atmospheric Uncertainty

uncertainty. MC simulation is straightforward to implement and captures the

full non-linearities of state errors. Depending on the physics, however, N may

need to be large in order to accurately reflect the uncertainty, which can drive

computational resources, especially for long-duration cases.

Lincov analysis, by contrast, operates on a defined nominal trajectory.

State errors are assumed small enough to be adequately characterized by a

first-order Taylor series expansion about the nominal trajectory, and the re-

sulting ODEs are solved to provide the state uncertainty as a function of time.

Loosely speaking, Lincov analysis has the opposite advantages/disadvantages

of MC simulation. Lincov has several mathematical subtleties and requires

bookkeeping multiple state deviations that can make its implementation more

challenging. Furthermore, by definition it models only linear state deviations,

which may be inadequate for highly non-linear problems. Once configured,

however, Lincov is significantly faster than MC simulation and can deliver

comparable statistical results.

Given the 6-month to 2-year time frames considered for the ISS, and

since state errors in LEO and constraint errors for the ISS are well character-

ized by a linear model, Lincov analysis is selected for uncertainty propagation

in this work. As described in Chapter 5, Lincov equations are applied to

the trajectory optimization framework developed in Chapter 3, which enables

the uncertainty to be computed in line with the nominal state in a single

trajectory propagation arc. This approach provides the necessary influence

of uncertainty on the trajectory design process while minimizing additional

computational overhead.

1.4 Related Work

Two principal themes in this work are 1) uncertainty quantification

and propagation in the context of LEO, and 2) trajectory optimization under

uncertainty, both of which have been explored in the literature using a variety

of techniques in multiple regimes. An excellent summary of the available

techniques for the former is given by Luo and Yang [58].

Specific applications of the former include Wilkins and Alfriend [97],

who employ a Gauss-Markov colored-noise process to capture atmospheric

density model errors, which are assumed to be correlated in both space and

time, and then perform covariance propagation in LEO using an EKF with

this process noise model. The novelty in their approach is to model multi-

ple atmospheric perturbations, each as a separate Gauss-Markov process with

unique time constant and standard deviation parameters.

Naasz et. al. [66] examine the effect of solar flux variations on or-

bital decay predictions. Their approach assumes different smoothed solar flux

profiles and superimposes short-term solar flux variations to each profile via

Monte Carlo sampling. The resulting effects orbital lifetimes are analyzed

statistically, and they conclude that short-term solar flux variations must be

included in orbit decay predictions.

Schiemenz [84] seeks to quantify the uncertainty in the dominant forces

17



1.4 Related Work

acting on a spacecraft in LEO, specifically atmospheric drag and non-spherical

gravity. For atmospheric drag, he implements both a simple white noise pro-

cess as well as a Brownian motion process as proposed by Emmert [26] to

capture solar flux errors, and a simple Gaussian distribution to capture geo-

magnetic index errors. These process noise models are integrated into standard

orbit determination algorithms to generate and propagate a more realistic nav-

igation error covariance.

Lopez-Jimenez et. al. [56] aim to improve covariance realism by using a

consider parameter to account for uncertainties in atmospheric drag. The vari-

ance of this parameter is estimated by minimizing the difference between the

observed and consider covariance matrices through least-squares optimization.

Delle’Elce and Kerschen [23] and Delle’Elce et. al. [22] consider both

Monte Carlo (MC) as well as Polynominal Chaos Expansion (PCE) [51] tech-

niques to characterize and propagate orbit uncertainty due to atmospheric drag

in the context of the orbital lifetime problem. To overcome the curse of di-

mensionality in PCE, they consolidate the stochastic variables associated with

the drag force into a single variable termed the drag correction factor. In [23],

however, they assume a constant equivalent solar activity profile throughout

the propagation, and in [23] they assume a random effective solar activity.

Neither of these assumptions is made in this work.

The latter theme (trajectory optimization under uncertainty) has been

also explored using multiple techniques in several different flight phases as

well as non-flight regimes. For example, Janson et. al. [49] develop an ap-

18



1.5 Thesis Contributions

proach termed Monte Carlo Motion Planning (MCMP) to deduce an optimal

path for robotic path planning based on Collision Probability (CP) estima-

tion. Breitner and Pesch [14] treat the Space Shuttle re-entry problem as a

two-person differential game and develop an optimal feedback controller un-

der defined worst-case atmospheric disturbances. Jewison and Miller [50] study

optimal trajectory re-planning given path constraint uncertainty during prox-

imity operations between space vehicles. Low-thrust trajectory optimization

is also explored by Oguri and McMahon [72], who solve the trajectory opti-

mization problem by developing a stochastic primer vector that honors chance

constraints, which are probabilistic path constraints to be met to within a

specified confidence level. Finally, Saunders [82] augments the traditional op-

timal control problem cost function to include covariance states from linear

covariance analysis, with the goal of minimizing the covariance at the trajec-

tory end point, thus reducing sensitivity to trajectory uncertainty. Saunders’s

work is particularly resonant, as this work also aims to embed results from

linear covariance analysis in the trajectory optimization process.

The related work cited in this section pushes the envelope of under-

standing in the two principal themes noted earlier. This work adds to this

understanding in several unique ways, as described in the following section.

3.1 Background

The dominant perturbing forces on the ISS orbit are Earth’s non-

spherical gravity and atmospheric drag.1 Drag causes altitude loss over time,

while non-spherical gravity causes nodal regression to the west, primarily due

to the secular effect of J2. Combining nodal regression to the west with Earth’s

rotation to the east, the ISS ground track shifts approximately 23.3◦ westward

per orbit, as shown in Figure 3.1. Furthermore, since the ISS orbital period

is roughly 92 minutes, there are either 15 or 16 Daily Orbits (DOs) each day,

with DO1 defined as the first orbit with a LAN west of 20◦E, also shown in

Figure 3.1.

The ISS orbit is planned and maintained in part by the ISS Trajec-

tory Operations (TOPO) group at the NASA Johnson Space Center. TOPO

personnel routinely generate long-term trajectory plans for the ISS, ranging

from 6 months to almost 2 years in length. Each plan contains the trajectory-

this dynamic, one of the goals in generating a trajectory plan is to target the

upstream burns by adjusting their ∆v magnitudes, thereby shifting the ground

tracks as needed to meet the downstream LAN constraints, or adjusting the

orbital altitude as needed to meet any downstream SMA altitude constraints.

total ∆v subject to the trajectory constraints, as noted at the beginning of

Section 3.2. The key to obtaining an optimal solution lies in determining the

gradients for the burn optimization problem.

3.6 Initial Estimate for the Controls

An initial estimate of each ∆vj can be computed by examining the first

downstream constraint to be met by burn j. This estimate is appropriate

when the ISS trajectory sequence consists of alternating burns and constraints

(i.e., burn → constraint → burn → constraint). It may be less appropri-

ate though for other sequences such as 1) burn → burn → constraint, or 2)

burn → constraint → constraint. In case 1), this approach would result in a

zero ∆v estimate for the second burn (and any subsequent burns prior to the

constraint), since the ∆v for the first burn would be estimated to meet the

downstream constraint, thus obviating the need for subsequent burns prior to

the constraint. In case 2), this approach may result in the second constraint

not being met at all, since the burn ∆v would be estimated to only meet the

56

first constraint. Notwithstanding these shortcomings, this approach provides

a reasonable initial estimate to the solver with minimal computational effort.

LAN (λ) or mean SMA altitude (h̄ = ā−Re). Beginning with LAN constraints,

and the ∆t from the burn epoch to the constraint epoch. This value of λ must

14◦ to 15◦. Assuming first that ∆vj = 0 at the burn epoch, the ISS is simply

that point. If the constraint is not met, say λ = 16◦, then a ∆λ of −1◦ is

the burn epoch in order to achieve this ∆λ.

3.7 Real-World ISS Problems and Results

The above framework is now used to analyze two problems, 1) a no-

tional ISS trajectory event sequence to reflect the sequence in Figure 3.5, and

2) an actual ISS trajectory event sequence from March through early Septem-

ber, 2017. In both cases, the ISS trajectory is numerically integrated from

event to event, saving off the state, state transition matrix (STM), and other

necessary results at each event. After reaching tf, a script performs chain

rule operations on the saved results in order to form the constraint gradients.

Given the time frames spanned by actual ISS look-ahead trajectory plans—6

weeks to roughly 2 years—obtaining the constraint gradients in this manner

(via single propagation from t0 to tf) is generally more efficient than using

finite differences.

The above numerical integration and constraint gradient computation

process is treated as a black box, which is called by a gradient-based optimizer

to solve the constrained burn optimization problem. At each iteration, the

optimizer sends the current ∆v values to the numerical integration routine,

which in turn updates the event sequence with these values, re-integrates the

59



3.7 Real-World ISS Problems and Results

ISS trajectory from t0 to tf, re-computes the constraint gradients, and returns

the results to the optimizer. The optimizer then uses these gradients along

with the objective gradient in Eq. 3.2 to update its estimate of the ∆v values.

This process repeats until the optimizer converges on a locally-optimal (i.e.

minimum total ∆v) solution, assuming a feasible solution exists.

3.8 Extension to Global Minimization

The optimization to this point assumes a fixed date for each burn.

The next logical step is to vary the burn dates in an effort to find a global

minimum for total ∆v. This is accomplished by first converting each burn

date to a Mission Elapsed Time (MET), defined as an integer number of days

from a reference epoch. Next, a set of allowable MET ranges for the burns

is constructed using the constraint METs as the range endpoints. The first

burn range extends from 0 days MET to 1 day before the first constraint.

The second burn range extends from 1 day after the first constraint to 1 day

before the second constraint, and so on. A ±1-day pad is inserted around each

constraint since in actual operations, a reboost burn would not occur on the

same day as a launch or landing.

epoch of March 2, 2017 00:00:00.0 UTC. Like problem 1) from Section 3.7,

the events in Table 3.3 are notional, reflecting real event names but not event

dates.

4.1.2 Solar Cycle Prediction Techniques

Due diligence in finding an alternative approach requires first exam-

ining solar cycle prediction techniques broadly, and then focusing on those

approaches that meet the above requirements. Predicting the solar cycle has

a rich history with a spectrum of techniques that vary in both approach and

scope. Pesnell [75], Hathaway [42], and more recently Petrovay [76] provide

excellent surveys of the available techniques. All three authors group the avail-

able prediction techniques into three broad categories: 1) precursor methods,

which attempt to predict the current cycle based on geomagnetic and related

indicators in the previous cycle, 2) model-based predictions, which rely on

physical models of solar dynamics to predict the current cycle, and 3) numeri-

cal methods, which include statistical techniques, spectral analysis, regression

methods, and non-linear methods such as neural networks for predicting cycle

performance. Notwithstanding the breadth and depth of research and vari-

ety of techniques in each category, the general focus across all of them is in

predicting the amplitude and duration of the current cycle to within some

statistical confidence, and usually in the context of sun spot number, rather

than a procedure for generating a time history of F10.7 and Ap/Kp over a

82



4.1 Background

fixed prediction window. Du [24] appears at first to get closer to this need

by attempting to model the actual shape of the remaining portion of the so-

lar cycle using a modified Gaussian function. As before though, his approach

is ultimately used for predicting the peak of the cycle in terms of sun spot

number.

An alternative to predicting the solar cycle is to predict atmospheric

density directly. Gondelach and Linares [36, 37], for example, investigate

thermospheric density estimation and prediction using a reduced order den-

sity state in an unscented Kalman filter. This approach allows them to ob-

tain highly accurate estimates of density over a span of 5 days. While cer-

tainly promising, it is less applicable to this work, which seeks to predict

space weather data (and by extension density) over the course of several years

rather than days, utilizing monthly mean rather than daily or hourly data.

Since a procedure is sought in this work to predict space weather indices

as functions of time, a time series analysis technique would seem to be most

appropriate. Petrovay [76], however, notes time series methods in general have

not been particularly successful in predicting the amplitude of an upcoming

cycle. In addition, he points out that since time series analysis is purely

mathematical, it offers little physical insight into the physical processes behind

cycle-to-cycle variations. Given this critique, our work is clearly cut out for

us to find a suitable and reliable time series technique for predicting space

weather indices.

4.1.3 Gaussian Process Regression

Predicting space weather is naturally a regression problem, for which

we desire to use historical data to create a probabilistic model that allows us

to make predictions on future performance. In order to maintain flexibility, it

makes sense to leverage non-parametric models rather than those that impose

a particular parametric form (e.g., polynomial regression). Gaussian Process

Regression (GPR) is such a technique that has been successfully used in diverse

prediction problems such as robotic path planning [10], soil map creation [38],

atmospheric temperature prediction [48], and extended target tracking [96],

and is therefore a strong candidate for this task. For consistency in terminology

and notation, the following overview draws primarily from Rasmussen and

Williams [78].

A Gaussian Process (GP) is “a collection of random variables, any finite

number of which have a joint Gaussian distribution.” If one considers a func-

At this point, the only open question is the choice of covariance func-

tion. Returning to Figure 1.4 and the data modeling challenges noted earlier,

it is not obvious which covariance function to choose for space weather pre-

diction. Looking at F10.7, clearly some kind of periodic function is needed,

but a periodic function alone will not suffice given the above variabilities in

the cycle. One approach is to use a composite function, but it is not readily

apparent how to construct such a function in a structured, repeatable manner.

Duvenaud [25] provides a hierarchical, automated approach to con-

structing composite kernels, and even applies this automated process to derive

a composite kernel function that captures over 400 years of sun spot data,

from 1610-2011. This is an impressive result, as that time frame includes

the well-known Maunder minimum that occurred from roughly 1645 to 1715,

during which sun spots were quite rare, in contrast to the surrounding time

periods that showed periodic sun spot activity in concert with the 11-year

solar cycle [52]. While the composite kernel posterior performs well on the ob-

served data during the above time period, it does not extrapolate well beyond

that time period. Furthermore, implementation of the automated process is

challenging given the multiple levels of kernel construction and checking re-

quired to determine the “best” kernel function. A comparably structured but

flat (i.e., single level) approach to kernel construction is sought that provides

equivalently, to identify structure in the data that enables reliable prediction

should be used for final model selection, as the global minima in the validation

form of these metrics almost never coincide. While included for completeness,

NLML is used principally for training, not validation. SMSE is certainly a

commonly-used metric for validation, however Rasmussen and Williams [78]

caution that squared error loss is a function only of the predicted mean and ig-

nores the validation set variances, whereas the full predictive distribution does

depend on the scale of the covariance function. For this and other reasons,

they avoid using squared error loss as the metric for hyperparameter selec-

tion. Given this guidance, the final model for prediction is selected by finding

the global minimum in the validation form of MSLL and then selecting the

hyperparameters associated with that minimum point.

4.5 Next Questions

As the name suggests, this section zooms out to examine the assump-

tions and choices in the selected methodology for atmospheric modeling, and to

ask appropriate next questions for further research beyond this thesis. These

questions/suggestions are noted below in italics. In this work, an atmospheric

model is needed to provide a value of atmospheric density at each time t. The

density ρ is then used to compute drag acceleration aD, which in turn updates

the state dynamics for trajectory prediction. As noted in Chapter 2, ρ in this

work is computed using the Jacchia-Roberts density model. Jacchia-Roberts

is chosen for its analytic tractability, allowing for the partial derivatives in

Sections 2.3 and 2.4, as well as Eq. 5.11. These derivatives enable formulation

of a linear covariance driver in Chapter 5 to determine constraint uncertainty

with a single trajectory propagation arc. This is a key efficiency for trajec-

tory optimization under atmospheric uncertainty, given the 6-month to 2-year

trajectory timeframes being considered.

Jacchia-Roberts (JR) is an analytic model that provides ρ as a function

of the spacecraft state, space weather, and time. Under this model, uncertainty

in density is not computed directly, but is derived from uncertainties in the

constituent inputs, which are then mapped to uncertainty in density through

the aforementioned partial derivatives. Of these inputs, uncertainty in F10.7 is

studied in this work, and uncertainties in the remaining inputs are left to future

work. This chapter develops a model for predicting F10.7 and its uncertainty

using GPR. The outputs of this model are a predictive mean and covariance

124



4.5 Next Questions

for F10.7 at set intervals (daily or monthly epochs), with a piecewise constant

assumption in between updates.

The above description summarizes the methodology selected in this

work for atmospheric modeling. Clearly, the assumptions and choices embed-

ded in this approach give rise to several questions worth considering. The

first is the choice of density model itself. Is the Jacchia-Roberts density accu-

rate enough for LEO trajectory planning purposes? The answer, of course, is

mission and spacecraft-specific, and mission designers must provide trajectory

accuracy requirements in order to address this question.

Unpacking the question further, one can compare two (or more) models

with identical inputs, as well as two models with different inputs. In the for-

mer case, two models may take in identical inputs but output different density

values depending on model fidelity. In the latter case, two models of compa-

rable fidelity may output different density values as a result of different input

sets. For example, Jacchia-Roberts assumes F10.7 and Kp for space weather

index inputs. There are other indices though, including sunspot number, Ap,

and potential combinations of indices. Which indices are most appropriate

for modeling atmospheric uncertainty? Again, this depends in part on the

trajectory accuracy requirements imposed by mission designers.

A related question is: What is the sensitivity of density to changes in

the different indices and how do those sensitivities vary from model to model?

The sensitivities can be explored via computation and comparison of partial

derivatives, parametric sweeps, or other relevant methods for sensitivity anal-

125



4.5 Next Questions

ysis. These sensitivities help inform which indices require the sharpest pencil

in terms of uncertainty. If a chosen density model is order(s) of magnitude

more sensitive to a particular input, then it becomes much more critical to

accurately bound the uncertainty in that quantity, and vice versa for a less

sensitive input.

A second family of questions involves how GPR is applied to estimate

space weather. As GPR provides a distribution over functions, it is not limited

to predicting at discrete times; it is the user that decides how frequently to

predict, and what approach to use for points in between predictions. The ex-

amples in this chapter generate monthly predictions, while the work in Chap-

ter 5 uses daily predictions. In both cases, the predictions are assumed to

be piecewise constant. The piecewise constant value diverges most from the

GPR value at that point when the slope magnitude of the mean prediction is

highest, i.e., on the way towards the top of the cycle, and on the way towards

the bottom. In the former case (large positive slope), a piecewise constant

assumption under predicts the index, while in the latter case (large negative

slope), it over predicts the index. As the time between updates increases (daily

vs. monthly), the discrepancy between the piecewise constant value and the

GPR value at that point increase commensurately. Interpolation or splining

between updates will mitigate these errors, and it is worthwhile to investigate

the tradeoff between computational overhead (in the form of introducing inter-

polation/splining and/or predicting at a higher rate) and prediction error at

points in between updates.

126



4.5 Next Questions

Continuing this theme, one can also ask what is the desired behavior of

the GP as the prediction density (monthly, daily, hourly) changes?. The exam-

ples in this work all strive to have the GP produce a smooth, slowly varying

mean F10.7 signal over the roughly 11-year cycle that does not attempt to

capture the annual or more frequent variations in the data, and the covariance

kernel hyperparameters are chosen to meet this goal. If a prediction is needed

on an hour-by-hour basis over the course of a few days, however, that calls

for a different density of training data (hourly, instead of daily or monthly),

and a different tuning of the hyperparameters to capture much more of the

immediate trend. It is worth noting, however, that spikes in space weather

routinely occur (see Figure 1.4) due to solar storms and related space weather

phenomena. These events often have a dramatic effect on Earth’s atmosphere

and are generally not predictable via regression regardless of the prediction

time scale.

Finally, in the extreme case, it may be possible to forgo estimating un-

certainty in the density model inputs altogether and leverage GPR to estimate

density and its uncertainty directly. This is by no means trivial, however, as

density varies not only with time, as in the case with F10.7, but also (at least

with the Jacchia-Roberts model) with spacecraft state and space weather. Es-

timating density therefore requires performing GPR in multiple dimensions

that are disparate in nature, e.g., spatial (position), physical (space weather),

and temporal (time).

Notwithstanding the formidable challenge, if density and its uncertainty

127



4.5 Next Questions

can be estimated in this matter with sufficient accuracy, this would render

moot many of the previous questions regarding model pedigree, appropriate

indices, etc. This approach is also beneficial in cases such as this work, where

the end use is orbit prediction, and a direct estimate of density can be im-

mediately input to the state dynamics, rather than an estimate of F10.7 (or

other index), which must first be mapped to density and then input to the

state dynamics.

In summary, the methodology selected in this work satisfies the need

for an at-epoch determination of atmospheric uncertainty and its relation to

constraint uncertainty. This approach raises several question worth exploring

in future work. The answers to these questions have the potential to substan-

tially improve the manner and accuracy with which atmospheric density and

its uncertainty are predicted.

5.1 Background

Linear covariance analysis, commonly referred to as simply “Lincov,”

provides an analytic, highly-efficient means of studying trajectory dispersions

and navigation errors in response to environmental modeling deficiencies, sen-

sor limitations, and actuator errors. Geller [31] generalizes and expands upon

previous work by Battin [7] and Maybeck [81] to provide a contemporary and

comprehensive treatment of Lincov that implements the above components.

Lincov has been used to study a wide range of problems in astrodynamics,

including orbital rendezvous [53, 101, 34, 105, 32], powered descent and land-

ing [33, 18], and autonomous optical navigation [88, 104].

While Lincov enables treatment of both navigation errors and trajec-

tory dispersions, this work does not focus on navigation uncertainty and as-

sumes perfect navigation (i.e., state knowledge) throughout. As such, Lincov

usage in this work is restricted to analysis of trajectory dispersions, and fu-

ture work can incorporate navigation uncertainty in order to further refine the

results.

129



5.1 Background

Applying Lincov to the ISS trajectory optimization problem requires

the fundamental concepts reviewed in Chapter 2: the Jacchia-Roberts at-

mosphere model, state dynamics, the state transition matrix (STM) Φ, and

gradient-based optimization. Also needed are the GPR prediction results

from Chapter 4, a burn execution uncertainty model, and equations to propa-

gate/update the trajectory dispersion covariance due to space weather uncer-

tainty and burn execution error.

This chapter brings these components together to form a Lincov frame-

work for assessing uncertainty in meeting ISS trajectory constraints. The

Lincov-based results are first validated against results obtained through Monte

Carlo (MC) simulation. Lincov is then used to quantify constraint uncertainty

as a function of coasting time to the constraint. This result informs when addi-

tional stochastic trajectory correction burns are needed in the design to reduce

constraint uncertainty to within allowable limits.

Finally, the Lincov framework is embedded in the trajectory optimiza-

tion framework developed in Chapter 3, with a modification of the constraints

to account for atmospheric uncertainty. In the combined framework, both the

constraint times and the deterministic burn times are fixed, just as they are in

Chapter 3. As such, if a given constraint is already met to within the allowable

limits, the upstream burn(s) can be optimized to reduce total ∆v. Conversely,

if the constraint currently violates the allowable limits, one or more stochastic

burns are needed between the affected deterministic burn and constraint in

order to reduce the constraint uncertainty.

5.5.1 Monte Carlo Validation

Monte Carlo (MC) simulation is a widely-used tool for studying un-

certainty effects in non-linear systems as well as validating results obtained

through Lincov analysis. This work employs MC simulation for the latter

purpose as follows. First, initial state (position/velocity) errors and F10.7 er-

rors are sampled from their respective covariance matrices and then added to

their respective mean values (0 for the initial state, and the GPR predictive

mean for F10.7) to derive each sample’s initial state and F10.7 values. The

initial state is then propagated to the time of the constraint and the constraint

value recorded. The sample → propagation → constraint value procedure is

repeated 1500 times and statistics collected on the resulting constraint data.

Figures 5.3 and 5.4 show histograms of the MC-based constraint values

for LAN and SMA altitude, respectively. Each figure also plots a normal PDF

derived from the MC data and a separate normal PDF derived from the Lincov

data. In both cases, the Lincov results are slightly more optimistic than the

MC results, possibly due to uncertainty information in higher moments not be-

ing adequately captured by a linear covariance propagation. Notwithstanding,

Chapter 6

Conclusions

The increased interest in space over the past 20 to 25 years has brought

about a commensurate increase in not only the number of launches and orbiting

spacecraft, but also in the scope, complexity, and longevity of their missions,

and the level of space logistics planning needed to ensure mission success has

increased in response. For spacecraft that are designed to be periodically

serviced, a large part of space logistics is re-supply missions, with propellant

having the largest up-mass. It is therefore desirable to minimize propellant

usage onboard to reduce the number of propellant-driven re-supply missions.

This thesis focuses on LEO spacecraft, and specifically the ISS given the

unique challenges it presents for space logistics in general and propellant re-

supply in particular. The problem of minimizing ISS propellant usage begins

with a review of the current process for ISS trajectory design. This process

suffers from limited knowledge of the problem sensitivities due to its reliance

on simple rules of thumb, a lack of automation, and an inability to optimize

the ISS trajectory to minimize total ∆v. Repeat ground track orbits, while

conceptually similar to the ISS orbit, are driven by different requirements and

trajectory constraints than the ISS, and the algorithms used to both define

163



and maintain RGTOs are not appropriate to the ISS orbit.

A new framework for the ISS has therefore been presented to overcome

the deficiencies in the current process. This framework solves the constrained

burn optimization problem for the ISS in an automated manner, using total

∆v as the cost function and constraints imposed on LAN and SMA altitude.

Analytic derivatives for the cost function and variational equations for the con-

straints provide exact sensitivities for use in the differential corrections process.

Initial estimates for the controls are provided by the same rules of thumb that

serve as the mathematical core of the current process. These expressions are

implemented in a combined gradient-based optimizer / numerical integrator

framework to provide a local minimum total ∆v solution assuming fixed dates

for the burns. Randomly varying the burn dates and re-optimizing allows for

multiple local minimum solutions, which are easily scanned for a candidate

global minimum ∆v solution. This approach is successfully applied to both

notional and actual ISS trajectory event sequences.

The solution time and ∆v savings realized for the ISS problem motivate

application of this approach to other missions in LEO and beyond with inter-

spersed trajectory burns and constraints. For each such mission, this approach

provides exact partial derivatives of the constraints with respect to the burns

that can be used in a gradient-based search for locally optimal solutions.

While random selection of burn dates is helpful in identifying a can-

didate global minimum, a more systematic approach is ultimately desired for

faster convergence. A hidden genes genetic algorithm was implemented to

164



address this need, but its application did not result in solution diversification

due to genetically different cases mapping to physically identical cases, an issue

that is left for future work to resolve.

The above framework provides the first step towards the larger archi-

tecture built up in this work. For simplicity, this first stage assumes no uncer-

tainty in atmospheric density in its state dynamics. The next stage seeks to

develop a probabilistic model for predicting space weather. Historically, space

weather has been predicted using the MSAFE model along with limitations in

that model’s predictive performance. Gaussian Processes (GPs) are a natural

fit to the time series nature of space weather data, and are selected to pro-

vide a probabilistic model for predicting space weather that overcomes these

limitations.

A GP model of F10.7 data is constructed that utilizes the spectral

mixture kernel for its covariance function. The model selection and prediction

process is summarized and shown to be repeatable across two solar cycles

in 1-year increments, and is designed to be repeated at any frequency going

forward ad infinitum. Early stopping is used in the model selection process

to avoid overfitting. Given this safeguard, if a model appears to overfit the

training data but still performs well on the validation data, this is likely due to

one or more high frequency kernel components acting like noise. Future work

can employ a constrained optimizer in the model selection process to eliminate

these high frequency components.

The GPR prediction results are compared both qualitatively and quan-

165



titatively with MSAFE’s results and the former is shown to have better pre-

dictive performance on the test data. Finally, the GP model is applied to

the satellite lifetime prediction problem, highlighting the variation in orbital

lifetime due to F10.7 uncertainty.

The GP model selection and prediction framework can be readily ex-

tended to analyze Ap/Kp data, correlations between F10.7 and Ap/Kp, and

their joint effect on atmospheric density. Improvements in the framework can

also be explored, including using a constrained optimizer for selecting hyper-

parameters and optimizing the number of spectral mixture components.

The final stage in this work combines the first and second stages, and

explores the trajectory design and optimization problem for the ISS in the

presence of atmospheric uncertainty. A burn execution uncertainty model

is developed for the ISS along with equations to propagate and update the

trajectory dispersion covariance matrix and map the final state dispersions

to constraint parameters. These components form a linear covariance driver

that is used to analyze the effects of atmospheric uncertainty on constraint

dispersions. A key outcome of this analysis is a mechanism for identifying

when stochastic trajectory correction burns are needed to reduce constraint

uncertainties. The above optimization framework is updated using the linear

covariance driver, and a methodology is presented for solving ISS trajectory

planning problems under atmospheric uncertainty. The complete architecture

is then summarized for deployment in an operational setting.
