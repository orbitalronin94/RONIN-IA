A Markov Random Field Model for Texture-based Segmentation of Small Cracks in Thin Inconel 
Tubes 

 
Peter Spaetha, Russel A. Wincheskia, Eric Burkea, and David Dawickeb 

 
aNASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, VA, 23681-2199 
bAnalytical Services and Materials, Inc., Hampton, VA 23666 

 
ABSTRACT 

 
Markov random fields have been used for image segmentation since their introduction in the 
1980s. This work applies a method from Principal Component Thermography to enhance the 
contrast in damage regions in 3D images derived from X-ray computed tomography (CT) 
inspections. The developed method is applied to sizing of small cracks in thin Inconel tubes 
designed as probability of detection (POD) samples for radiographic inspection. Misclassification 
errors arising from artifacts due to beam-hardening are reduced by fitting the boundary of the 
segmented damage region with the arc of an ellipse. Results are compared against those 
obtained through manual inspection. 
 
Keywords: Image segmentation, Markov Random fields, Principal Component Thermography, 
Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC), crack inspection and metrology 
 

1. INTRODUCTION 
 
Techniques from image analytics and data science were applied to characterize small cracks in 
thin Inconel tubes. Three-dimensional images derived from x-ray computed tomography were 
segmented using a texture-based Markov Random Field (MRF) approach [1]. Beam hardening 
and other artifacts in the CT images cause significant difficulty in automatically segmenting crack 
regions in the specimens in an obvious fashion. An approach from Principal Component 
Thermography [2] enhanced the contrast of the crack regions by transforming the 3D image into 
a more easily segmented 2D summary image. 
 
We observe that the binary segmentation of the summary image into damaged and non-
damaged regions contains misclassified pixels. A more accurate measurement of the damaged 
region is produced by estimating the boundary of the segmentation as an arc of an ellipse. 
Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) [3] overlays a Least Squares fit of an elliptical arc to the 
segmented damage region boundary. Finally, our results are compared against those obtained 
through manual observation. 
 

2. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP  
 
Thin Inconel tubes were prepared as fatigue crack specimens to be used in probability of 
detection (POD) testing of radiographic inspection methods. The tubes, with a nominal 1” 
diameter, were machined to produce a reduced wall thickness in the central, gauge, section. A 



small circumferential notch was placed in the midsection of the tubes to act a stress concentrator 
for fatigue crack growth through high cycle fatigue. Following crack growth, the wall was further 
thinned, removing the starter notch. The final target wall thickness and crack depth were .030” 
and .021” respectively.   
 
The experimental design of the POD testing specified the as-manufactured length and depth of 
each crack in the sample set.  These measurements were to be accomplished using a combination 
of eddy current (EC), scanning election microscopy (SEM), and CT imaging. High frequency EC 
scans were performed to locate and mark the location of the cracks after notch removal. SEM 
imaging was then applied to measure the surface length of the cracks. Finally, CT imaging was 
applied to measure the crack depth. 
 
High resolution images of the tubes were produced using a micro focus X-ray CT with a maximum 
resolution of 5 microns and magnification up to 160x. A 16-bit amorphous silicon digital detector 
with a 2000 x 2000-pixel array was used to collect radiographs at each rotation angle as the X-ray 
path intersected the sample (360 degrees in 0.11-degree increments). Samples were positioned 
with the center of rotation aligned with the marked EC crack location. The source to tube distance 
was minimized to achieve maximum gain, resulting in a voxel size of approximately 10 microns.  
The three-dimensional reconstruction of the collected radiographs produced tomographic data 
that could be viewed along any plane in the sample volume. Grayscale 32-bit float images of the 
region of interest (typical dimensions of 1300 x 900 x 500 voxels) were obtained from the X-ray 
CT inspections of the Inconel specimens. The tube axis of the images was parallel to the 𝑧-axis 
and the crack face was in the 𝑥𝑦-plane. A sample image corresponding to 𝑧 = 243 can be seen

4. CONCLUSIONS 

 
Initial results suggest that the PCA projection used extensively in active thermography combined 
with a supervised texture-based segmentation model can be used to quantify crack dimensions 
in thin Inconel tubes. Fitting the boundary of the segmented damage region by an elliptical arc 
using a recursive method such as RANSAC provides a more accurate boundary of the indication 
and agrees well with manual inspection. Ongoing out of sample testing on additional data 
combined with comparisons against destructive crack measurement data will help assess the 
quality of the model.
