Robust adaptive mesh refinement for transonic conditions is one of the most difficult challenges to advancing
CFD usage for certification by analysis. Transonic fluid-structure interaction cases represent an even more
difficult challenge for adaptive mesh refinement. Production aircraft furthermore have structural models that
have gaps and discontinuities that make the interdomain transfer of loads and deflections difficult. This adds
to the challenge of adaptive mesh refinement. An initial attempt to create an adaptive mesh refinement process
that refines regions of high flow gradient based around the HeldenMesh software is presented. The method is
applied to the Integrated Adaptive Wing Technology Maturation (IAWTM) configuration. That has developed
an aspect ratio 13.5 wing aeroelastic wind-tunnel model for testing in the Transonic Dynamics Tunnel (TDT).
The high levels of deformation of this wing make the adaptation of meshes for the static shape difficult and has
necessitated some creativity in how to efficiently adapt meshes. A time-domain identification of the unsteady
system based on flexible modes using the adapted meshes is performed.



I. Introduction
As pointed out by Ref. [1], solution convergence by global refinements on independently generated and proport-



edly high quality grids has been difficult to achieve. For this and other reasons, the NASA CFD Vision 2030 study
has identified automated mesh generation, adaptivity and error management as critical needs with the goals of im-
proving solution efficiency, robustness and minimization of error [2]. Automated adaptive mesh refinement (AMR)
that accounts for geometry and flow physics to reduce error is seen as a means to achieve solution grid independence
in a systematic and painless way. It also has the added benefit of potentially enhancing efficiency if refinement is
combined with coarsening of the grid. Some applications have complex multiscale fluid physics, as in vorticity shed-
ding or shock structures in the case of transonic flows. While the advantages are clear, robust AMR for such flows is
one of the most difficult challenges to advancing CFD usage for certification by analysis. Mesh anisotropy to align
a mesh with flow features and error estimation are deemed essential for successful AMR. Combining AMR with an
error metric is one of the current challenges [3]. Various error metrics coupled with mesh enhancement have been
proposed, as in adjoint methods with a global objective, having been used for steady and unsteady flows [1, 4]. On the
other hand, error estimates using local residuals have been used. A more heuristic means attempts to keep the product
of the field gradient (pressure, vorticity, velocity) times the grid spacing uniform across the entire mesh [5]. Methods
along a similar vane use the flow-field Hessian to formulate efficient anisotropic mesh spacing [6–8].



Adaptive mesh refinement has been applied to cases with deforming or merely moving boundaries, and in a

few instances, fluid-structure interaction [4, 5, 9–15]. In the case of time-varying geometry, resolving the details of
transient flows with boundary motion using adaptive mesh refinement during time integration can be prohibitively
expensive. For this reason, fluid-structure interaction (FSI) or moving boundary flow adaptations have been done
typically for simple geometries, or over relatively short time durations, or have resorted to mesh updates at sparse time
intervals (e.g. Ref. [13]). One approach has been to use adaptive refinement on the steady state (rigid or flexed under
loading) and then solve the transient fluid dynamics without further adaptation [5, 9]. If the flow transients are expected
to be minimal, this renders significant advantages especially when a long simulation time record is required. One
such simulation, considered here, is a time-domain system identification for a production aircraft involving complex
geometry with high Reynolds number flow. This requires lengthy time histories over many cycles of motion to create
repeatable data records in the presence of nonlinear flow-fields.



Likewise, using anisotropic cells with unlimited aspect ratio to reduce the computational burden is acceptable for
boundaries that at most move rigidly [14, 16, 17]. In the case of production aircraft, sizable amounts of bending and
torsion limit the permissible aspect ratio of surface elements in order to maintain the integrity of the surface [14]. Fur-
thermore, the deformed surface of a production aircraft with a detailed structural model may not always be smoothly
varying. Surface pressure spikes and ridges such as shown in Figure 1 can result from a deflected flap hinge line and
from the interpolation within the gap between two flaps. Shown in that figure are the hinge line of one flap and the
interpolated region between it and another. The hinge lines and flap edges have already been pre-refined. Features
such as these should not be further refined by the adaptation software. For these reasons, constraints need to be placed
on the allowable anisotropy, and on where and on what surface adaptation takes place. Robustness of the resulting
mesh is also critical. It certainly should be possible to put some limited constraints into existing automated AMR
software. That capability is already available in a limited sense for the AMR software refine used with a wall-modeled
LES (WMLES) simulation [18]. Recent computations combining refine [23] with the NASA FUN3D solver and its
mesh deformation show it is not fully robust for production aircraft deforming surfaces [14]. Furthermore, the NASA
Langley FUN3D CFD software, used here, requires a fixed-mesh topology and currently does not allow for adapting
a deforming volume and surface mesh.



For these reasons the present study will leverage the already advanced spacing control available in the commercial
software HeldenMesh [19]. A certain amount of user intervention is necessary at this time to preclude the afore
mentioned issues. It is possible to call the HeldenMesh tool within the CFD solver, and the ultimate goal is of course
full automation. The HeldenMesh suite of tools generally produces exceptional quality meshes and seems to be rapid
and robust. The case geometry and structure modeled here results in very high deflections. While HeldenMesh can
parameterize a surface and deform it before remeshing, a highly deformed wing with separately deforming flaps may
be difficult to parameterize and regrid. For this reason, the regridding will be done around the original shape. The CAD
model of the original configuration is embedded in the HeldenMesh restart file so that all mesh changes will follow
the CAD surface. Finally the deformed mesh solution can be interpolated to the new mesh, and static computations be
resumed.



It is assumed that the geometry features such as leading edges, corners, geometric discontinuities or attached
boundary layer flows have already been sufficiently resolved by the various automated feature resolving capability in
HeldenMesh. Flap hinge lines and edges have also to be prerefined. The remaining flow features to be resolved are
shock surfaces, separated boundary layers, wake or shed vorticity. At this time, there is no error criteria enforced and
the refinement requires modest user input. It must be understood that this work is somewhat preliminary. This paper
will discuss the process used to adapt the mesh, relative robustness compared to other mesh adaptation processes, and
present results.

II. Adaptation Process
A variety of sources are available in HeldenMesh to cluster meshing. Source shapes available are hexahedral



blocks, cylinders, annular regions, tetrahedra, prisms, spheres and ellipses. Ellipses are appealing because of their
directional control on spacing and source shape. The source used here will be the hexahedral block because it allows
definition of three-direction spacing and aspect ratio at each of eight block corners. That allows tight control over
spacing. No direct creation of HeldenMesh source input is necessary, since the user introduced and controlled adapta-
tion is automated, but still requires some user input. The approach taken here will be to adapt the mesh at the statically
deflected solution and vehicle shape before commensing the time-accurate simulation. The a priori mesh generation
will necessarily need to take into account the expected motion of features such as the shock during the time-accurate

the flow-field to surface motion may occur away from steady flow features, mesh quality away from adapted features
is also considered important. This is addressed by extending multiple blocks ahead of and downstream of flow features
being resolved. Currently there is no automated process to quantify flow-field gradients, assess error and modify the
mesh. Rather, the refinement is based on surface pressure gradients, shock extent into the flow-field and velocity field
in general.



The FUN3D v14 is used in this study. Reference [22] indicates the use of the Spalart-Allmaras (SA) model without
corrections because the consistency of implementation is easier to establish than with its corrections. Current work has
also found that the uncorrected SA model is a little more robust for unsteady problems with moving boundaries. For
these reasons, the uncorrected SA model is used. The Roe flux-difference scheme is used with the Venkatakrishnan
limiter with a coefficient of 0.01. Figure 4 shows the overall process. The first step, on the lower left of that figure, is to
generate a statically deflected solution on a relatively coarse mesh. From that solution, features that require refinement
can be identified. Surface pressures and velocities from flow cross section planes indicate where the mesh needs to
be refined. The second step is to extract locations of surface points that frame those features. An automated process
transfers those points back to the original shape and automatically creates assemblies of blocks, and the HeldenMesh
source ASCII input. Once HeldenMesh has created the new mesh, the refine interpolate functionality in the refine
software interpolates the static aeroelastic solution from the original mesh to the new mesh. The static aeroelastic
solution can be restarted from that point.

Reference [22] indicates that some solutions in the drag prediction workshop DPW-4 exhibited a large side of body
separation bubble that was not observed in the wind-tunnel results. While there is some uncertainty whether such a
separation bubble should be present, the present capability can handle well the clustering that would be required.
Figure 10 illustrates the ability to create a mesh clustering in a wedge shaped region at the wing/fuselage junction.
This is useful in refining a separation region that might occur in that area at high angle of attack. Figure 10a shows the
isosurface of 0.01 streamwise spacing. The spacing refinement curves around the junction. As seen in Figure 10b, the
clustering decays smoothly away from the junction.

IV. Conclusions
This paper has presented a method of selectively refining flow features using the HeldenMesh software and the



CFD code FUN3D on a high aspect ratio aeroelastic wind-tunnel model. The difficult application of a production
aircraft undergoing large deformation having anomalous surface features is considered. A reasonably painless method
of mesh refinement of high flow gradients is presented. Examples of the quality of the meshes produced is shown and
both spatial and time convergence is demonstrated for the present application. Finally, the system identification using
multimodal multisine time-marching versus linearized frequency domain are compared.



The current method requires some user intervention and it would appear there is some necessity to identify regions
to exclude from further refinement. Future work would include making the method more automated, but still with user
specified constraints applied to the mesh adaptation. These will be ongoing efforts.
