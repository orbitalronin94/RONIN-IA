<p>NASA’S EARTH SYSTEM OBSERVATORY— ATMOSPHERE OBSERVING SYSTEM 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Scott A. Braun1, John Yorks1, Tyler Thorsen2, Dan Cecil3, Dalia Kirschbaum1 
</p>
<p> 
1NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Greenbelt, MD 
</p>
<p>2NASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, VA  
3NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, AL 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>ABSTRACT 
</p>
<p>NASA has begun pre-formulation studies for the Earth 
</p>
<p>System Observatory, a constellation of observatories 
</p>
<p>designed to implement the recommendations of the 2017 
</p>
<p>NASA Earth Science Decadal Survey. For the atmosphere, 
</p>
<p>the Atmosphere Observing System (AOS) will focus on 
</p>
<p>aerosols, clouds, convection, and precipitation, their mutual 
</p>
<p>interactions, and interactions with atmospheric radiation. 
</p>
<p>AOS consists of two projects, one in an inclined orbit to 
</p>
<p>measure sub-daily variability across all times of day, 
</p>
<p>particularly for deep convection and its attendant high clouds, 
</p>
<p>and one in a polar orbit to provide globally distributed 
</p>
<p>observations with more advanced capabilities, coupling to 
</p>
<p>radiation, and an eye toward continuity of key cloud and 
</p>
<p>aerosol data records. This paper describes the AOS science 
</p>
<p>objectives, the architectures of the two projects, planned sub-
</p>
<p>orbital activities, and relevant applications.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Index Terms— Aerosols, clouds, convection, 
</p>
<p>precipitation, radiation 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>1. INTRODUCTION 
</p>
<p>The NASA Earth Science Decadal Survey [3] recommended 
</p>
<p>a range of Earth Observing programs to be advanced by 
</p>
<p>NASA over the following decade. Included among those 
</p>
<p>recommendations were a set of 5 designated observables 
</p>
<p>(DOs) focused on aerosols (A); clouds, convection, and 
</p>
<p>precipitation (CCP); mass change; surface biology and 
</p>
<p>geology; and surface deformation and change. From late 
</p>
<p>2018 through 2020, NASA conducted studies to explore 
</p>
<p>architectures that could implement the DOs within cost and 
</p>
<p>schedule constraints. In response to those studies, NASA 
</p>
<p>announced the Earth System Observatory (ESO) 
</p>
<p>(https://science.nasa.gov/earth-science/earth-system-
</p>
<p>observatory), which will implement the five DOs as a 
</p>
<p>coordinated constellation. Because aerosols, clouds, and 
</p>
<p>precipitation are fundamentally linked at the microphysical 
</p>
<p>scale, the aerosol and CCP DOs were studied (under the name 
</p>
<p>ACCP) and are being implemented as a pair of joint missions 
</p>
<p>addressing aspects of the combined science under the 
</p>
<p>preliminary name, the Atmosphere Observing System 
</p>
<p>(AOS), https://aos.gsfc.nasa.gov/. This paper summarizes the 
</p>
<p>AOS science objectives, the spaceborne architecture and 
</p>
<p>suborbital program, and expected applications.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>2. AOS SCIENCE OBJECTIVES 
</p>
<p>The Decadal Survey identified a range of science objectives 
</p>
<p>related to A and CCP, rating them as important, very 
</p>
<p>important, or most important. The ACCP study team focused 
</p>
<p>on three of the “most important” science objectives related to 
</p>
<p>convective formation processes, pollution processes and 
</p>
<p>distribution, and climate feedback and sensitivity to 
</p>
<p>formulate a set of related ACCP science objectives (Table 1) 
</p>
<p>that are also adopted by AOS.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Table 1: AOS Science Objectives 
</p>
<p>Low clouds: Determine the sensitivity of boundary layer 
</p>
<p>cloud bulk physical, microphysical and radiative 
</p>
<p>properties to large-scale and local environmental factors 
</p>
<p>including thermodynamic and dynamic properties. 
</p>
<p>High clouds: Relate the vertical structure, horizontal 
</p>
<p>extent, ice water path, microphysical and radiative 
</p>
<p>properties of convectively generated high clouds to 
</p>
<p>convective vertical transport and large-scale high clouds to 
</p>
<p>environmental factors. 
</p>
<p>Convective storm systems: Relate vertical motion within 
</p>
<p>convective storms to their a) cloud and precipitation 
</p>
<p>structures, b) microphysical properties, c) latent heating, 
</p>
<p>d) local environment conditions, e) ambient aerosol 
</p>
<p>loading, and d) diurnal variability.  
</p>
<p>Cold clouds and precipitation processes: Detect and 
</p>
<p>quantify the vertical structure, cloud bulk, microphysical 
</p>
<p>and radiative properties (including mixed-phase 
</p>
<p>precipitation and snowfall) of high-latitude cloud systems.   
</p>
<p>Aerosol attribution and air quality: Quantify optical and 
</p>
<p>microphysical aerosol properties in the boundary layer and 
</p>
<p>free troposphere to improve process understanding, 
</p>
<p>estimates of aerosol emissions (including diurnal 
</p>
<p>variability), speciation, and predictions of near-surface 
</p>
<p>particulate concentrations.  
</p>
<p>Aerosol wet removal, vertical redistribution, and 
</p>
<p>processing: Relate the vertical structure of aerosol 
</p>
<p>properties to cloud and precipitation properties to improve 
</p>
<p>understanding of processes impacting aerosol vertical 
</p>
<p>transport, removal, overall lifecycle, aerosol processing. 
</p>
<p>Aerosol direct effects: Reduce uncertainties in estimates 
</p>
<p>of clear and all-sky shortwave direct radiative effects 
</p>
<p>(DRE) and provide measurements to constrain the </p>

<p>anthropogenic contribution to the DRE. Quantify 
</p>
<p>vertically resolved aerosol radiative heating rates to 
</p>
<p>improve understanding of the impacts of absorbing aerosol 
</p>
<p>on atmospheric stability. 
</p>
<p>Aerosol indirect effects: Provide measurements to 
</p>
<p>constrain process-level understanding of aerosol-cloud 
</p>
<p>interactions to improve estimates of aerosol indirect 
</p>
<p>radiative forcings. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>3. ORBITAL ARCHITECTURE 
</p>
<p>To explore potential observing systems to address the ACCP 
</p>
<p>science objectives, the ACCP study team identified a list of 
</p>
<p>required geophysical variables and their characteristics in a 
</p>
<p>science and applications traceability matrix 
</p>
<p>(https://aos.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/ACCP_SATM_Rel_Candida
</p>
<p>te_G.pdf). The team explored more than 50 architectures and 
</p>
<p>recommended a two-orbit solution to NASA HQ 
</p>
<p>(https://aos.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/ACCP_Science_Narrative-
</p>
<p>2021.07.19.pdf) that became the baseline AOS spaceborne 
</p>
<p>architecture. AOS consists of two projects (Fig. 1), currently 
</p>
<p>in the mission concept study phase known as Pre-Phase A. 
</p>
<p>The first project, with an estimated launch date of mid 2028 
</p>
<p>and denoted AOS-I, includes sensors in an inclined 
</p>
<p>(nominally 55°), precessing orbit to deliver early science 
</p>
<p>focused on sub-daily time scales, with an emphasis on deep 
</p>
<p>convection [4], high clouds [5], and aerosols [2]. The second 
</p>
<p>project, with an estimated launch in early 2030 and denoted 
</p>
<p>AOS-P, includes advanced sensors in a polar orbit, nominally 
</p>
<p>at an 0130 equatorial crossing time, focused on globally 
</p>
<p>distributed observations to assess aerosol-cloud-
</p>
<p>precipitation-radiation interactions and their role in climate 
</p>
<p>and climate change. These two projects are covered in the 
</p>
<p>following subsections. An additional measurement 
</p>
<p>component of AOS is a sustained suborbital program, 
</p>
<p>described in section 4, to address science that cannot be 
</p>
<p>addressed from space or can be addressed better from 
</p>
<p>airborne or ground-based observing systems. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>3.1. Inclined orbit 
</p>
<p>The baseline AOS-I project includes two satellites carry a W-
</p>
<p>band radar, a Ku-band Doppler radar using the Displaced 
</p>
<p>Phase Center Antenna (DPCA) approach [1], a two-
</p>
<p>frequency (532 and 1064 nm) backscatter lidar, a high 
</p>
<p>frequency (nominally 89 to 325 GHz and possibly higher) 
</p>
<p>passive microwave radiometer, a multi-angle polarimeter 
</p>
<p>[channels from ultra violet (UV) to shortwave infrared 
</p>
<p>(SWIR)], and a pair of stereo visible-frequency cameras 
</p>
<p>designed to use measurements approximately 45 seconds 
</p>
<p>apart to infer cloud or aerosol plume top horizontal and 
</p>
<p>vertical air motions. The cameras drive the need for the two 
</p>
<p>spacecraft.  
</p>
<p>As part of pre-Phase A, the team is examining potential 
</p>
<p>international contributions to the inclined orbit from the 
</p>
<p>Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) and the Centre 
</p>
<p>National d'Etudes Spatiales (CNES). JAXA may provide a 
</p>
<p>wide-swath Ku-band radar with nadir DPCA Doppler 
</p>
<p>capability while CNES may provide a pair of passive 
</p>
<p>microwave radiometers (89-325 GHz) for time-differenced 
</p>
<p>brightness temperatures to infer evolving microphysical 
</p>
<p>properties and vertical mass flux.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>3.2. Polar orbit 
</p>
<p>The baseline AOS-P project includes a single satellite 
</p>
<p>carrying W- and Ka-band DPCA Doppler radars, a passive 
</p>
<p>microwave radiometer (118-880 GHz), high spectral 
</p>
<p>resolution lidar (HSRL at 532 nm, backscatter at 1064 nm), 
</p>
<p>multi-angle polarimeter (similar to AOS-I), a UV to SWIR 
</p>
<p>shortwave spectrometer, and a Canadian Space Agency 
</p>
<p>(CSA) contributed longwave and far infrared spectrometer. 
</p>
<p>The CSA is also exploring the addition of another satellite in 
</p>
<p>the polar orbit carrying aerosol and moisture limb sounders, 
</p>
<p>with a focus on properties of the upper troposphere and lower 
</p>
<p>stratosphere.   
</p>
<p>4. SUB-ORBITAL SCIENCE 
</p>
<p>The AOS includes a suborbital science component designed 
</p>
<p>to collect data for investigations that cannot be adequately 
</p>
<p>addressed from spaceborne measurements alone. Satellite 
</p>
<p>“blind zones” can come into view with airborne or surface-
</p>
<p>based instrumentation that provides higher spatial resolution 
</p>
<p>and/or views from below overlying cloud and precipitation 
</p>
<p>layers. Surface-based measurements also provide temporal 
continuity that will be lacking from the low-earth-orbit 
</p>
<p>measurements.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The ESO-AOS suborbital program is organized around three 
</p>
<p>science themes with highly synergistic objectives. Those 
</p>
<p>themes include low clouds and aerosol-cloud-radiation 
</p>
<p>interactions; deep convection and high clouds; and aerosol-
</p>
<p>cloud-radiation interaction, aerosol attribution, and aerosol 
</p>
<p>redistribution. These science themes have numerous 
</p>
<p>interrelated aspects. Investigation of these themes will also 
</p>

<p>require many measurements that are required for 
</p>
<p>development and evaluation of the satellite-based algorithms, 
</p>
<p>and for calibration/validation of the orbital instruments. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Implementation strategies for the suborbital program include 
</p>
<p>a mix of leveraging and augmenting existing surface-based 
</p>
<p>measurement capabilities, partnering in relevant external 
</p>
<p>field campaigns, and leading a small number of major 
</p>
<p>airborne field campaigns. Existing operational or research 
</p>
<p>measurement sites may be augmented by deployment of 
</p>
<p>instrumentation such as research radars, profilers, in-situ 
</p>
<p>aerosol and precipitation instrumentation, and research 
</p>
<p>aircraft overflying heavily instrumented surface sites. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Multi-aircraft campaigns will likely include a mix of satellite-
</p>
<p>simulator remote-sensing instruments and in-situ 
</p>
<p>measurements. They will be designed to address 
</p>
<p>complementary themes. For example, a multi-aircraft 
</p>
<p>program from a coastal or tropical island location may 
</p>
<p>include flights measuring aerosol characteristics, cloud and 
</p>
<p>precipitation initiation and aerosol interactions in shallow 
</p>
<p>oceanic cumulus, and the growth and evolution of deep 
</p>
<p>tropical convection systems. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>5. APPLICATIONS 
</p>
<p>Due to rapid changes in the Earth system and the need to 
</p>
<p>thrive on our changing planet, the transition of scientific 
</p>
<p>research to applications is essential. A focus on the cross-
</p>
<p>benefit of science and applications through partnerships 
</p>
<p>between scientists and stakeholders can reduce the timelines 
</p>
<p>from analysis to decision and increase the value and benefit 
</p>
<p>of NASA's Earth Science missions. The AOS Applications 
</p>
<p>Team solicits, synthesizes, and articulates stakeholder needs 
</p>
<p>throughout the study and development phases of the mission 
</p>
<p>to ensure that applications are considered to the greatest 
</p>
<p>extent possible in mission design.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The applications activities have centered around stakeholder 
</p>
<p>community engagement, the development of a Community 
</p>
<p>Assessment Report (CAR), internal participation on science 
</p>
<p>and engineering working groups, and discussion with other 
</p>
<p>Designated Observable mission teams. Stakeholder feedback 
</p>
<p>has been solicited and gathered through thematically focused 
</p>
<p>community workshops, focus groups, individual interviews, 
</p>
<p>and surveys. The synthesis of this information, in addition to 
</p>
<p>knowledge acquired from previous mission application 
</p>
<p>activities, form the basis for identifying potentially high-
</p>
<p>impact applications (Fig. 2) and supporting the development 
</p>
<p>of the CAR. The goal of the AOS CAR is to provide relevant 
</p>
<p>information on stakeholder organizations and their needs that 
</p>
<p>can be used to inform AOS design and instrument 
</p>
<p>considerations, algorithm needs, data latency, and data 
</p>
<p>product generation. Throughout mission development, the 
</p>
<p>applications effort will focus on engaging early adopters, 
</p>
<p>conducting trainings, and continuing to engage with 
</p>
<p>stakeholder communities to further refine opportunities, 
</p>
<p>challenges, and needs. 
</p>
