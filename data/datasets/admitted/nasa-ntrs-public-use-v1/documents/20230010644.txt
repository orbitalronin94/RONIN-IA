Background
• The International Space Station (ISS) Air Quality Monitors 



(AQMs) monitor trace concentrations of 23 targeted volatile 
organic compounds in the ISS atmosphere 



• Two units with differing GC column chemistries are 
used to cover the target list



• Target compounds were chosen based on years of 
archival data



• Compounds on the target list had one or more of the 
following characteristics



• Compounds frequently detected in spacecraft atmosphere 
(ethanol, acetone, xylenes, and 2-butanone)



• Compounds with significant toxicity at low concentrations, 
even though they are detected infrequently on spacecraft 
(acrolein, benzene)



• Compounds that can affect ECLSS (siloxanes and alcohols)
• The target list is fluid, dictated by experience and 



changes in materials of construction or ECLSS 



• The data from the AQM is used by NASA toxicologists as 
part of the assessment of air quality aboard the ISS



• Some archival sampling using grab sample containers (GSCs) 
continues (~ every 45 days; 1 GSC is always in the U.S. LAB)



• Samples collected at visiting vehicle ingress
• The AQM can also provide data for ECLSS troubleshooting
• In case of contingency, the AQM can help to monitor clean 



up efforts











Successes











Support for Investigation of Potential Ammonia Leak
• On January 14, 2015, an alarm indicated an ammonia leak near Node 2



• Crew evacuated to the Russian Service Module safe haven
• There were pressure fluctuations in the US LAB indicative of an ammonia leak
• Crew did not smell ammonia or experience symptoms



• ECLSS engineers reviewed downlinked data and suspected a false alarm. 
There were only two options to verify, however:



• Send the crew, with PPE into the unknown LAB atmosphere to take Dräger tube 
measurements



• Control the AQM from the ground to analyze the atmosphere and downlink the data
• The AQM can provide trending data for ammonia based on a shifted RIP



• AQM was activated and data was received that showed no ammonia in the 
atmosphere 



• Given the seriousness of the situation, a subsequent analysis was performed using 
other techniques. These verified that no ammonia was present











Air Quality Monitor (AQM) Role in Resolution of 
Potential Ammonia Leak



• AQM data confirmed the analysis of the ECLSS personnel, and the 
crew was sent back to the LAB (with PPE) with high confidence that 
they were not entering a harmful environment



• The AQM permitted confirmation of the LAB atmosphere situation 
without putting the crew or their safe haven at risk

Recent Concerns



2-Propanol











SpaceX-Demo1 (SpX-DM1)



• Docked on March 3rd, 2019
• AQM runs started within 30 minutes of hatch opening
• First ingress GSC collected at hatch opening
• Report of unknown odor reported by crew in Russian segment service 



module, so expedited AQM results were requested
• Results were nominal except for 2-propanol (IPA)



• AQM1 reported 5.9 mg/m3



• Expedited analysis underestimated true AQM value (saturation)











ISS Nominal IPA



• IPA regularly seen in pre-flight offgas
tests



• Upon docking, offgas products will 
diffuse through the stack



• AQMs in LAB v/s visiting vehicle hatch
• Concentrations measured by the AQM at 



first ingress should be lower than that 
collected in GSCs



• IPA never observed by AQM above 0.8 
mg/m3 when started at first ingress



• 7-day/180-day SMAC for IPA is 150 
mg/m3; total alcohol guideline for U.S. 
Water Recovery System is 5 mg/m3











Increased Atmospheric Monitoring
• High IPAAQM led to MCC-H request 



to monitor atmosphere more 
often (daily/as needed)



• After initial drop to 2.2 mg/m3 on 
March 4th, IPAAQM returned to 
saturated levels for remainder of 
docked operations



• Other compounds were stable



• After undock on March 8th, IPAAQM
returned to nominal levels



• Contingency GSCs collected 
during docked ops were returned 
and analyzed shortly after SpX-
DM1 return.

occurred in the JSC Environmental Chemistry Labs; final prep (w/Autorun) performed in offsite flight hardware labs



• Autorun sequence: 2 High-temp clean 
runs, Regular clean run, then No sample 
run before any sample runs



• Presence of IPA/acetone prior to any 
sample runs suggests contamination



• This was not seen during calibration and 
science verification testing of AQM2



• While VOC usage is controlled in the TEC 
labs, there are no controls in place in 
the flight hardware labs



• IPA is commonly used to clean flight 
hardware



• Why is it showing up in runs where a 
sample is not collected?

Conclusions from Ground Testing
• Powering On



• If there is no IPA present, powering on the AQM results in no IPA contamination in the initial clean/no 
sample runs



• If the AQM is powered on in an atmosphere containing IPA, contamination is present
• If the IPA is removed, then clean runs will successfully remove the IPA



• If the AQM is powered on in an atmosphere containing IPA, and that source remains, then IPA accumulates 
in the system, regardless of the scrubbing provided by the sieve cartridges



• Normal Ops
• If there is an extremely large transient [IPA], a large peak will be seen in the sample runs



• If the IPA is removed, clean runs will remove the IPA from the system
• If high [IPA] is present in the atmosphere, it will be pulled into the AQM during clean/no sample runs via the 



purge, leading to contamination
• If this source is removed, the AQM functionality can be recovered following clean runs



• These results suggest that continued operation of the AQM in the presence of 
elevated IPA could lead to interference in normal target compound measurements 











IPA Anomaly Conclusions
• If our understanding of what happened during SpX-DM1 and NG-11 is 



correct, then it suggests that our approach to calibration may not be 
appropriate



• Current (and legacy) calibration methods utilize a “slip-stream” 
approach, in which the AQM sample pump pulls from an ~ 150 
cc/min flow of standard



• Purges included with the methods simply pull from the lab 
atmosphere



• This assumes that potentially-high analyte concentrations in-flight are 
transient



• If the elevated analyte concentrations are long-term, we could 
overreport due to carryover











Recent Concerns



Benzene











Background
• On April 13, 2020, AQM1 (VF624 column) detected benzene above the 



reporting limit
• Other compounds seemed normal, so AQM1 appeared to be working 



nominally
• Data from AQM1 showed increasing benzene concentrations that exceeded 



the 180-day and 30-day SMACs
• After failing in mid-June, 2020, AQM1 was returned on SpX-DM2 to support a 



benzene source investigation
• Archive air samples returned on SpX-DM2 showed no detectable benzene in 



the ISS atmosphere
• Focus of investigation shifted to determining what caused false positive 



readings for benzene on AQM1
• Following repair of AQM1, testing began at the JSC Environmental Chemistry 



Lab

Benzene Anomaly Conclusions



• Initial detection/reporting of benzene by the AQM in 2020 led to a large amount of concern 
by multiple NASA organizations



• Deeper analysis of the data indicated that it was unlikely to be benzene
• Archival samples confirmed that no benzene was present



• Ground testing of the AQM showed that benzene was not being produced in the system
• Trace VOCs found in archival samples did not coelute/interfere with benzene detection
• No identification of “unknown peak” was achieved



• Repeat of “benzene anomaly” in early 2022 was easily attributed to the same(?) interfering 
peak; No concern from the toxicologists

Where is the Elevated IPA Coming From?
• No significant releases of IPA were reported during NG-11 docked operations, and 



no payloads containing large quantities of IPA delivered or operating in this 
timeframe.



• However, new hardware that could be releasing IPA into the ISS atmosphere was
delivered on NG-11



• Charcoal HEPA Integrated Particle Scrubbers (CHIPS)
• The filters were processed in a facility that uses IPA, and a final cleaning was 



performed with IPA
• The charcoal could potentially adsorb IPA and release it into the atmosphere



• Thermal Amine Scrubber (TAS)
• CO2-removal Technology Demonstration
• Ground testing showed elevated IPA in some samples
• Acrylonitrile also present in some samples



• Traces of acrylonitrile detected in ISS GSCs returned on SpX-18, SpX-20, and 
SpX-DM2
