Velocities.

A novel method to measure the velocity of ejecta generated by the impingement of rocket
plumes on a lunar or planetary surface is presented. Dust Ejecta RADAR Technology (DERT),
is a Millimeter Wave Doppler Radar sensor that provides a unique dataset based on direct
measurements of ejecta particle velocities during plume-surface interactions, which will help
inform risk assessments associated with impacts by high-speed ejecta particles. DERT has
measured velocities of a pendulum ( 1 m/s), a rotating strip (60 m/s), projected regolith simulant
BP-1 ( 250 m/s) and rifle rounds ( 820 m/s). Signal-to-noise ratios of up to 60 dB with regolith
simulant BP-1 have been demonstrated with a CW 94 GHz unit. Data collected from DERT
will inform the development of high-fidelity computational models of plume-surface interaction
effects and will help address NASA Strategic Knowledge Gaps related to characterizing entry,
descent and landing effects, and the risks associated with high-speed ejecta on the lander and
lunar surface assets. Recent efforts to develop DERT as a flight instrument are presented as well
as the development of a Lunar Regolith Ejecta Simulator (LuRES) system to calibrate DERT.

II. Introduction



Within the next few years, NASA plans to land astronauts on the surface on the Moon to explore more of the lunar
surface than ever before for scientific discovery. This goal of long-term lunar surface exploration will be enabled



by the construction of a lunar surface base camp, called Artemis Base Camp, and a small, human-tended space station
orbiting the Moon, called Gateway. Longstanding human exploration and habitation plans for the lunar surface will
require multiple landings and launches on the lunar surface in order to transfer equipment, supplies, and personnel
between Gateway and the lunar surface. During descent and landing, a lunar lander’s rocket exhaust plume will interact
with surface regolith causing cratering and the generation of a stream of high-speed, abrasive particles. Depending on
their speed and trajectory, the ejecta may pose risks to both nearby and distant surface assets, including the Artemis
Base Camp and the historic Apollo sites. The speed of the ejecta particles may even be sufficiently large to inject them
into lunar orbit where they would potentially risk orbiting assets. The subsequent ascent from the lunar surface, e.g.,
using an ascent vehicle to return lunar samples to Earth or an orbital Gateway platform, may also produce plume surface

The only data that exists on extraterrestrial ejecta velocities are from video footage of the Apollo lunar module
landings. Analysis of the footage has indirectly estimated and modelled particle velocities >2 km/s from PSI [1, 2].
Such particles can impact any lunar latitude and can reach lunar altitudes that could intersect with objects in low-lunar
orbit or Gateway [3]. High-speed PSI ejecta pose a significant risk to lunar surface assets due to impact energy/flux
and abrasive blasting effects [4]. Risk assessment requires knowledge of PSI ejecta particle size, velocity, and density.
However, to-date, there have never been any direct measurements of extraterrestrial particle velocities accelerated by
plume-surface interactions. The MWDR provides a unique dataset based on direct measurements of ejecta particle
velocities during plume surface interactions, which will help inform risk assessments associated with impacts by
high-speed PSI ejecta particles. The PSI data obtained by the MWDR will also inform the development of high-fidelity
computational models of PSI effects and will help address NASA Strategic Knowledge Gaps related to characterizing
entry, descent and landing effects, and the risks associated with high-speed PSI ejecta on the lander and lunar surface
assets. Recent terrestrial launches have demonstrated the catastrophic power of launching rockets without launch pads.

Millimeter Wave Doppler Radar (MWDR) has been developed as descent, guidance, and landing radar for space
applications, military applications, cloud monitoring instruments for meteorology applications, mechanical vibration
sensors, imaging instrumentation, automotive radar’s, interferometry applications, and human movement detectors
for security and surveillance applications [5–16]. MWDR has also been used to study sand- and dust-storms, and
dust devils [17–20]. It is currently being used by CloudSAT in the C-train Constellation in Earth orbit to study cloud
abundance, distribution, structure and radiative properties [10]. Here we present how a 94 GHz MWDR can be used to
measure accelerated regolith particle velocities in PSI, and our recent advances in the Technology Readiness Level
(TRL) of such an instrumentation for future lunar and mars landers.



III. Method
MWDR works by transmitting RF waves that scatters off objects where the return signal’s frequency is shifted in



proportion to the velocity of the objects. Smaller particles (< 300 `m) are expected to have velocities from 0.1 – 2.5
km/s and are in Rayleigh Scattering regime (D < 0.1_) [2]. The signal strength received depends upon particle diameter,
index of refraction, particle count along the radar cone (mass flow rate from plume) and the physical parameters of the
radar itself. The analog voltage received from the down converting heterodyne mixer is sampled, and then transformed
into the frequency domain. Bulk velocity data are limited to returned signals that are sufficiently above the Noise Floor

( 10dB Signal to Noise Ratio (SNR)) and frequency data that is not derived from known interference: i.e. backgrounds
and vibrations, instrumentation artefacts, RF spurs, etc. Secondary measurements from other instruments improve upon
understanding of particle ejecta events.

The MWDR instrument used for DERT Prototype development is a 94 GHz Continuous Wave (CW) Radar with a
Voltage Controlled Attenuator (VCA) on the transmit chain. The VCA can attenuate the transmitted signal by 25 dB
for an output range of about 0 dBm to 24.5 dBm. At 24.5 dBm transmit power, there are safety considerations which
require us to cordon off an area to be in accordance with the IEEE Standard for Safety Levels with Respect to Human
Exposure to Electric, Magnetic, and Electromagnetic Fields, 0 Hz to 300 GHz [21]. Our large 35 dBi antennas as seen
in Fig. 2 were used for most of our experiments, except when not in a safe configuration or the target measurement area
was too close.



Fig. 3 DERT Prototype testing at Johns Hopkins University Jet Facility and Shadowgraph Setup



In order to calibrate DERT, experiments were conducted at John Hopkins University (JHU) using their Jet Facility
and Shadowgraph Setup. JHU has the ability to generate a consistent high speed dusty gas flow that enabled DERT
to make measurements that could be correlated with a High Speed Camera and Particle Image Velocitmetry (PIV)
analysis. Fig. 3 shows the experimental setup at JHU. A DERT Prototype was placed at the same elevation as the
particles leaving the nozzle. The radar unit was placed at a 45 degree offset, with the focal point oriented directly at the



3











nozzle exit. A high-speed camera is used to independently verify the gas flow velocity. A common trigger between
the High Speed Camera, Jet Flow initiation and Spectrum Analyzer was used. Trigger data was recorded separately
and analyzed for accuracy. Radar absorptive material is used to minimize radar reflections. Data from this experiment
establishes returned signal strength against a consistent mass flow rate.



Fig. 4 LuRES polyimide conveyor belt setup



Another method that was developed at Kennedy Space Center to test the regolith velocity measurement capabilities
of DERT is the Lunar Regolith Ejecta Simulator (LuRES). LuRES was developed to allow a user to customize and fix
the distribution of scattering targets on the belt so that a well-characterized target can be presented to an electromagnetic
radiation source to calibrate a PSI measurement instrument such as DERT. The conveyor consists of a polyimide belt,
drive motor, drive roller, secondary roller, radar absorption panels, a high-speed camera, and support structure. The
drive and secondary rollers are horizontally spaced to span the belt enough to appropriately sample the radar cross
section (RCS) as projected at the distance of the LuRES from the DERT instrument. A laser pointer was used to check
the alignment of the RCS onto the belt. The belt tension is adjusted by moving the relative positions of each roller. The
drive motor contacts the drive roller which in turn rotates the belt. The belts linear velocity is controllable between 0
and approximately 5 m/s using a DC motor with a pulse width modulated controller. A radar zero-point on the belt is
produced by the segment of tape that holds the belt together (green tape shown in Fig. 4).



Regolith samples are adhered to the outer side of the belt and opposite from the site of the zero-point. The resulting
radar data includes a strong signal for the zero point and an expected position in the time series for the regolith signal. A
high-speed camera is used to independently verify the belt velocity. Radar absorptive material is used to minimize radar
reflections. BP-1 lunar regolith simulant particle size and surface area that was used (to correlate to radar return signal)
is determined by a high-powered microscope. Fig. 5 shows the images of the analysis of the particle size and density
using the microscope method.

IV. Results



A. Johns Hopkins University Jet Facility and Shadowgraph Tests and Analysis



Fig. 6 An example single high speed camera frame overlapped with extracted particle tracks for a stream of
BP-1 lunar regolith simulant. The frame rate, pixel scale, and tracked vector of the particles was used to calculate

To independently assess the accuracy of the DERT Prototype, the frequency output from Keysight N9020B Spectrum
Analyzer was compared to High-Speed Camera (HSC) data of the BP-1 stream from a Shimadzu HPV-X2. The HSC
data was analyzed frame-by-frame for particle velocity and concentration using the trackpy Python package [23]. To
minimize source confusion in the HSC data, conservative values for edge/object detection, linking, and tracking were
used. Fitted tracks perpendicular or anti-parallel to the bulk stream motion were rejected and attributed to residual
source confusion. This ensured only positive velocity values were recorded from the HSC data Fig. 6. A 2 million
frames per second rate, and a 13.1 µm per pixel scale, was used to calculate the particle velocities. The DERT velocity
data were extracted from the Spectrum Analyzer output using the find_peaks Python function in the scipy library.
Both the DERT and HSC velocity distribution data were fitted with gaussians for comparison Fig. 7, as were the total
detections for concentration comparisons Fig. 8.



Fig. 7 Comparison between high-speed camera derived velocities and DERT derived velocities. The straight
line indicates a one-to-one relationship.



Fig. 6 demonstrates that particle detection and tracking from the HSC data still suffers from source confusion
despite tight controls on the fitting algorithms. There is a clear anisotropy in the particle size distribution where large



5











particles at low density are predominately to the left, while small particles at higher densities are to the right. This
means that the particle streams used to derive velocities from the HSC data may not temporally coincide with the
window during which DERT collected velocity data. It can also be seen that small particles are not tracked consistently.
This is due to the irregular shape of BP-1 particles. As the particles tumble their brightness varies. This causes these
particles to drop in and out of the noise, and thus not be tracked. Similarly, there are large diffuse signals that could
either be shadows of foreground particles or irregular BP-1 particles with low reflectivity. Therefore, while a significant
number of particles are tracked in the HSC data, there is a population of small, and potentially large, particles that are
not included in the final velocity distributions and concentrations. Fig. 7 demonstrates that DERT consistently measures
velocities below those derived from the HSC. In addition, there is no clear offset between the derived velocities that
would indicate a calibration issue with either DERT or the HSC. As demonstrated by Fig. 6, the particle size distribution
and concentration varies significantly within a single stream of particles. In addition, the HSC only records a small
spatial window of the entire stream. DERT, with a 3-degree radar opening angle, even at a range of 0.15 m, samples a
much larger spatial region. Consequently, DERT will record particles that have decelerated rapidly once leaving the
nozzle, specially the large particles with large cross sections. So, while the velocity comparisons between DERT and
the HSC do not directly correlate, they are within the differences expected from inconsistent spatial sampling of the
particle streams between DERT and the HSC.



Fig. 8 demonstrates the particle concentrations calculated from the HSC compared to the number of individual
peaks fitted from the DERT Spectrum Analyzer data. As with Fig. 7, there is no clear correlation between the HSC
concentration and the number of DERT detections. Nevertheless, what can be seen from the data is DERT consistently
detects particle sizes, both for glass beads and BP-1, of 125 µm. DERT also demonstrates the detection of glass bead
particles between 40 and 70 µm. DERT struggles to detect BP-1 particles between 38 and 75 µm. This result can be
understood by the scattering efficiency of spherical particles (glass beads) compared to irregularly shaped particles
(BP-1). The lack of direct correlation can again be attributed to the inconsistent spatial sampling of DERT and the HSC.



Fig. 8 Comparing the particle concentrations from the high-speed camera data and the DERT data for two
different types of particles (glass beads and BP-1) and two difference particle size distributions.



In summary, the JHU tests of DERT have shown that this prototype instrument can detect BP-1 particles down to at
least 125 µm in diameter. However, the direct comparison of the HSC data gathered and the contemporaneous DERT
data shows that further testing is needed. The field of view of the HSC and the geometry of the DERT radar cross
section must be managed in such a way as to draw velocity data that can be fairly compared between the two. These
observations led the team at KSC to develop the experimental test set up Lunar Regolith Ejecta Simulator (LuRES).

V. Discussion
DERT has been successfully tested with both high speed metallic objects and low speed regolith particles. Developing



test fixtures to calibrate a DERT Prototype has proved challenging and will require further experimentation. Future
work for DERT is to advance the TRL of the instrument by testing in vacuum environments and with hot fire PSI
testing in a large vacuum chamber. Further calibration of DERT will be conducted with the LuRES test setup as well as
Electromagnetic (EM) simulation and modeling software with CST Microwave Studio that will refine our measurement
capability. DERT may one day become standard equipment on Landers, including Landers that will implement 94 GHz
Doppler Radar as a landing radar, which would be a perfect match for our instrument as we can combine our sensor with
an additional receiver chain on to a landing radar unit.



VI. Conclusion
A sensor for ejecta velocities has been developed, and demonstrated repeatedly with both high speed metallic objects



and low speed regolith particles in an accelerated stream. The instrument has acquired velocity data that has been
validated by comparison of a well understood pendulum motion, known radial velocity of a DC Motor, and a High Speed
Camera. Velocities of up to 820 m/s have been verified with solid metal projectiles and velocities up to 250 m/s have
been verified with a lunar regolith simulant (BP-1). Sufficient Signal to Noise Ratio’s (SNR) of up to 60 dB with regolith
simulant BP-1 have been demonstrated with our 250mW vacuum compatible system []. Ongoing work has shown that
DERT is a reliable tool for measuring ejecta particles. Advancements in post processing methods can improve our
understanding of PSI phenomenon and enable us to assess the risk in visiting other planetary bodies. In conclusion, this
work informs the community of a technology capability of determining the kinetic energy in plume-surface interaction
ejecta. Kinetic energy from PSI effects must be a well characterized parameter if we are going to have a sustained
presence on the Moon or Mars.
