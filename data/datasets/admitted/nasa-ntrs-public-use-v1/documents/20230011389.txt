The baseline analysis consists of Monte Carlo results for primary and back up launch opportunities as well as the maximum possible value (MPV) and the current best estimate (CBE) mass properties. Further analysis beyond preparation for powered flight is required to understand and mitigate challenging dynamic stability during the transonic regime. Concept of Operations


The POST2 simulation begins 10s prior to cruise stage separation. The entry vehicle is separated from the cruise stage using a spring mechanism and is designed to eliminate far-field recontact risk. After entry interface, the vehicle undergoes peak heating and deceleration. Then the drogue parachute deploys during a 5 s window after reaching a sensed acceleration, using a smart trigger to minimize the angle of attack. This is to ensure the entry vehicle does not become unstable and that the drogue parachute opening loads are within requirements. The vehicle continues until the drogue lines are cut and the main parachute is deployed. The heatshield is jettisoned 120 s after the main parachute is deployed. The lander is lowered into a “posed” position after main parachute deployment. By 800 m and a vertical descent of 2.9 m/s, the lander is released. The simulation continues to track all jettisoned bodies to their respective touchdowns.






























































Vehicle and Parachute Design



























































Fig 4. Geometry of the drogue and main parachute (not to scale)


Fig 3. Entry vehicle geometry [1]


The entry vehicle design, a 4.5 m 60° half-angle sphere cone, is based on the Genesis Sample Return Capsule (GSRC) and was modified to meet requirements for lander geometry and mission heating.





This work adapted the aerodatabase from GSRC. The GSRC free molecular and hypersonic data was adjusted using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) analysis. Relative to the previous study the data collected from the Langley Transonic Dynamic Tunnel (TDT) has been incorporated to the aerodatabase. Due to the geometry of the capsule, there are dynamic stability challenges during the transonic regime that require further investigation.





At PDR, The EDL vehicle used a 5.4 m Disk Gap Band (DGB) drogue parachute and 16.55 m DGB main parachute that were modelled as separate, six-degree-of-freedom rigid bodies. The drogue parachute is mortar deployed. The deployment, inflation, and opening loads are defined by the models provided by the parachute design team. The models are expected to update with upcoming drop tests. The main chute canopy is expected to be updated to a Ringslot canopy.















































































































































Preparation for Powered Flight


One of the novel challenges for the Dragonfly mission is the lander release segment. This section presents the preparation for powered flight modeling efforts made for mission PDR. The lander is lowered into the “posed” position while attached to the backshell to avoid clearance issues while the rotors are turned on. As the vehicle is descending, the rotors are used to control the lander’s yaw rate (Fig 6). The lander release trigger is initialized at 1000 m to meet optimal release conditions for the lander to separate. Further logic is included in the release mechanism to ensure the lander is released no lower than 800 m (Fig 5). 





Due to complexity of the dynamics, this problem is being modeled concurrently by the POST2 EDL simulation, the APL Mobility simulation, and the LM ADAMs simulation. Fig 8 shows the model integration including an aerodatabase (ADB) for the entry vehicle without the heatshield (PPF ADB) and a Despin Controller provided by APL to ensure consistency among all simulations.

Dragonfly is a New Frontiers Program mission led by Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory (APL) which will deliver a rotorcraft lander to Saturn’s moon, Titan [1]. Relative to previous studies presented, Dragonfly recently passed its mission Preliminary Design Review (PDR) [2]. This study presents the updated design and analysis performed for the Dragonfly mission PDR. The entry vehicle analysis includes the phases of entry and descent prior to cruise stage separation until lander separation. These results discuss the robustness of the overall entry sequence as assessed through a Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis as well as sensitivity analysis.

The integrated trajectory is simulated and analyzed using Program to Optimize Simulated Trajectories II (POST2) [3]. Models are provided by interdisciplinary teams from APL, the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL), NASA Ames Research Center (ARC), NASA Langley Research Center (LaRC), Lockheed Martin (LM), and University of Central Florida (UCF). Fig 1 shows an overview of the models and the corresponding model owners. 





During the design analysis cycle, the models are incorporated and combined in POST2, which enables overall trajectory performance assessment. The integrated trajectory is provided to model developers to support the subsystem design process. These models will be refined as the design matures during Phase C and as hardware is built.
