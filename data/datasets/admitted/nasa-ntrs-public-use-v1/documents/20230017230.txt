<p>Concept of Operations of Cryogenic Fluid 
</p>
<p>Management for Nuclear Thermal 
</p>
<p>Propulsion 
</p>
<p>Matthew Duchek1, Sean Greenhalge2, and Will Machemer3 
Analytical Mechanics Associates, Denver, CO, 80211, United States of America 
</p>
<p>Cryogenic Fluid Management (CFM) is a critical capability for a hydrogen NTP vehicle 
</p>
<p>for a crewed mission to Mars. AMA has investigated the concept of operations for zero boiloff 
</p>
<p>CFM systems and provided feasibility assessment for a zero-boiloff CFM system based on 
</p>
<p>gravity-independent technologies.  This includes identification of CFM-critical mission stages, 
</p>
<p>modeling of CFM operational modes in steady state and transient conditions, thermal analysis 
</p>
<p>of the NTP vehicle and CFM systems, hydrogen leakage analysis, and system margins and 
</p>
<p>fault tolerance. This work illustrates the feasibility of a zero boiloff mission with a set of 
</p>
<p>technology performance assumptions. 
</p>
<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>Nuclear thermal propulsion (NTP) is under development by NASA space nuclear propulsion (SNP) project as 
</p>
<p>propulsion for a crewed mars mission. For this mission, NTP utilizes hydrogen as propellant, stored as liquid hydrogen 
</p>
<p>(LH2). NTP achieves greater architectural robustness compared to traditional chemical systems by utilizing a nuclear 
</p>
<p>fission reactor to heat hydrogen to high temperatures for high specific impulse (Isp) rocket thrust. Due to the low 
</p>
<p>atomic mass of hydrogen, NTP produces twice the Isp compared to chemical propulsion, reducing propellant mass and 
</p>
<p>enabling faster transit times. Additionally, NTP offers programmatic flexibility, such as wider launch windows and 
</p>
<p>mission abort options. These capabilities are vital to facilitate manned missions to Mars.  
</p>
<p>To efficiently store and transport the hydrogen propellant during the Mars mission, it must be cryogenically cooled 
</p>
<p>at 20 K to prevent boil-off. Otherwise, the tanks require venting from over-pressurization, which results in significant 
</p>
<p>propellant losses. Zero boil-off (ZBO) is achieved through passive and active CFM. The technology to achieve ZBO 
</p>
<p>for hydrogen for long duration missions has yet to be demonstrated, but advanced cryogenic storage concepts have 
</p>
<p>been and are in development that combine cryocoolers, broad area cooling (BAC) networks, high-performance multi-
</p>
<p>layer insulation (MLI) and thermal isolation design [1] [2]. 
</p>
<p>This paper outlines a ZBO CFM system for a Mars mission and describes the analysis techniques and technology 
</p>
<p>assumptions made to estimate the heat lift required of cryocoolers and the sensitivity of the system to variations in 
</p>
<p>technology performance assumptions. The technologies included are based on those tested by NASA projects [3] [4] 
</p>
<p>[5] [6]. 
</p>
<p>In addition to steady-state thermal performance analysis, this work treats several transient events at a high level. 
</p>
<p>These phenomena could result in the need for venting during transient events or the incorporation of additional CFM 
</p>
<p>technologies like mixing. The post-launch transient presents the greatest risk of a need for propellant venting, as the 
</p>
<p>heat loads are orders of magnitude larger before the spacecraft reaches steady-state temperature compared to after.  
</p>
<p>This highlights the importance of a quick orbit insertion, separation, and power-up of the active CFM systems.  
</p>
<p>Ultimately, the analysis and research presented demonstrate the importance of continued investment in CFM 
</p>
<p>technology development and focused analysis of CFM for the NTP application to mitigate risks to the feasibility of 
</p>
<p>an NTP vehicle.   
</p>

<p>II. Description of CFM System 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>CFM manages the storage and transfer of cryogenic propellants for extended duration missions. In order to reduce 
</p>
<p>vehicle mass and mission transit durations, CFM aims to eliminate propellant boiloff and leakage. CFM technologies 
</p>
<p>are outlined in Figure 1.  
</p>
<p>Figure 1. Cryogenic Fluid Management Technologies 
</p>
<p>A. Description of CFM Technologies 
</p>
<p>Spray-on foam insulation (SOFI) is applied to the liquid hydrogen tanks for thermal isolation in atmospheric 
</p>
<p>environments and multi-layer insulation (MLI) surrounds the SOFI for thermal isolation in high-vacuum 
</p>
<p>environments. Two zones of broad area cooling (BAC) is integrated around the propellant tank through distributed 
</p>
<p>cooling networks of tubes. The first BAC zone is coupled to a shield in the tank insulation, and the second zone is 
</p>
<p>coupled to the tank wall itself. Copper straps intercept heat from the support struts to the BAC shield to be lifted by 
</p>
<p>the 90K-class cryocooler. Thermal isolation of the tank from the BAC shield is critical to maximizing the heat lift at 
</p>
<p>the BAC shield, which in turn maximizes the overall efficiency of the system. Load-bearing MLI and SOFI insulates 
</p>
<p>the tank from the shield. Reverse turbo-Brayton cycle cryocoolers expand and cool the helium working fluid to 
</p>
<p>circulate heat away from the tank. The tube-on-shield BAC integrates with a high capacity 90K-class cryocooler to 
</p>
<p>reduce the heat flux substantially and efficiently into the liquid hydrogen tank. The tube-on-shield BAC is a uniformly 
</p>
<p>distributed tubing network attached to an aluminum shield embedded in the MLI. The tube-on-tank BAC integrates 
</p>
<p>with a 20K-class cryocooler to directly control tank pressure and prevent boil-off. Each zone has two additional fully 
</p>

<p>B. CFM Operations 
</p>
<p>Over the course of the mission, heat from the 
</p>
<p>environment and spacecraft components result in 
</p>
<p>unacceptable heat leaks into the liquid hydrogen 
</p>
<p>propellent tanks. MLI reflects away the bulk of the 
</p>
<p>environmental radiation, but inefficiencies and 
</p>
<p>structural interfaces introduce significant heat leak 
</p>
<p>into the tank. In order to limit liquid hydrogen boil-
</p>
<p>off and control the tank ullage pressure, tube-on-
</p>
<p>shield and tube-on-tank broad area cooling (BAC) is 
</p>
<p>integrated with reverse turbo-Brayton cryocoolers as 
</p>
<p>depicted in Figure 4. 
</p>
<p>The tube-on-shield BAC significantly reduces the 
</p>
<p>heat flux into the tank and is capable of maintaining 
</p>
<p>the shield at 55K in the cis-lunar environment, which 
</p>
<p>is the hottest environment in the reference mission. 
</p>
<p>The tube-on-tank 20K-class cryocooler directly 
</p>
<p>controls ullage pressure and maintains the ZBO even 
</p>
<p>under the transients investigated. 
</p>
<p>This optimized dual-zone configuration results in 
</p>
<p>almost half the mass and power requirements 
</p>
<p>compared to a single-zone, tube-on-tank 
</p>
<p>configuration with multiple 20 K, 20 W class 
</p>
<p>cryocoolers [7]. This is because the 20 K cryocoolers 
</p>
<p>are significantly less efficient due to the large temperature difference between the cold point and the heat rejection 
</p>
<p>point.  
</p>
<p>The 90K-class cryocooler is assumed to be able to run at a range of temperatures, down to 50K. Operating such that 
</p>
<p>the shield is at 55K was found to be the optimal set point for steady state operations [8]. At lower temperatures the 
</p>
<p>cryocooler does not have the same level of heat lift. During transients, the shield heats up, allowing the 90K-class 
</p>
<p>cryocooler to operate at a higher heat lift capacity. 
</p>

</p>
<p>investigate other missions using similar assumptions. This section will describe the mission utilized for this set of 
</p>
<p>analyses.

<p>A. Mission Overview 
</p>
<p>A conjunction mission with LDHEO aggregation is assumed for 
</p>
<p>this analysis. The NTP vehicle analyzed consists of multiple stages 
</p>
<p>assembled in cis-lunar space, one segment at a time: the habitation 
</p>
<p>module followed by inline stages 1, 2, and 3 followed by the core

</p>
<p>burn times and transient analyses. Aggregation in lower Earth orbits 
</p>

<p>B. Vehicle Architecture 
</p>
<p>The NTP crew vehicle consists of a habitat, three inline stages, and the core stage. The inline stages required to 
</p>
<p>supply the liquid hydrogen propellant for transport to and from Mars are identical. Additionally, many of the 
</p>
<p>subsystems and structures are common between the core and inline stages, including the RCS bus, solar arrays, 
</p>
<p>radiators, and avionics.  
</p>
<p>The liquid hydrogen tanks are constructed of an aluminum lithium alloy to reduce thermal conductivity. Tank 
</p>
<p>sizing assumes a maximum ullage pressure of 40 psi. Composite struts secure the tanks and provide thermal isolation 
</p>
<p>from the spacecraft bus. The avionics, batteries, cryocoolers and RCS tanks are also mounted to the aluminum skirt 
</p>
<p>buses via struts for improved thermal isolation. Additionally, heat from the bus is collected via pumped cooling loops 
</p>
<p>and rejected via graphite reinforced composite radiators with titanium-water heat pipes. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>IV. Analyses Overview 
</p>
<p>To evaluate CFM system performance and feasibility, a 1-D thermal model was developed in Simulink. The model 
</p>
<p>simulates the spacecraft’s overall internal and external thermal sources and heat transfer modes throughout all mission 
</p>
<p>phases using the vehicle architecture as defined above. The key performance parameters of the individual CFM 
</p>
<p>technologies to achieve ZBO were identified to substantiate a viable technology development and maturation plan. 
</p>
<p>The 1-D model is parameterized in order to rapidly evaluate technology performance and iterate on the system design. 
</p>
<p>In addition, Thermal Desktop was utilized to check environmental heating rates in Mars loiter orbit, confirming 
</p>
<p>heat loads used in the 1D model were reasonable, and cross-checking radiation between internal components. 
</p>

<p>The cryocoolers are modeled using proportional only control, which is sufficient given the relatively long 
</p>
<p>timescales for the hydrogen temperature to increase. The 90K-class cryocooler controls the BAC shield temperature 
</p>
<p>to 55 K and the 20K-class cryocooler controls the tank temperature to 20 K. The model does not account for parasitic 
</p>
<p>and integration losses in the refrigeration system from the cryocoolers, BAC tubing network, and helium working 
</p>
<p>fluid. The cryocoolers are modeled as directly lifting heat at the loads. 
</p>
<p>The liquid hydrogen propellant is modeled as a thermal mass of bulk temperature, which does not account for 
</p>
<p>localized heating and stratification. Additionally, perfect heat transfer is assumed between the propellant tank wall 
</p>
<p>and the liquid hydrogen. The details of heat transfer effects will influence the effectiveness of the CFM system, but 
</p>
<p>this is beyond the scope of this paper. 
</p>
<p>The 1-D model assumes all the avionics are powered on and the radiator rejection temperature is 300 K. Thermal 
</p>
<p>Desktop was used to determine the thermal radiation contribution from component view factors given the vehicle 
</p>
<p>geometry. This is described in the section on Internal Radiation Analysis below. 
</p>
<p>A. Steady State  
</p>
<p>Steady state analyses include the entire vehicle stack, assuming the vehicle is on-axis with the sun in cis-lunar 
</p>
<p>orbits, LDHEO and NRHO. The nominal steady state case assumes the reactor vessel temperature is at 300 K and the 
</p>
<p>habitat is occupied. The vehicle attitude is not controlled in the cooler Mars loiter orbit in order to minimize RCS 
</p>
<p>propellant expenditure. 
</p>
<p>The steady state cryocooler heat lift to maintain the temperature set points in LDHEO, NRHO, and Mars orbit is 
</p>
<p>captured in Table 3. The maximum cryocooler heat lift represents the worst-case thermal environment for the 
</p>
<p>respective orbits at solar cycle maximum and zero degrees inclination. For all stages, the largest heat rate into the 
</p>
<p>tanks is from the bus. Copper straps intercept heat from the support struts to the BAC shield to be lifted by the 90K-
</p>
<p>class cryocooler. Thermal isolation of the tank from the BAC shield is critical to maximizing the heat interception at 
</p>
<p>the BAC shield, which in turn maximizes the overall efficiency of the system. Load-bearing MLI and SOFI insulates 
</p>
<p>the tank from the shield. Additionally, a thermal resistance of 100 K/W is included at the tank struts and run pipe 
</p>
<p>interfaces to resist heat flow into the tank. If this resistance is not inherent in the tank integration, thermal isolating 
</p>
<p>joints can be used to limit the conductive heat transfer at the contact joint. The heat lift margin for the 90K and 20K-
</p>
<p>class cryocoolers in the worst-case thermal environment is 100% and 300% respectively. 
</p>

<p>1. Launch &amp; Ascent 
</p>
<p>As discussed above, each NTP stage is assumed to reach steady state in the terrestrial environment (~300 K) at 
</p>
<p>launch, whereas the liquid hydrogen propellant is initially at 20 K. The stage then experiences varying thermal 
</p>
<p>environments over the course of the ascent timeline. The cryocoolers are powered-on one hour after launch following 
</p>
<p>orbit insertion. This assumed duration until cryocooler on is expected to be conservative, but additional launch vehicle 
</p>
<p>trajectory analysis is needed to confirm. 
</p>
<p>The bulk liquid hydrogen temperature and the corresponding heat lift required of the two zones of refrigeration is 
</p>
<p>plotted in Figure 7. 
</p>
<p>The 90K-class cryocooler operates at full power for approximately 20 hours to intercept heat from the warm 
</p>
<p>spacecraft. The 20K-class cryocooler operates at full power for approximately 10 days in order to cool the liquid 
</p>
<p>hydrogen to 20 K. 
</p>
<p>Although the peak transient temperature of the liquid hydrogen is negligible, if the duration until cryocooler power-
</p>
<p>on is extended, the liquid hydrogen will act as a heat sink and absorb heat from the warm vehicle. This highlights the 
</p>
<p>need for an aggressive orbit insertion, launch vehicle separation, and power-up of the NTP stages. The largest heat 
</p>
<p>rate is from the insulted BAC shield through the load-bearing MLI. Furthermore, thermal stratification could further 
</p>
<p>exasperate the risk of boil-off if natural convection doesn’t sufficiently distribute the heat in the hydrogen. 
</p>

<p>2. NTP Burns 
</p>
<p>For the transient analysis of the NTP burns, the reactor vessel is modeled as a temperature source of 1000 K during 
</p>
<p>burn operations, 575 K during cooldown, and 300 K steady state. Additionally, nuclear radiation heating is modeled 
</p>
<p>as heat flow rates of approximately 47 kW to each nuclear radiation shadow shield, 5.3 kW to the BAC shield, and 30 
</p>
<p>kW to the liquid hydrogen propellant [11]. NTP burns considered for various mission architectures include 15-30 
</p>
<p>minute burns. For the sake of this analysis, a 30 minute worst case burn is utilized in the models. 
</p>
<p>The cryocoolers are powered off for the duration of the burn in order to save power and maintain pressurization in 
</p>
<p>the tank. The liquid hydrogen is modeled as a thermal mass equal to the remaining propellant in the core tank at 
</p>
<p>completion of the burn. The thermodynamics resulting from engine ingestion, inertial acceleration, direct nuclear 
</p>
<p>heating, and autogenous pressurization is a topic for future study. 
</p>
<p>As captured in the peak liquid hydrogen temperature in the core tank during the burn transient is a function of the 
</p>
<p>thermal environment, burn duration, and propellant fill level in the core. The burn transient is modeled as initially 
</p>
<p>steady state with the vehicle attitude on-axis to the sun. Throughout the burn, the attitude is not maintained to be 
</p>
<p>collinear with the sun vector and instead is collinear with the thrust vector. Longer burn durations and reduced thermal 
</p>
<p>capacitance from lower fill levels results in the most significant transient temperatures of the bulk liquid hydrogen. 
</p>
<p>However, the temperature difference for the nominal burn transients is negligible. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>10 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Additionally, the MOI and TEI burns and subsequent cooldowns are modeled sequentially in order to reflect 
</p>
<p>compounding transients. A more detailed burn timeline is needed to refine the model.Of interest is the engine soakback 
</p>
<p>following the burn plotted in. The soakback peaks about five and a half hours after the engine burn. This is followed 
</p>
<p>by engine cooldown and nuclear decay which delays the return to steady state until approximately five days after the 
</p>
<p>burn. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 8. Engine Soakback from 15-Minute NTP Burn 
</p>
<p>A worst-case burn is considered, with a high duration and low tank fill. The burn lasts for 30 minutes, and the core 
</p>
<p>tank is approximately 20% full upon completion. The bulk liquid hydrogen temperature and the corresponding heat 
</p>
<p>lift required of the two zones of refrigeration is plotted in Figure 9. The 90K-class cryocooler operates at full power 
</p>
<p>for approximately one and a half days to intercept heat from engine soakback. The 20K-class cryocooler operates at 
</p>
<p>full power for approximately 45 days in order to cool the liquid hydrogen to 20 K. The peak transient temperature of 
</p>
<p>the liquid hydrogen is 22 K, which corresponds to a saturation pressure of 22.8 psia. Given a maximum tank pressure 
</p>
<p>of 40 psia, the factor of safety for the worst-case transient is 1.75. The heat load caused directly by the soakback on 
</p>
<p>the tank is minimal due to the low conductivity paths from the engine to the propellent. 
</p>

<p>The sensitivity of CFM performance to key system parameters was analyzed based on the required heat lift by the 
</p>
<p>20K and 90K-class cryocoolers to maintain the tank temperature at 20 K and the BAC shield temperature at 55 K 
</p>

<p>The required heat lift at the tank is most sensitive to the thermal isolation from the bus. The predicted thermal 
</p>
<p>conductivity of the tank support struts (kstrut) for S-glass shank struts with titanium inserts based on current state-of-
</p>
<p>the-art is 1.46 W/mK. The required cryocooler heat lift at the tank and the BAC shield in LDHEO for a range of 
</p>
<p>conductivities from S-glass to titanium is plotted on the left in Figure 12. Although the strut conductivity has a 
</p>
<p>significant impact on required cryocooler heat lift, there is no anticipated obstacles to achieving a strut thermal 
</p>
<p>conductivity of 1.46 W/mK. 
</p>
<p>Thermal isolation of the tank from the BAC shield is also critical to maximizing the heat lift of the 90K-class 
</p>
<p>cryocooler, which in turn maximizes the overall efficiency of the system. Copper straps intercept heat from the support 
</p>
<p>struts to the BAC shield to be lifted by the 90K-class cryocooler; however, thermal resistance (Rtank) between the tank 
</p>
<p>and the BAC shield is necessary to resist heat flow into the tank. This is shown on the right in Figure 12. If there is no 
</p>
<p>resistance at the tank through the struts and the run pipe, the 90K-class cryocooler is rendered inoperable, and 80 W 
</p>
<p>of heat would need to be lifted at the tank by the 20K cryocooler. If at least 25 K/W of thermal resistance is not 
</p>
<p>inherent in the tank integration, thermal isolators can be used to limit the conductive heat transfer to the tank. The 
</p>
<p>design necessary to achieve sufficient thermal resistance has yet to be defined, but thermal isolators are of high heritage 
</p>
<p>for spacecraft applications. There is some inherent thermal resistance through the load-bearing MLI in the design. 
</p>

<p>D. Leakage Analysis 
</p>
<p>Liquid hydrogen is by nature a very small molecule. This results in traditional valves leaking stored liquid 
</p>
<p>hydrogen. It is necessary for space-rated cryogenic low leak valves to be developed. NASA is already in the process 
</p>
<p>of developing these low leak valves [12]. In addition, connecting each stage of the NTP spacecraft are low leak cryo-
</p>
<p>couplers. The following assumptions are made for each stage of an NTP spacecraft: 
</p>
<p>• Low-leak GH2 valve technology development is completed 
</p>
<p>• A single 8” valve is closed until first engine firing, and then reclosed between engine burns 
</p>
<p>• Three 3” valves are necessary per tank for vent, relief, and fill and drain 
</p>
<p>• Cryo-coupler full leakage rate is radial 
</p>
<p>• Valve full leakage rate is axial 
</p>
<p>• Leak rate in SCIM is Gaseous Hydrogen 
</p>
<p>Each valve has an assumed leak rate as described in Table 4. These leakage rates were developed in partnership 
</p>
<p>with NASA subject matter experts.  
</p>
<p>Table 4. Assumed Leak Rates of CFM Valves 
</p>
<p>Component Leak Rate (SCIM) 
</p>
<p>3” Valve 0.5 
</p>
<p>8” Valve 1.5 
</p>
<p>Cryo-coupler 1.5 
</p>
<p>The general layout of an NTP tank and associated valves can be 
</p>
<p>seen in Figure 13. Standard Cubic Inches per Minute (SCIM) is a 
</p>
<p>molar flow rate used for standard leak rates. This translates to moles 
</p>
<p>of liquid hydrogen lost over time. Each additional stage results in 
</p>
<p>additional cryo-couplers and valves, and therefore result in increased 
</p>
<p>propellent  lost. Additionally, leak rate becomes more of a potential 
</p>
<p>risk given longer mission times. This may be especially true in 
</p>
<p>mission architectures with several drop tanks, as the aggregation 
</p>
<p>timeline stretches, so does the potential for total leakage. However, 
</p>
<p>the assumption made that gaseous hydrogen is leaking and not liquid 
</p>
<p>hydrogen is a key assumption. In all missions investigated, the overall 
</p>
<p>leakage is minimal. However, if the assumption changes to liquid 
</p>
<p>hydrogen leaking, then the overall mass loss results in mission 
</p>
<p>failure. It is critical that further testing and development be perfromed 
</p>
<p>to ensure valve performance meets expectations. Low leak 
</p>
<p>technology testing and development is ongoing at NASA MSFC and 
</p>
<p>should remain high priority, as high TRL state-of-the-art valves result 
</p>
<p>in CONOPS failure as a result of leakage.

<p>E. Internal Radiation Analysis

<p>The 1-D model assumes all the avionics are powered on and the 
</p>

</p>
<p>to model two separate potential intra-spacecraft issues: 
</p>
<p>1) Determine the thermal radiation contribution from 
</p>
<p>component view factors given the vehicle geometry. 
</p>
<p>2) Determine the contributions from of heat flux from 
</p>
<p>radiation of internal heat sources, including the avionics 
</p>
<p>and RCS propellant tanks.  
The radiation from internal heat sources within the spacecraft bus 
</p>
<p>were determined to be negligible, assuming the internal bus surface 
</p>
<p>and components are wrapped in MLI; however, the radiation 
</p>
<p>backload from the radiators and solar arrays is significant to the bus 
</p>
<p>skirts and the tank.  Thermal Desktop was also utilized to check environmental heating rates in Mars orbit, confirming 
</p>
<p>the heat loads used in the model are reasonable.

<p>V. Comparison to RBO and Fully Passive CFM Systems 
</p>
<p>There are multiple reasons why the expense of a full ZBO CFM system may be undesirable. For shorter missions, 
</p>
<p>such as missions in cis-lunar space may be short enough that boil-off propellant mass is less than cryocooler mass. In 
</p>
<p>addition, pursuing Reduced-Boil Off (RBO) or a fully passive system reduces system complexity. In many Mars 
</p>
<p>mission architectures, several fully passive drop tanks are included in addition to ZBO main stage tanks. This section 
</p>
<p>attempts to take a preliminary estimate of boil-off mass comparisons between different cases. 
</p>
<p>A. System Descriptions 
</p>
<p>RBO and fully passive systems in this analysis both include the full layup described in Figure 2. The only difference 
</p>
<p>is the inclusion of the cryocoolers, and the associated BAC fluid loops. The passive system removes both classes of 
</p>
<p>cryocoolers and BAC. Two cases of RBO will be investigated, one with just the tube-on-tank 20K-class cryocooler, 
</p>
<p>and one with just the 90K-class cryocooler with tube-on-shield BAC. 
</p>
<p>B. Steady State Results 
</p>
<p>For the sake of this analysis, a simple single-stage vehicle will be modeled in each environment to demonstrate the 
</p>
<p>difference in boil-off rates of a stage given various environments. This assumes that all tanks are the same size, 10 
</p>
<p>meters in length and 8 meters in diameter. Table 6 shows the results of this preliminary analysis. The breakeven point 
</p>
<p>is calculated using the mass of the cryocoolers and associated BAC versus the mass of liquid hydrogen that is boiled-
</p>
<p>off and therefore unusable. Each case is compared to the masses of the cryocooler systems that are removed; the RBO 
</p>
<p>case with the 20K cryocooler breakeven point is compared to the mass of just the 90K system that is removed, etc. 
</p>
<p>The tank definitions are described in Table 5. The mass of the cryocooler systems includes the mass of the BAC and 
</p>
<p>the two redundant cryocoolers per system. The 90K cryocooler mass includes the mass of the shield. Pointing to 
</p>
<p>reduce heat load is still done in the cis-lunar environment, but not in the Mars loiter environment. The 20K only case 
</p>
<p>and the passive case do not include the load-bearing MLI.  
</p>

<p>The 90K-class cryocooler development has focused on operation in the 80-90K range. In this analysis it is assumed 
</p>
<p>to operate at colder temperatures which require additional development testing.  
</p>
<p>In addition, this breakeven comparison does not include system transients. In the following section transient 
</p>
<p>analysis is discussed in more detail.  
</p>
<p>C.  Transient Discussion 
</p>
<p> Large quantities of liquid hydrogen propellent are boiled-off during an NTP burn. This analysis assumes the 
</p>
<p>nuclear heating will be acting directly on the core stage. The worst-case 30 minute NTP burn is utilized. Table 7 shows 
</p>
<p>that the majority of the boil-off mass occurs during the burn itself, as opposed to during the soakback phase from the 
</p>
<p>engine. This is a result of the highly effective passive thermal isolation, as described above. For this preliminary 
</p>
<p>analysis it is assumed that all boiled-off propellent is unusable. However, it is likely necessary for a thermodynamic 
</p>
<p>vent system (TVS) to be utilized in this case. A TVS expands liquid hydrogen by opening a valve to vacuum prior to 
</p>
<p>venting it. Venting to vacuum causes a rapid expansion and vaporization, and therefore rapid cooling. This is done via 
</p>
<p>the use of a Joule-Thomson device. Cooled liquid is then routed back into the tank, effectively removing heat from 
</p>
<p>the hydrogen propellent. This allows for a high amount of cooling while venting off gaseous hydrogen. It is necessary 
</p>
<p>to vent hydrogen to maintain tank pressurization at 20 K without the use of a cryocooler, so a vent system must be 
</p>
<p>included. A TVS is incapable of throttling and maintaining specific heat lifts as the active cryocoolers can do. 
</p>
<p>However, in the case of a burn where a large amount of hydrogen is vented all at once, the cooling provided by a TVS 
</p>
<p>can have a large impact. By venting off hydrogen and cooling the tank, the system can effectively reduce the amount 
</p>
<p>of lost propellent due to boil-off. Further work is needed to capture the effective cooling done by such a system, as 
</p>
<p>well as to capture the additional mass a TVS would require. For RBO cases it is likely that a similar amount of boil-
</p>
<p>off will be present from the NTP burn, but when combined with a TVS for pressure control, the total amount may be 
</p>
<p>reduced.  
</p>
<p>This preliminary analysis assumes that the core tank receives the bulk of the direct nuclear heating and shields the 
</p>
<p>other tanks. However, drop tanks that are not in line with the core tank will also receive direct nuclear heating during 
</p>
<p>an NTP burn. Further analysis is needed to identify the amount of heating on these drop tanks. 
</p>

</p>
<p>Burn Duration 30 Minutes

<p>NTP Burn Boil-Off Mass 112kg

<p>The amount of boil-off for a 30 minute NTP burn is roughly 20% of the mass of a full ZBO system for the core 
</p>
<p>stage. Considering that multiple burns are necessary, in addition to boil-off during loiter in all environments and 
</p>
<p>aggregation, it is likely that a ZBO system may be more mass effective. However, this may depend on specific vehicle 
</p>
<p>architecture, and the effectiveness of the various critical technologies. While each stage must include the mass of the 
</p>
<p>associated CFM system, the core stage will take the bulk of the direct nuclear heating, as it will shield other inline 
</p>
<p>tanks. Tank fill levels at specific burns and the number of inline versus drop stages will impact the percentage of 
</p>
<p>propellent lost to boil-off from direct nuclear heating during burns. 
</p>
<p>D. Impact of Size of Tank 
</p>
<p>The drop tanks assumed for many Mars mission architectures are much smaller than other main stage tanks. Drop 
</p>
<p>tanks are assumed to utilize only passive CFM technologies. Compares how modifying the size of the drop tanks 
</p>
<p>might impact the boil-off rate of the tanks. However, the solar load assumptions require development. For the sake of 
</p>
<p>this preliminary analysis, the tanks were assumed 45 degrees onto the sun. Drop tanks in a real vehicle architecture 
</p>
<p>will have shading on each other, as well as from/on the rest of the vehicle. Figure 13 shows preliminary results of this 
</p>
<p>analysis. The percentage of hydrogen in the y-axis is calculated by taking the boil-off rate in kg per day, and dividing 
</p>
<p>it by the total propellent held by each respective tank size. Larger tanks result in a higher total loss from boil-off, but 
</p>
<p>less on a percentage basis.  </p>

<p>VI. Conclusion 
</p>
<p>Based on the analysis presented, with the assumption that technology development of CFM technologies is 
</p>
<p>successful, zero boiloff during steady-state operations appears feasible.  Additionally, the simplified thermal models 
</p>
<p>predict that the CFM system can endure the heat loads during driving transient operations cases (launch and engine 
</p>
<p>firing) without requiring venting of the liquid hydrogen for pressure relief. This is dependent on the maturation of all 
</p>
<p>critical technologies, including cryocoolers, BAC, thermal isolation joints, structural load-bearing MLI, and low leak 
</p>
<p>valves/cryo-couplers. A fully passive, or RBO system may possibly close with the inclusion of a TVS and highly 
</p>
<p>effective thermal isolation technologies, but when accounting for boil-off from transients it is likely that a ZBO system 
</p>
<p>will be more mass effective for a typical Mars mission architecture. Specific trades into vehicle architecture options 
</p>
<p>must be done to determine effectiveness for a given mission.
