Cryogenic Fluid Management (CFM) is a critical capability for a hydrogen NTP vehicle 



for a crewed mission to Mars. AMA has investigated the concept of operations for zero boiloff 



CFM systems and provided feasibility assessment for a zero-boiloff CFM system based on 



gravity-independent technologies. This includes identification of CFM-critical mission stages, 



modeling of CFM operational modes in steady state and transient conditions, thermal analysis 



of the NTP vehicle and CFM systems, hydrogen leakage analysis, and system margins and 



fault tolerance. This work illustrates the feasibility of a zero boiloff mission with a set of 



technology performance assumptions. 



I. Introduction 



Nuclear thermal propulsion (NTP) is under development by NASA space nuclear propulsion (SNP) project as 



propulsion for a crewed mars mission. For this mission, NTP utilizes hydrogen as propellant, stored as liquid hydrogen 



(LH2). NTP achieves greater architectural robustness compared to traditional chemical systems by utilizing a nuclear 



fission reactor to heat hydrogen to high temperatures for high specific impulse (Isp) rocket thrust. Due to the low 



atomic mass of hydrogen, NTP produces twice the Isp compared to chemical propulsion, reducing propellant mass and 



enabling faster transit times. Additionally, NTP offers programmatic flexibility, such as wider launch windows and 



mission abort options. These capabilities are vital to facilitate manned missions to Mars. 



To efficiently store and transport the hydrogen propellant during the Mars mission, it must be cryogenically cooled 



at 20 K to prevent boil-off. Otherwise, the tanks require venting from over-pressurization, which results in significant 



propellant losses. Zero boil-off (ZBO) is achieved through passive and active CFM. The technology to achieve ZBO 



for hydrogen for long duration missions has yet to be demonstrated, but advanced cryogenic storage concepts have 



been and are in development that combine cryocoolers, broad area cooling (BAC) networks, high-performance multi-



layer insulation (MLI) and thermal isolation design [1] [2]. 



This paper outlines a ZBO CFM system for a Mars mission and describes the analysis techniques and technology 



assumptions made to estimate the heat lift required of cryocoolers and the sensitivity of the system to variations in 



technology performance assumptions. The technologies included are based on those tested by NASA projects [3] [4] 



[5] [6]. 



In addition to steady-state thermal performance analysis, this work treats several transient events at a high level. 



These phenomena could result in the need for venting during transient events or the incorporation of additional CFM 



technologies like mixing. The post-launch transient presents the greatest risk of a need for propellant venting, as the 



heat loads are orders of magnitude larger before the spacecraft reaches steady-state temperature compared to after. 



This highlights the importance of a quick orbit insertion, separation, and power-up of the active CFM systems. 



Ultimately, the analysis and research presented demonstrate the importance of continued investment in CFM 



technology development and focused analysis of CFM for the NTP application to mitigate risks to the feasibility of 



an NTP vehicle.

II. Description of CFM System 



 



CFM manages the storage and transfer of cryogenic propellants for extended duration missions. In order to reduce 



vehicle mass and mission transit durations, CFM aims to eliminate propellant boiloff and leakage. CFM technologies 



are outlined in Figure 1. 



Figure 1. Cryogenic Fluid Management Technologies 



A. Description of CFM Technologies 



Spray-on foam insulation (SOFI) is applied to the liquid hydrogen tanks for thermal isolation in atmospheric 



environments and multi-layer insulation (MLI) surrounds the SOFI for thermal isolation in high-vacuum 



environments. Two zones of broad area cooling (BAC) is integrated around the propellant tank through distributed 



cooling networks of tubes. The first BAC zone is coupled to a shield in the tank insulation, and the second zone is 



coupled to the tank wall itself. Copper straps intercept heat from the support struts to the BAC shield to be lifted by 



the 90K-class cryocooler. Thermal isolation of the tank from the BAC shield is critical to maximizing the heat lift at 



the BAC shield, which in turn maximizes the overall efficiency of the system. Load-bearing MLI and SOFI insulates 



the tank from the shield. Reverse turbo-Brayton cycle cryocoolers expand and cool the helium working fluid to 



circulate heat away from the tank. The tube-on-shield BAC integrates with a high capacity 90K-class cryocooler to 



reduce the heat flux substantially and efficiently into the liquid hydrogen tank. The tube-on-shield BAC is a uniformly 



distributed tubing network attached to an aluminum shield embedded in the MLI. The tube-on-tank BAC integrates 



with a 20K-class cryocooler to directly control tank pressure and prevent boil-off. Each zone has two additional fully

B. CFM Operations 



Over the course of the mission, heat from the 



environment and spacecraft components result in 



unacceptable heat leaks into the liquid hydrogen 



propellent tanks. MLI reflects away the bulk of the 



environmental radiation, but inefficiencies and 



structural interfaces introduce significant heat leak 



into the tank. In order to limit liquid hydrogen boil-



off and control the tank ullage pressure, tube-on-



shield and tube-on-tank broad area cooling (BAC) is 



integrated with reverse turbo-Brayton cryocoolers as 



depicted in Figure 4. 



The tube-on-shield BAC significantly reduces the 



heat flux into the tank and is capable of maintaining 



the shield at 55K in the cis-lunar environment, which 



is the hottest environment in the reference mission. 



The tube-on-tank 20K-class cryocooler directly 



controls ullage pressure and maintains the ZBO even 



under the transients investigated. 



This optimized dual-zone configuration results in 



almost half the mass and power requirements 



compared to a single-zone, tube-on-tank 



configuration with multiple 20 K, 20 W class 



cryocoolers [7]. This is because the 20 K cryocoolers 



are significantly less efficient due to the large temperature difference between the cold point and the heat rejection 



point. 



The 90K-class cryocooler is assumed to be able to run at a range of temperatures, down to 50K. Operating such that 



the shield is at 55K was found to be the optimal set point for steady state operations [8]. At lower temperatures the 



cryocooler does not have the same level of heat lift. During transients, the shield heats up, allowing the 90K-class 



cryocooler to operate at a higher heat lift capacity.

B. Vehicle Architecture 



The NTP crew vehicle consists of a habitat, three inline stages, and the core stage. The inline stages required to 



supply the liquid hydrogen propellant for transport to and from Mars are identical. Additionally, many of the 



subsystems and structures are common between the core and inline stages, including the RCS bus, solar arrays, 



radiators, and avionics. 



The liquid hydrogen tanks are constructed of an aluminum lithium alloy to reduce thermal conductivity. Tank 



sizing assumes a maximum ullage pressure of 40 psi. Composite struts secure the tanks and provide thermal isolation 



from the spacecraft bus. The avionics, batteries, cryocoolers and RCS tanks are also mounted to the aluminum skirt 



buses via struts for improved thermal isolation. Additionally, heat from the bus is collected via pumped cooling loops 



and rejected via graphite reinforced composite radiators with titanium-water heat pipes. 



 



IV. Analyses Overview 



To evaluate CFM system performance and feasibility, a 1-D thermal model was developed in Simulink. The model 



simulates the spacecraft’s overall internal and external thermal sources and heat transfer modes throughout all mission 



phases using the vehicle architecture as defined above. The key performance parameters of the individual CFM 



technologies to achieve ZBO were identified to substantiate a viable technology development and maturation plan. 



The 1-D model is parameterized in order to rapidly evaluate technology performance and iterate on the system design. 



In addition, Thermal Desktop was utilized to check environmental heating rates in Mars loiter orbit, confirming 



heat loads used in the 1D model were reasonable, and cross-checking radiation between internal components.

The cryocoolers are modeled using proportional only control, which is sufficient given the relatively long 



timescales for the hydrogen temperature to increase. The 90K-class cryocooler controls the BAC shield temperature 



to 55 K and the 20K-class cryocooler controls the tank temperature to 20 K. The model does not account for parasitic 



and integration losses in the refrigeration system from the cryocoolers, BAC tubing network, and helium working 



fluid. The cryocoolers are modeled as directly lifting heat at the loads. 



The liquid hydrogen propellant is modeled as a thermal mass of bulk temperature, which does not account for 



localized heating and stratification. Additionally, perfect heat transfer is assumed between the propellant tank wall 



and the liquid hydrogen. The details of heat transfer effects will influence the effectiveness of the CFM system, but 



this is beyond the scope of this paper. 



The 1-D model assumes all the avionics are powered on and the radiator rejection temperature is 300 K. Thermal 



Desktop was used to determine the thermal radiation contribution from component view factors given the vehicle 



geometry. This is described in the section on Internal Radiation Analysis below. 



A. Steady State 



Steady state analyses include the entire vehicle stack, assuming the vehicle is on-axis with the sun in cis-lunar 



orbits, LDHEO and NRHO. The nominal steady state case assumes the reactor vessel temperature is at 300 K and the 



habitat is occupied. The vehicle attitude is not controlled in the cooler Mars loiter orbit in order to minimize RCS 



propellant expenditure. 



The steady state cryocooler heat lift to maintain the temperature set points in LDHEO, NRHO, and Mars orbit is 



captured in Table 3. The maximum cryocooler heat lift represents the worst-case thermal environment for the 



respective orbits at solar cycle maximum and zero degrees inclination. For all stages, the largest heat rate into the 



tanks is from the bus. Copper straps intercept heat from the support struts to the BAC shield to be lifted by the 90K-



class cryocooler. Thermal isolation of the tank from the BAC shield is critical to maximizing the heat interception at 



the BAC shield, which in turn maximizes the overall efficiency of the system. Load-bearing MLI and SOFI insulates 



the tank from the shield. Additionally, a thermal resistance of 100 K/W is included at the tank struts and run pipe 



interfaces to resist heat flow into the tank. If this resistance is not inherent in the tank integration, thermal isolating 



joints can be used to limit the conductive heat transfer at the contact joint. The heat lift margin for the 90K and 20K-



class cryocoolers in the worst-case thermal environment is 100% and 300% respectively.

1. Launch & Ascent 



As discussed above, each NTP stage is assumed to reach steady state in the terrestrial environment (~300 K) at 



launch, whereas the liquid hydrogen propellant is initially at 20 K. The stage then experiences varying thermal 



environments over the course of the ascent timeline. The cryocoolers are powered-on one hour after launch following 



orbit insertion. This assumed duration until cryocooler on is expected to be conservative, but additional launch vehicle 



trajectory analysis is needed to confirm. 



The bulk liquid hydrogen temperature and the corresponding heat lift required of the two zones of refrigeration is 



plotted in Figure 7. 



The 90K-class cryocooler operates at full power for approximately 20 hours to intercept heat from the warm 



spacecraft. The 20K-class cryocooler operates at full power for approximately 10 days in order to cool the liquid 



hydrogen to 20 K. 



Although the peak transient temperature of the liquid hydrogen is negligible, if the duration until cryocooler power-



on is extended, the liquid hydrogen will act as a heat sink and absorb heat from the warm vehicle. This highlights the 



need for an aggressive orbit insertion, launch vehicle separation, and power-up of the NTP stages. The largest heat 



rate is from the insulted BAC shield through the load-bearing MLI. Furthermore, thermal stratification could further 



exasperate the risk of boil-off if natural convection doesn’t sufficiently distribute the heat in the hydrogen.

2. NTP Burns 



For the transient analysis of the NTP burns, the reactor vessel is modeled as a temperature source of 1000 K during 



burn operations, 575 K during cooldown, and 300 K steady state. Additionally, nuclear radiation heating is modeled 



as heat flow rates of approximately 47 kW to each nuclear radiation shadow shield, 5.3 kW to the BAC shield, and 30 



kW to the liquid hydrogen propellant [11]. NTP burns considered for various mission architectures include 15-30 



minute burns. For the sake of this analysis, a 30 minute worst case burn is utilized in the models. 



The cryocoolers are powered off for the duration of the burn in order to save power and maintain pressurization in 



the tank. The liquid hydrogen is modeled as a thermal mass equal to the remaining propellant in the core tank at 



completion of the burn. The thermodynamics resulting from engine ingestion, inertial acceleration, direct nuclear 



heating, and autogenous pressurization is a topic for future study. 



As captured in the peak liquid hydrogen temperature in the core tank during the burn transient is a function of the 



thermal environment, burn duration, and propellant fill level in the core. The burn transient is modeled as initially 



steady state with the vehicle attitude on-axis to the sun. Throughout the burn, the attitude is not maintained to be 



collinear with the sun vector and instead is collinear with the thrust vector. Longer burn durations and reduced thermal 



capacitance from lower fill levels results in the most significant transient temperatures of the bulk liquid hydrogen. 



However, the temperature difference for the nominal burn transients is negligible. 











10 



 



Additionally, the MOI and TEI burns and subsequent cooldowns are modeled sequentially in order to reflect 



compounding transients. A more detailed burn timeline is needed to refine the model.Of interest is the engine soakback 



following the burn plotted in. The soakback peaks about five and a half hours after the engine burn. This is followed

D. Leakage Analysis 



Liquid hydrogen is by nature a very small molecule. This results in traditional valves leaking stored liquid 



hydrogen. It is necessary for space-rated cryogenic low leak valves to be developed. NASA is already in the process 



of developing these low leak valves [12]. In addition, connecting each stage of the NTP spacecraft are low leak cryo-



couplers. The following assumptions are made for each stage of an NTP spacecraft: 



• Low-leak GH2 valve technology development is completed 



• A single 8” valve is closed until first engine firing, and then reclosed between engine burns 



• Three 3” valves are necessary per tank for vent, relief, and fill and drain 



• Cryo-coupler full leakage rate is radial 



• Valve full leakage rate is axial 



• Leak rate in SCIM is Gaseous Hydrogen 



Each valve has an assumed leak rate as described in Table 4. These leakage rates were developed in partnership 



with NASA subject matter experts.

The general layout of an NTP tank and associated valves can be 



seen in Figure 13. Standard Cubic Inches per Minute (SCIM) is a 



molar flow rate used for standard leak rates. This translates to moles 



of liquid hydrogen lost over time. Each additional stage results in 



additional cryo-couplers and valves, and therefore result in increased 



propellent lost. Additionally, leak rate becomes more of a potential 



risk given longer mission times. This may be especially true in 



mission architectures with several drop tanks, as the aggregation 



timeline stretches, so does the potential for total leakage. However, 



the assumption made that gaseous hydrogen is leaking and not liquid 



hydrogen is a key assumption. In all missions investigated, the overall 



leakage is minimal. However, if the assumption changes to liquid 



hydrogen leaking, then the overall mass loss results in mission 



failure. It is critical that further testing and development be perfromed 



to ensure valve performance meets expectations. Low leak 



technology testing and development is ongoing at NASA MSFC and 



should remain high priority, as high TRL state-of-the-art valves result 



in CONOPS failure as a result of leakage.

V. Comparison to RBO and Fully Passive CFM Systems 



There are multiple reasons why the expense of a full ZBO CFM system may be undesirable. For shorter missions, 



such as missions in cis-lunar space may be short enough that boil-off propellant mass is less than cryocooler mass. In 



addition, pursuing Reduced-Boil Off (RBO) or a fully passive system reduces system complexity. In many Mars 



mission architectures, several fully passive drop tanks are included in addition to ZBO main stage tanks. This section 



attempts to take a preliminary estimate of boil-off mass comparisons between different cases. 



A. System Descriptions 



RBO and fully passive systems in this analysis both include the full layup described in Figure 2. The only difference 



is the inclusion of the cryocoolers, and the associated BAC fluid loops. The passive system removes both classes of 



cryocoolers and BAC. Two cases of RBO will be investigated, one with just the tube-on-tank 20K-class cryocooler, 



and one with just the 90K-class cryocooler with tube-on-shield BAC. 



B. Steady State Results 



For the sake of this analysis, a simple single-stage vehicle will be modeled in each environment to demonstrate the 



difference in boil-off rates of a stage given various environments. This assumes that all tanks are the same size, 10 



meters in length and 8 meters in diameter. Table 6 shows the results of this preliminary analysis. The breakeven point 



is calculated using the mass of the cryocoolers and associated BAC versus the mass of liquid hydrogen that is boiled-



off and therefore unusable. Each case is compared to the masses of the cryocooler systems that are removed; the RBO 



case with the 20K cryocooler breakeven point is compared to the mass of just the 90K system that is removed, etc.

C. Transient Discussion 



 Large quantities of liquid hydrogen propellent are boiled-off during an NTP burn. This analysis assumes the 



nuclear heating will be acting directly on the core stage. The worst-case 30 minute NTP burn is utilized. Table 7 shows 



that the majority of the boil-off mass occurs during the burn itself, as opposed to during the soakback phase from the 



engine. This is a result of the highly effective passive thermal isolation, as described above. For this preliminary 



analysis it is assumed that all boiled-off propellent is unusable. However, it is likely necessary for a thermodynamic 



vent system (TVS) to be utilized in this case. A TVS expands liquid hydrogen by opening a valve to vacuum prior to 



venting it. Venting to vacuum causes a rapid expansion and vaporization, and therefore rapid cooling. This is done via 



the use of a Joule-Thomson device. Cooled liquid is then routed back into the tank, effectively removing heat from 



the hydrogen propellent. This allows for a high amount of cooling while venting off gaseous hydrogen. It is necessary 



to vent hydrogen to maintain tank pressurization at 20 K without the use of a cryocooler, so a vent system must be 



included. A TVS is incapable of throttling and maintaining specific heat lifts as the active cryocoolers can do. 



However, in the case of a burn where a large amount of hydrogen is vented all at once, the cooling provided by a TVS 



can have a large impact. By venting off hydrogen and cooling the tank, the system can effectively reduce the amount 



of lost propellent due to boil-off. Further work is needed to capture the effective cooling done by such a system, as 



well as to capture the additional mass a TVS would require. For RBO cases it is likely that a similar amount of boil-



off will be present from the NTP burn, but when combined with a TVS for pressure control, the total amount may be 



reduced. 



This preliminary analysis assumes that the core tank receives the bulk of the direct nuclear heating and shields the 



other tanks. However, drop tanks that are not in line with the core tank will also receive direct nuclear heating during 



an NTP burn. Further analysis is needed to identify the amount of heating on these drop tanks.

VI. Conclusion 



Based on the analysis presented, with the assumption that technology development of CFM technologies is 



successful, zero boiloff during steady-state operations appears feasible. Additionally, the simplified thermal models 



predict that the CFM system can endure the heat loads during driving transient operations cases (launch and engine 



firing) without requiring venting of the liquid hydrogen for pressure relief. This is dependent on the maturation of all 



critical technologies, including cryocoolers, BAC, thermal isolation joints, structural load-bearing MLI, and low leak 



valves/cryo-couplers. A fully passive, or RBO system may possibly close with the inclusion of a TVS and highly 



effective thermal isolation technologies, but when accounting for boil-off from transients it is likely that a ZBO system 



will be more mass effective for a typical Mars mission architecture. Specific trades into vehicle architecture options 



must be done to determine effectiveness for a given mission.
