PRC Phase 1 (Dewar/Distributed Mixing) Tests ODA: 



Introduction



An Optimized Data Analysis or ODA is a final data analysis package for a test or test series. The 



primary goal is to develop and present key data, observations, and findings in a form that is most 



useful to the LOX/Methane Assessment Physics-Based Modeling and Engineering Models Teams, 



as well as test teams and stakeholders. This ODA expands and builds upon the Quick Look Data 



Analysis (QLDA) completed by the LMA Data Analysis Subteam (DAS) for each PRC Phase 1 test. 



Please note that values reported in the ODA often differ from the preliminary values reported in the 



relevant QLDAs. This ODA represents the FINAL, most accurate data values and yield 



assessments for each included test event. 



This ODA provides analyses of the blast data recorded on the PRC Phase 1 (Dewar/Distributed 



Mixing) Tests. The PRC designation refers to “Propellant Reaction Characterization ”, a specialized 



evaluation conducted to understand the chemical, thermal, and explosive properties of rocket fuels 



and oxidizers. The PRC Phase 1 tests each featured a nominal 150 lbm (total) of LOX/LNG; the test 



articles consisted of an aluminum pan to contain the LNG and LOX-filled dewars. The dewars were 



broken by the command detonation of detonating cord run beneath the LNG pan. The tests were 



designed to quantify and understand the contribution to yield from a distributed mixing scenario.











5



The PRC Phase 1 Tests were designed to evaluate the effects of the following variables for the specific case of 



distributed mixing of LOX/LNG:



• Use of lower-purity LNG (97% methane)



• Fuel/oxidizer mass ratio



• Ignition delay (due to auto-ignition, ignition delay was not actually tested as a controlled variable)



Two pathfinder tests were conducted prior to the start of production testing. Baseline tests (designated as BLL-



baseline/long ignition delay) were also conducted as a means of evaluating the effects of the above-listed 



variables. Analyses focused on blast overpressure data within the TB 700 window (10-0.5 psi peak 



pressure) in order to provide assessments of explosive yield. Note that explosive outputs are reported as 



yield percentage with TNT=100% for a hemispherical surface burst condition. Yield and yield uncertainty were 



calculated only from the qualifying far-field signals according to the TB 700-2 window (peak overpressures 



between 10 and 0.5 psi and associated peak impulse values). For each test, the TNT % yield was determined for 



each qualifying signal; the mean yield and 95% confidence interval from the T-Student distribution is reported



Uncertainty only reflects statistical variation in the analyzed far-field signals

PRC Phase 1 (Dewar) ODA: Executive Summary



The following observations* are offered based on the Phase 1 (Dewar/Distributed Mixing) test results:



1. Weighted averages from PRC Phase 1 generally show that the range of yields** overlap across all 



test variables (MR, ignition delay, LNG purity), limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions about 



parameter-dependent yields from this test series in isolation. An exception occurred for MLM-02, 



which had a much shorter ignition delay and a lower yield than the other tests with MR = 4.3 (MLM-



03/04)



2. MR (LOX/LNG) varied only from 3.2 to 4.3 in the test series. Thus, little definitive correlation between 



MR and yield is possible.



3. Average assessed yields in the TB 700-2 window (peak overpressure 0.5 to 10 psi) ranged from 68-



89% for overpressure and 64-82% from impulse for the range of test configurations. Note that 



these yield values do not include uncertainty bounds.



4. Auto-ignition occurred on all tests. Ignition times after dewar breakage ranged from 0.4 to 106 ms.
* Observations apply only to specific scenarios evaluated in this test series. Additional testing/modeling is required to determine broader applicability.



** Note that the reported yield ranges include data analytics-related uncertainty bounds; they do not represent all potential sources of uncertainty, 



such as test parameter variabilities.

PRC Phase 1 (Dewar) ODA: Executive Summary (cont.)



The following observations are offered based on the Phase 1 (Dewar/Distributed Mixing) test results:



5. Overpressure-based yield trends generally increased with distance, indicating some degree of 



propellant deflagration.



6. With the exception of tests with MR = 4.3, yields tended to be generally insensitive to mixing time, 



although the yield uncertainty bounds may mask subtle trends.

PRC Phase 1 Background



• Commercial launch providers are actively developing launch vehicles (LV) that will utilize LOX 



and LCH4 (a.k.a. liquid natural gas/LNG) as the rocket propellants. 



• Insufficient testing has been conducted to fully characterize the explosive risk associated with 



the LOX/LNG propellant combination. 



• Preliminary testing suggests that these propellants may be highly energetic and extremely 



hazardous if not properly controlled. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct appropriate testing to 



define the explosive characteristics and safety criteria for safe handling and use. 



• Previous tests of LOX/Liquid Hydrogen (LH2) and LOX/RP (kerosene) have been completed 



over the past several decades with valuable lessons learned for helping create a test overview 



for LOX/LNG.



• PRC (Propellant Reaction Characterization) is one test campaign as part of the overall LMA 



program



• PRC consisted of Phase 1 (distributed mixing in a Dewar configuration) and Phase 2 



(well-mixed LOX/methane (MOX)). This document addresses only Phase 1 test results.











12



PRC Phase 1 Description



The PRC Phase 1 test campaign was conducted as part of the NASA-USSF sponsored LOX/Methane (LNG) 



Assessment, or LMA. PRC Phase 1 (Dewar) tests were intended to examine consequences of a static launch 



pad explosion at relatively small scale for the case of distributive mixing of LOX and LNG. The distributive mixing 



test (DMT) apparatus used in the PRC tests is believed to produce maximum mixing of the propellants while 



providing minimum confinement. 



• Test design maximizes propellant mixing



• Nominal propellant mass of 150 lb on each test event



• Conducted at the NASA White Sands Test Facility, Las Cruces, NM 



Note: PRC Phase 1 was a distinct and separate effort from PRC Phase 2, which examined well-mixed 



LOX/methane (MOX) explosive yields. Phase 1 and Phase 2 together aim to establish the explosive 



energy bounds between distributed-mixing and fully mixed conditions. Phase 1 results should not be 



extrapolated to MOX scenarios of PRC Phase 2. Additional background on Phase 2 and the 



corresponding results is provided in the forthcoming PRC Phase 2 ODA.

PRC Phase 1 Objectives



• Determine the potential yield of LOX/LNG in terms of TNT equivalence or Net Explosive Weight 



(NEW) sufficient to support hazard assessments for test and launch operations 



• Establish the reference blast environment (near and far field) for well-mixed LOX/LNG propellant 



explosions as a function of mass 



• Determine the explosive hazard of LOX/LNG as a function of mixing time including long mixing 



times to address the potential and hazard associated with the formation of a solution of MOX 



• Collect sufficient data for predictive methods for extrapolating the reference blast environments to 



larger quantities of LOX/LNG

Analysis Approach



Definitions:



QLDA – Quick-look Data Analysis. This is a preliminary analysis package for a test or test series that 



is conducted as quickly as possible upon availability of data. The analysis is conducted separately by 



several entities within DAS and then compiled into a single QLDA. The primary intent is to provide 



actionable feedback to the test team and stakeholders as quickly as possible in order to improve 



future test design and/or data collection. QLDAs are typically produced within one week or less of 



data availability.



ODA – Optimized Data Analysis. A final data analysis package for a test or test series. Primary goal 



is to develop and present key data, observations, and findings in a form that is most useful to the 



PBM and EMT, as well as test teams and stakeholders. The test masses and mass ratios were 



revised after the QLDAs were completed. This ODA reflects the final corrected values and 



associated corrected yields.



Note: A QLDA was developed for each PRC test. This ODA includes the entire PRC Phase 1 Test Series.

Analysis Approach: Overview



For all PRC tests, a QLDA with pressure- and impulse-based EEWs was developed using the JPL Explosion 



Data Interface (JEDI) tool and analyses conducted by Aerospace Corp. These analyses included yield 



assessments based on all reliable blast data recorded on the tests within the TB 700-2 window .



ERDC conducted manual, optimized data processing for selected pressure records within the TB 700-2 window 



for selected PRC tests to confirm data processing accuracy. These data were then used to determine EEWs. The 



ERDC analyses served as an additional confirmation of the JPL and Aero-reported EEWs, where applicable. 



The data processing and analysis methods used by Aero, JPL, and ERDC, while different to some degree, fall 



within the range of accepted blast data analysis. Because of this, the analyses represent the spectrum of 



expected yield values and uncertainty bounds* that might reasonably be expected from the provided blast 



datasets. The following slides detail the analysis methods used by each entity. 



*Uncertainty represents 95% confidence bounds from the T-Student distribution, which describes the confidence that the true test yield falls within 



the reported range based on the methodology used. It does not consider uncertainty due to sensor error, far-field criteria, parameter selection, 



test-to-test variability, test behavior, test setup, comparison to TNT, or TNT equivalency model.











29



Analysis Approach:
Uncertainty and Limitations of Yield Estimates



How Uncertainty is Quantified:



• Yield uncertainty is calculated only from the qualifying far-field signals according to the TB 700-2 window (peak 



overpressures between 10 and 0.5 psi and associated peak impulse values).



• For each test, the % TNT yield is determined for each qualifying signal; the mean yield and 95% confidence interval from 



the T-Student distribution is reported



• Uncertainty only reflects statistical variation in the analyzed far-field signals



• Uncertainty does not include yield model or physical uncertainty (including but not limited to uncertainty derived from 



applying the TNT yield model to LNG/LOX events, parameter selection, signal correction and filtering, fit applicability, 



sensor-level uncertainties, wave reflection, test-to-test variability, and test features such as ignition delay).



• Yields reported in the ODA are an inverse-variance weighted average of the yields from each source; uncertainty intervals 



are determined by the lower and upper bounds across each source (see Appendix A)



Key Limitations:



• Reported uncertainty should not be interpreted as the total uncertainty of LNG/LOX explosive yield



• Conclusions drawn from the data should account for unknown total uncertainty

Analysis Approach: JPL



JPL utilized the JPL Explosion Data Interface* (JEDI) v1.0 for all analyses. JEDI provides automatic baseline 



offset corrections to the raw pressure waveforms. The records are then analyzed using exponential fits and user-



directed processing methods, such as moving-average smoothing to account for noise transients. JEDI typically 



reports the following values:



• Hand-selected (with/without SL-correction)



• Local maxima (with/without SL-correction)



• Exponential fit (with/without SL-correction)



• Moving average (with/without SL-correction)



• JEDI also identified questionable data records not suitable for use in yield determinations.



For the PRC Phase 1 Test Series, JEDI determined % TNT yield from the Kingery-Bulmash DOE TIC-11268 



hemispherical model based on peak overpressures and positive phase impulses within the range 0.5-10 psi, 



where peak overpressure is hand-selected and both pressure and impulse are corrected for sea level conditions. 



All uncertainties reported represent 95% confidence bounds according to the T-Student distribution.



*Contact NASA JPL, Aerospace Nuclear Safety Engineering for information regarding this tool which was developed by Noelle Wells, Don Li, Shervin Taghavi and Elan Borenstein.

Analysis Approach: Aero



Analysis performed using Aerospace-developed tool that expedites data entry, reduction, analysis, 



and results formatting. Minor data cleaning automatically performed before analysis



– Data dropouts removed; pre-blast pressure tared as needed



– Identified questionable data records not suitable for use in yield determinations.



– Standardized to windless sea-level conditions



– Slant heights used for elevated gauge sensors



Three independent methods are used to assess pressure data and produce unique estimations of 



explosive equivalent weight (EEW) and yield



– Positive-phase impulse (PPI): integrates pressure over positive phase response



– Peak overpressure: pressure at ToA using best-fit curve



• Peak overpressures determined via exponential fit and Friedlander curve fit to positive pressure portion of 



waveform



– Time of arrival (ToA): yield from derived ignition time (not reported, for comparison only)



Far-field yield is used for overall explosive yield calculations



– Per TB 700-2, defined as region where overpressure is 0.5 – 10 psig











Analysis Approach: Aero Peak Pressure Determinations



• Curve fitting is used to mitigate the effect of nonidealities and sensor artifacts*



– Noisy data and pressure spikes are damped



– Overpressure error corrected by excluding slow-rise data and extrapolating back to time of arrival (ToA)



• Fit bounds are determined from standard guidelines and truncated where necessary



– Exponential fit ideally applied from ToA to 20% peak pressure



– Friedlander fit ideally applied from ToA into negative phase



– Bounds truncated when large slope deviations or pressure spikes are present



• Time bound selection and fit quality drive overpressure uncertainty



– Uncertainty ranges are not quantified in this package



* Curve fitting was used as early as Operation Snowball (1964) [3] to obtain peak overpressure and positive phase duration.

Analysis Approach: ERDC



ERDC conducted manual, optimized data processing of select pressure records within the TB 700-2 window 



for certain tests.



The ERDC methodology utilizes manual, user-directed modifications to the raw recorded data time histories to minimize 



noise and extraneous readings. Processes typically included the following (as-needed):



1. Truncation of records to begin at zero-time and conclude when the pressure record returned to a stable baseline after 



the end of the negative overpressure phase.



2. Baseline correction. Typically, this consisted of a constant value added or subtracted from the record to provide a 



mean zero pressure reading prior to shock arrival. In some cases, a variable baseline correction was applied to better 



account for baseline drift during the pressure event due to thermal-induced sensor response or other factors.



3. Filtering via either low-pass filter application or the use of smoothing techniques to minimize noise transients that 



impeded accurate selection of pressure peaks.



ERDC also identified questionable data records not suitable for use in yield determinations and produced csv files of the 



final, processed records.

PRC Phase 1 Observations*



• Weighted averages from PRC Phase 1 generally show that the range of yields** overlap across all test 



variables (MR, ignition delay, LNG purity), limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions about parameter-



dependent yields from this test series in isolation. An exception occurred for MLM-02, which had a much 



shorter ignition delay and a lower yield than the other tests with MR = 4.3 (MLM-03/04)



• MR (LOX/LNG) varied only from 3.2 to 4.3 in the test series. Thus, little definitive correlation between MR 



and yield is possible.



• Average assessed yields in the TB 700-2 window (peak overpressure 0.5 to 10 psi) ranged from 68-89% for 



overpressure and 64-82% from impulse for the range of test configurations. Note that these yield values do 



not include uncertainty bounds.



• Auto-ignition occurred on all tests. Ignition times after dewar breakage ranged from 0.4 to 106 ms.



* Observations apply only to the specific scenarios evaluated in this test series. Additional testing/modeling is required to determine 



broader applicability.



** Note that the reported yield ranges include data analytics-related uncertainty bounds; they do not represent all potential sources of 



uncertainty, such as test parameter variabilities.

• Current state of uncertainty reporting:



– Individual analysts provide yield uncertainty purely from variance in far-field signal yields



• Tied to the data, but does not include all sources of uncertainty in the data



– Everything from the event itself to capturing of the data (e.g. sensor uncertainty) to interpretation (e.g. parameter, yield model)



– Matters most in the very far-field (yield is highly sensitive to small variations for peak overpressure < 1 psi)



» Why low-yield events are best reported as “low yield”



• Best to provide clarity on what this uncertainty is and is not wherever it is reported



• Statistical methods for determining uncertainty (e.g. T-Student distribution) only work for 



independent samples (e.g. x uncorrelated samples in the 0.5-10 psi range)



• When we begin merging yields and uncertainties from individual entities for a single test, we are 



dealing with correlated samples
– E.g. x samples analyzed one way merged with x samples analyzed another way are the same sample set



– This is no longer a statistical problem because it is not statistical variance, rather method differentiation



– Risks of treating this as a statistical problem: artificially increase the sample size; convoluted results; misrepresent the source of 



uncertainty; sideline the benefit of having several analysts in the first place
