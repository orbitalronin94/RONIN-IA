<p>PRC Phase 1 (Dewar/Distributed Mixing) Tests ODA: 
</p>
<p>Introduction
</p>
<p>An Optimized Data Analysis or ODA is a final data analysis package for a test or test series. The 
</p>
<p>primary goal is to develop and present key data, observations, and findings in a form that is most 
</p>
<p>useful to the LOX/Methane Assessment Physics-Based Modeling and Engineering Models Teams, 
</p>
<p>as well as test teams and stakeholders. This ODA expands and builds upon the Quick Look Data 
</p>
<p>Analysis (QLDA) completed by the LMA Data Analysis Subteam (DAS) for each PRC Phase 1 test. 
</p>
<p>Please note that values reported in the ODA often differ from the preliminary values reported in the 
</p>
<p>relevant QLDAs. This ODA represents the FINAL, most accurate data values and yield 
</p>
<p>assessments for each included test event. 
</p>
<p>This ODA provides analyses of the blast data recorded on the PRC Phase 1 (Dewar/Distributed 
</p>
<p>Mixing) Tests. The PRC designation refers to “Propellant Reaction Characterization ”, a specialized 
</p>
<p>evaluation conducted to understand the chemical, thermal, and explosive properties of rocket fuels 
</p>
<p>and oxidizers. The PRC Phase 1 tests each featured a nominal 150 lbm (total) of LOX/LNG; the test 
</p>
<p>articles consisted of an aluminum pan to contain the LNG and LOX-filled dewars. The dewars were 
</p>
<p>broken by the command detonation of detonating cord run beneath the LNG pan. The tests were 
</p>
<p>designed to quantify and understand the contribution to yield from a distributed mixing scenario.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>5
</p>
<p>The PRC Phase 1 Tests were designed to evaluate the effects of the following variables for the specific case of 
</p>
<p>distributed mixing of LOX/LNG:
</p>
<p>• Use of lower-purity LNG (97% methane)
</p>
<p>• Fuel/oxidizer mass ratio
</p>
<p>• Ignition delay (due to auto-ignition, ignition delay was not actually tested as a controlled variable)
</p>
<p>Two pathfinder tests were conducted prior to the start of production testing. Baseline tests (designated as BLL-
</p>
<p>baseline/long ignition delay) were also conducted as a means of evaluating the effects of the above-listed 
</p>
<p>variables. Analyses focused on blast overpressure data within the TB 700 window (10-0.5 psi peak 
</p>
<p>pressure) in order to provide assessments of explosive yield. Note that explosive outputs are reported as 
</p>
<p>yield percentage with TNT=100% for a hemispherical surface burst condition. Yield and yield uncertainty were 
</p>
<p>calculated only from the qualifying far-field signals according to the TB 700-2 window (peak overpressures 
</p>
<p>between 10 and 0.5 psi and associated peak impulse values). For each test, the TNT % yield was determined for 
</p>
<p>each qualifying signal; the mean yield and 95% confidence interval from the T-Student distribution is reported
</p>
<p>Uncertainty only reflects statistical variation in the analyzed far-field signals
</p>

<p>PRC Phase 1 (Dewar) ODA: Executive Summary
</p>
<p>The following observations* are offered based on the Phase 1 (Dewar/Distributed Mixing) test results:
</p>
<p>1. Weighted averages from PRC Phase 1 generally show that the range of yields** overlap across all 
</p>
<p>test variables (MR, ignition delay, LNG purity), limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions about 
</p>
<p>parameter-dependent yields from this test series in isolation. An exception occurred for MLM-02, 
</p>
<p>which had a much shorter ignition delay and a lower yield than the other tests with MR = 4.3 (MLM-
</p>
<p>03/04)
</p>
<p>2. MR (LOX/LNG) varied only from 3.2 to 4.3 in the test series. Thus, little definitive correlation between 
</p>
<p>MR and yield is possible.
</p>
<p>3. Average assessed yields in the TB 700-2 window (peak overpressure 0.5 to 10 psi) ranged from 68-
</p>
<p>89% for overpressure and 64-82% from impulse for the range of test configurations. Note that 
</p>
<p>these yield values do not include uncertainty bounds.
</p>
<p>4. Auto-ignition occurred on all tests. Ignition times after dewar breakage ranged from 0.4 to 106 ms.
* Observations apply only to specific scenarios evaluated in this test series. Additional testing/modeling is required to determine broader applicability.
</p>
<p>** Note that the reported yield ranges include data analytics-related uncertainty bounds; they do not represent all potential sources of uncertainty, 
</p>
<p>such as test parameter variabilities.</p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>PRC Phase 1 (Dewar) ODA: Executive Summary (cont.)
</p>
<p>The following observations are offered based on the Phase 1 (Dewar/Distributed Mixing) test results:
</p>
<p>5. Overpressure-based yield trends generally increased with distance, indicating some degree of 
</p>
<p>propellant deflagration.
</p>
<p>6. With the exception of tests with MR = 4.3, yields tended to be generally insensitive to mixing time, 
</p>
<p>although the yield uncertainty bounds may mask subtle trends.</p>

<p>PRC Phase 1 Background
</p>
<p>• Commercial launch providers are actively developing launch vehicles (LV) that will utilize LOX 
</p>
<p>and LCH4 (a.k.a. liquid natural gas/LNG) as the rocket propellants. 
</p>
<p>• Insufficient testing has been conducted to fully characterize the explosive risk associated with 
</p>
<p>the LOX/LNG propellant combination. 
</p>
<p>• Preliminary testing suggests that these propellants may be highly energetic and extremely 
</p>
<p>hazardous if not properly controlled. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct appropriate testing to 
</p>
<p>define the explosive characteristics and safety criteria for safe handling and use. 
</p>
<p>• Previous tests of LOX/Liquid Hydrogen (LH2) and LOX/RP (kerosene) have been completed 
</p>
<p>over the past several decades with valuable lessons learned for helping create a test overview 
</p>
<p>for LOX/LNG.
</p>
<p>• PRC (Propellant Reaction Characterization) is one test campaign as part of the overall LMA 
</p>
<p>program
</p>
<p>• PRC consisted of Phase 1 (distributed mixing in a Dewar configuration) and Phase 2 
</p>
<p>(well-mixed LOX/methane (MOX)). This document addresses only Phase 1 test results.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>12
</p>
<p>PRC Phase 1 Description
</p>
<p>The PRC Phase 1 test campaign was conducted as part of the NASA-USSF sponsored LOX/Methane (LNG) 
</p>
<p>Assessment, or LMA. PRC Phase 1 (Dewar) tests were intended to examine consequences of a static launch 
</p>
<p>pad explosion at relatively small scale for the case of distributive mixing of LOX and LNG. The distributive mixing 
</p>
<p>test (DMT) apparatus used in the PRC tests is believed to produce maximum mixing of the propellants while 
</p>
<p>providing minimum confinement. 
</p>
<p>• Test design maximizes propellant mixing
</p>
<p>• Nominal propellant mass of 150 lb on each test event
</p>
<p>• Conducted at the NASA White Sands Test Facility, Las Cruces, NM 
</p>
<p>Note: PRC Phase 1 was a distinct and separate effort from PRC Phase 2, which examined well-mixed 
</p>
<p>LOX/methane (MOX) explosive yields. Phase 1 and Phase 2 together aim to establish the explosive 
</p>
<p>energy bounds between distributed-mixing and fully mixed conditions. Phase 1 results should not be 
</p>
<p>extrapolated to MOX scenarios of PRC Phase 2. Additional background on Phase 2 and the 
</p>
<p>corresponding results is provided in the forthcoming PRC Phase 2 ODA.
</p>
<p>Slide compiled from LMA Liquid Oxygen/Liquid Methane Propellant Reaction Characterization Test Plan</p>
<p></p>

<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>13
</p>
<p>PRC Phase 1 Objectives
</p>
<p>• Determine the potential yield of LOX/LNG in terms of TNT equivalence or Net Explosive Weight 
</p>
<p>(NEW) sufficient to support hazard assessments for test and launch operations 
</p>
<p>• Establish the reference blast environment (near and far field) for well-mixed LOX/LNG propellant 
</p>
<p>explosions as a function of mass 
</p>
<p>• Determine the explosive hazard of LOX/LNG as a function of mixing time including long mixing 
</p>
<p>times to address the potential and hazard associated with the formation of a solution of MOX 
</p>
<p>• Collect sufficient data for predictive methods for extrapolating the reference blast environments to 
</p>
<p>larger quantities of LOX/LNG </p>
<p></p>

<p>Analysis Approach
</p>
<p>Definitions:
</p>
<p>QLDA – Quick-look Data Analysis. This is a preliminary analysis package for a test or test series that 
</p>
<p>is conducted as quickly as possible upon availability of data. The analysis is conducted separately by 
</p>
<p>several entities within DAS and then compiled into a single QLDA. The primary intent is to provide 
</p>
<p>actionable feedback to the test team and stakeholders as quickly as possible in order to improve 
</p>
<p>future test design and/or data collection. QLDAs are typically produced within one week or less of 
</p>
<p>data availability.
</p>
<p>ODA – Optimized Data Analysis. A final data analysis package for a test or test series. Primary goal 
</p>
<p>is to develop and present key data, observations, and findings in a form that is most useful to the 
</p>
<p>PBM and EMT, as well as test teams and stakeholders. The test masses and mass ratios were 
</p>
<p>revised after the QLDAs were completed. This ODA reflects the final corrected values and 
</p>
<p>associated corrected yields.
</p>
<p>Note: A QLDA was developed for each PRC test. This ODA includes the entire PRC Phase 1 Test Series.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>28
</p>
<p>Analysis Approach: Overview
</p>
<p>For all PRC tests, a QLDA with pressure- and impulse-based EEWs was developed using the JPL Explosion 
</p>
<p>Data Interface (JEDI) tool and analyses conducted by Aerospace Corp. These analyses included yield 
</p>
<p>assessments based on all reliable blast data recorded on the tests within the TB 700-2 window .
</p>
<p>ERDC conducted manual, optimized data processing for selected pressure records within the TB 700-2 window 
</p>
<p>for selected PRC tests to confirm data processing accuracy. These data were then used to determine EEWs. The 
</p>
<p>ERDC analyses served as an additional confirmation of the JPL and Aero-reported EEWs, where applicable. 
</p>
<p>The data processing and analysis methods used by Aero, JPL, and ERDC, while different to some degree, fall 
</p>
<p>within the range of accepted blast data analysis. Because of this, the analyses represent the spectrum of 
</p>
<p>expected yield values and uncertainty bounds* that might reasonably be expected from the provided blast 
</p>
<p>datasets. The following slides detail the analysis methods used by each entity. 
</p>
<p>*Uncertainty represents 95% confidence bounds from the T-Student distribution, which describes the confidence that the true test yield falls within 
</p>
<p>the reported range based on the methodology used. It does not consider uncertainty due to sensor error, far-field criteria, parameter selection, 
</p>
<p>test-to-test variability, test behavior, test setup, comparison to TNT, or TNT equivalency model.</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>29
</p>
<p>Analysis Approach:
Uncertainty and Limitations of Yield Estimates
</p>
<p>How Uncertainty is Quantified:
</p>
<p>• Yield uncertainty is calculated only from the qualifying far-field signals according to the TB 700-2 window (peak 
</p>
<p>overpressures between 10 and 0.5 psi and associated peak impulse values).
</p>
<p>• For each test, the % TNT yield is determined for each qualifying signal; the mean yield and 95% confidence interval from 
</p>
<p>the T-Student distribution is reported
</p>
<p>• Uncertainty only reflects statistical variation in the analyzed far-field signals
</p>
<p>• Uncertainty does not include yield model or physical uncertainty (including but not limited to uncertainty derived from 
</p>
<p>applying the TNT yield model to LNG/LOX events, parameter selection, signal correction and filtering, fit applicability, 
</p>
<p>sensor-level uncertainties, wave reflection, test-to-test variability, and test features such as ignition delay).
</p>
<p>• Yields reported in the ODA are an inverse-variance weighted average of the yields from each source; uncertainty intervals 
</p>
<p>are determined by the lower and upper bounds across each source (see Appendix A)
</p>
<p>Key Limitations:
</p>
<p>• Reported uncertainty should not be interpreted as the total uncertainty of LNG/LOX explosive yield
</p>
<p>• Conclusions drawn from the data should account for unknown total uncertainty</p>
<p></p>
</div>

<p>30
</p>
<p>Analysis Approach: JPL
</p>
<p>JPL utilized the JPL Explosion Data Interface* (JEDI) v1.0 for all analyses. JEDI provides automatic baseline 
</p>
<p>offset corrections to the raw pressure waveforms. The records are then analyzed using exponential fits and user-
</p>
<p>directed processing methods, such as moving-average smoothing to account for noise transients. JEDI typically 
</p>
<p>reports the following values:
</p>
<p>• Hand-selected (with/without SL-correction)
</p>
<p>• Local maxima (with/without SL-correction)
</p>
<p>• Exponential fit (with/without SL-correction)
</p>
<p>• Moving average (with/without SL-correction)
</p>
<p>• JEDI also identified questionable data records not suitable for use in yield determinations.
</p>
<p>For the PRC Phase 1 Test Series, JEDI determined % TNT yield from the Kingery-Bulmash DOE TIC-11268 
</p>
<p>hemispherical model based on peak overpressures and positive phase impulses within the range 0.5-10 psi, 
</p>
<p>where peak overpressure is hand-selected and both pressure and impulse are corrected for sea level conditions. 
</p>
<p>All uncertainties reported represent 95% confidence bounds according to the T-Student distribution.
</p>
<p>*Contact NASA JPL, Aerospace Nuclear Safety Engineering for information regarding this tool which was developed by Noelle Wells, Don Li, Shervin Taghavi and Elan Borenstein.</p>

<p>Analysis Approach: Aero
</p>
<p>Analysis performed using Aerospace-developed tool that expedites data entry, reduction, analysis, 
</p>
<p>and results formatting. Minor data cleaning automatically performed before analysis
</p>
<p>– Data dropouts removed; pre-blast pressure tared as needed
</p>
<p>– Identified questionable data records not suitable for use in yield determinations.
</p>
<p>– Standardized to windless sea-level conditions
</p>
<p>– Slant heights used for elevated gauge sensors
</p>
<p>Three independent methods are used to assess pressure data and produce unique estimations of 
</p>
<p>explosive equivalent weight (EEW) and yield
</p>
<p>– Positive-phase impulse (PPI): integrates pressure over positive phase response
</p>
<p>– Peak overpressure: pressure at ToA using best-fit curve
</p>
<p>• Peak overpressures determined via exponential fit and Friedlander curve fit to positive pressure portion of 
</p>
<p>waveform
</p>
<p>– Time of arrival (ToA): yield from derived ignition time (not reported, for comparison only)
</p>
<p>Far-field yield is used for overall explosive yield calculations
</p>
<p>– Per TB 700-2, defined as region where overpressure is 0.5 – 10 psig</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>Analysis Approach: Aero Peak Pressure Determinations
</p>
<p>• Curve fitting is used to mitigate the effect of nonidealities and sensor artifacts*
</p>
<p>– Noisy data and pressure spikes are damped
</p>
<p>– Overpressure error corrected by excluding slow-rise data and extrapolating back to time of arrival (ToA)
</p>
<p>• Fit bounds are determined from standard guidelines and truncated where necessary
</p>
<p>– Exponential fit ideally applied from ToA to 20% peak pressure
</p>
<p>– Friedlander fit ideally applied from ToA into negative phase
</p>
<p>– Bounds truncated when large slope deviations or pressure spikes are present
</p>
<p>• Time bound selection and fit quality drive overpressure uncertainty
</p>
<p>– Uncertainty ranges are not quantified in this package
</p>
<p>* Curve fitting was used as early as Operation Snowball (1964) [3] to obtain peak overpressure and positive phase duration.
</p>

<p>Analysis Approach: ERDC
</p>
<p>ERDC conducted manual, optimized data processing of select pressure records within the TB 700-2 window 
</p>
<p>for certain tests.
</p>
<p>The ERDC methodology utilizes manual, user-directed modifications to the raw recorded data time histories to minimize 
</p>
<p>noise and extraneous readings. Processes typically included the following (as-needed):
</p>
<p>1. Truncation of records to begin at zero-time and conclude when the pressure record returned to a stable baseline after 
</p>
<p>the end of the negative overpressure phase.
</p>
<p>2. Baseline correction. Typically, this consisted of a constant value added or subtracted from the record to provide a 
</p>
<p>mean zero pressure reading prior to shock arrival. In some cases, a variable baseline correction was applied to better 
</p>
<p>account for baseline drift during the pressure event due to thermal-induced sensor response or other factors.
</p>
<p>3. Filtering via either low-pass filter application or the use of smoothing techniques to minimize noise transients that 
</p>
<p>impeded accurate selection of pressure peaks.
</p>
<p>ERDC also identified questionable data records not suitable for use in yield determinations and produced csv files of the 
</p>
<p>final, processed records.</p>

<p>PRC Phase 1 Observations*
</p>
<p>• Weighted averages from PRC Phase 1 generally show that the range of yields** overlap across all test 
</p>
<p>variables (MR, ignition delay, LNG purity), limiting the ability to draw definitive conclusions about parameter-
</p>
<p>dependent yields from this test series in isolation. An exception occurred for MLM-02, which had a much 
</p>
<p>shorter ignition delay and a lower yield than the other tests with MR = 4.3 (MLM-03/04)
</p>
<p>• MR (LOX/LNG) varied only from 3.2 to 4.3 in the test series. Thus, little definitive correlation between MR 
</p>
<p>and yield is possible.
</p>
<p>• Average assessed yields in the TB 700-2 window (peak overpressure 0.5 to 10 psi) ranged from 68-89% for 
</p>
<p>overpressure and 64-82% from impulse for the range of test configurations. Note that these yield values do 
</p>
<p>not include uncertainty bounds.
</p>
<p>• Auto-ignition occurred on all tests. Ignition times after dewar breakage ranged from 0.4 to 106 ms.
</p>
<p>* Observations apply only to the specific scenarios evaluated in this test series. Additional testing/modeling is required to determine 
</p>
<p>broader applicability.
</p>
<p>** Note that the reported yield ranges include data analytics-related uncertainty bounds; they do not represent all potential sources of 
</p>
<p>uncertainty, such as test parameter variabilities.</p>

<p>• JPL, Aero, and ERDC conduct yield analyses for the LOX/Methane Assessment (LMA) Program
</p>
<p>• All three entities have recognized expertise in blast data analysis and utilize accepted 
</p>
<p>methodologies to analyze the blast data from LMA tests to produce assessed yields.

</p>
<p>according to the T-Student distribution method.

<p>• Current state of uncertainty reporting:
</p>
<p>– Individual analysts provide yield uncertainty purely from variance in far-field signal yields
</p>
<p>• Tied to the data, but does not include all sources of uncertainty in the data
</p>
<p>– Everything from the event itself to capturing of the data (e.g. sensor uncertainty) to interpretation (e.g. parameter, yield model)
</p>
<p>– Matters most in the very far-field (yield is highly sensitive to small variations for peak overpressure &lt; 1 psi)
</p>
<p>» Why low-yield events are best reported as “low yield”
</p>
<p>• Best to provide clarity on what this uncertainty is and is not wherever it is reported
</p>
<p>• Statistical methods for determining uncertainty (e.g. T-Student distribution) only work for 
</p>
<p>independent samples (e.g. x uncorrelated samples in the 0.5-10 psi range)
</p>
<p>• When we begin merging yields and uncertainties from individual entities for a single test, we are 
</p>
<p>dealing with correlated samples
– E.g. x samples analyzed one way merged with x samples analyzed another way are the same sample set
</p>
<p>– This is no longer a statistical problem because it is not statistical variance, rather method differentiation
</p>
<p>– Risks of treating this as a statistical problem: artificially increase the sample size; convoluted results; misrepresent the source of 
</p>
<p>uncertainty; sideline the benefit of having several analysts in the first place
</p>
