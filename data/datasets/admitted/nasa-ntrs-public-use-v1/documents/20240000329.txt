<p>1.0 Introduction 
</p>
<p>Aluminum Alloy 2219 (AA2219) is an age-hardenable Al-Cu alloy that has been widely used 
</p>
<p>throughout the aerospace industry for applications such as cryogenic tanks of launch vehicles 
</p>
<p>because of its light weight, high strength, and fracture toughness at cryogenic temperatures.1 
</p>
<p>AA2219 is available in all wrought products, and it can be supplied in the form of bars, plates, 
</p>
<p>forgings, and extrusions in the full range of commercial sizes. Additionally, AA2219 is readily 
</p>
<p>weldable and suitable for applications over a temperature range of –450 °F to 600 °F (–268 °C to 
</p>
<p>315 °C). In the solution heat-treated and aged condition, its mechanical properties in the 500 to 
600 °F (260–315 °C) temperature range are superior to those of any other commercially 
</p>
<p>available aluminum alloy. 
</p>
<p>AA2219 continues to see frequent use in space and advanced aviation applications due to the 
</p>
<p>practicality and availability of the material. As the lead-time required for forged products has 
</p>
<p>increased, the use of thick plate products has become increasingly common for the manufacture of 
</p>
<p>arbitrary structural shapes. However, the mechanical properties of AA2219 in thick product forms 
</p>
<p>(thick plate greater than 3 inches) are not well documented. Specifically, little information is 
</p>
<p>known about the mechanical properties of AA2219 thick plate in the short-transverse (ST) 
</p>
<p>orientation. The ST properties are not controlled by the industry material procurement standards 
</p>
<p>nor are they available in commonly used material property databases such as the Metallic Materials 
</p>
<p>Properties Database and Standardization (MMPDS).15  
</p>
<p>The design review process within a recent project identified the use of AA2219-T87 5-in thick 
</p>
<p>plate for a variety of structural hardware. Through prior personal experience, the reviewer flagged 
</p>
<p>a concern about the low ductility in the ST orientation of this material. Based upon these concerns, 
</p>
<p>multiple material lots of thick plate AA2219-T87 from the same manufacturer were evaluated for 
</p>
<p>their tensile performance. These tests identified irregularities in the ST ductility—with total 
</p>
<p>fracture elongation varying from 1% to 7.35%. Thus, it is evident that a better understanding of 
</p>
<p>the microstructure and mechanical properties for the thick plate material in the ST orientation is 
</p>
<p>key to ensuring its safe use in critical applications. These early tensile test results prompted 
</p>
<p>additional testing and data analysis to understand the root cause of the low ductility. Materials 
</p>
<p>characterization techniques were utilized to observe the micro-structure of fractured tensile 
</p>
<p>specimens and remnant plate material. The mechanism and root cause of low ST ductility in thick 
</p>
<p>plate material was presented and discussed in a NASA TM.2 The drastic reduction in ST tensile 
</p>
<p>ductility can be attributed to the presence of abundant, coarse Cu-rich θ particles (Al2Cu) at grain 
</p>
<p>boundaries. Hard θ particles are stress risers during tensile testing, and the increase in stress 
</p>
<p>concentration around the hard θ particles promotes crack initiation and leads to significant 
</p>
<p>reduction in fracture elongation. 
</p>
<p>During the latter stages of the investigation into the low ST tensile ductility, the NASA 
</p>
<p>Engineering and Safety Center (NESC) team discovered an “edge effect,” where improved 
</p>
<p>ductility was observed in tensile specimens obtained from material close to the plate’s edges in the 
</p>
<p>width direction. Therefore, an in-depth metallurgical investigation was carried out to determine 
</p>
<p>the root causes of the edge effect. The main objective of this investigation is to obtain a better 
</p>
<p>understanding of the microstructural characteristics of 5-in thick AA2219-T87 plate material and 
</p>
<p>the mechanism that leads to the unexpected edge effect. The mechanism and root cause of the edge 
</p>
<p>effect in the thick AA2219 plate material is presented and discussed. </p>
<p></p>

<p>2.0 Edge Effect on Tensile Properties Across the Plate Width and Thickness 
</p>
<p>The NESC team discovered a “through-width” edge effect in 5-in thick AA2219-T87 plates, where 
</p>
<p>improved ductility and strength were observed in tensile specimens obtained close to the plate width 
</p>
<p>edge. In contrast, ductility and ultimate tensile stress decrease considerably in tensile specimens 
</p>
<p>obtained away from the plate width edge. The short transverse (ST) specimen orientation used for 
</p>
<p>tensile testing to determine the edge effect is shown in Figure 1. 
</p>
<p> 
Figure 1. The orientation of tensile specimens used to determine edge effect for 5-in thick 
</p>
<p>AA2219 plate. 
</p>
<p>Figure 2 and Figure 3 are plots of the same data set as a function of plate width location and plate 
</p>
<p>length location, respectively. Common data points are color matched across the figures. The data in 
</p>
<p>these figures was collected from samples in the ST direction. Figure 2 shows the variations in ultimate 
</p>
<p>tensile stress and inelastic strain as a function of plate width location of a 5-in AA2219 plate (from lot 
</p>
<p>930071). As shown in Figure 2, the ultimate tensile stress and inelastic strain values are highest at the 
</p>
<p>plate width edge, with values decreasing toward the width center of the plate. As shown in Figure 3, 
</p>
<p>both ultimate tensile stress and inelastic strain values vary significantly at different locations (through-
</p>
<p>width effect), but there is no clear sign of edge effect with respect to location across the plate length. 
</p>
<p>The variations in strength at a given length location are due to width edge location effects. One 
</p>
<p>explanation for this is the peripheral ends of the plate (length edge), which were used to grip and stretch 
</p>
<p>the plate after solution treatment, were trimmed off after completion of plate stretching. 
</p>

<p>The strength of AA2219 is related to the size and volume fraction of the primary strengthening 
</p>
<p>precipitate θ' (semi-coherent Al2Cu intermetallic compound). It was also discovered that the hard 
</p>
<p>and brittle θ (incoherent Al2Cu intermetallic compound) particles tend to agglomerate along grain 
</p>
<p>boundaries and promote brittle intergranular fracture in thick AA2219 plates.2 It is postulated that 
</p>
<p>the edge effect phenomenon is likely related to the combined effects of (1) the plate temperature 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>plate during quench. In-depth metallurgical analysis was performed to determine the root causes 
</p>
<p>of edge effect.

<p>4.3.1 Effect of θ Particle Size and Distribution 
</p>
<p>The distribution of θ particles across the plate thickness is very inhomogeneous in thick AA2219 
</p>
<p>plate.2 Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) backscatter montages illustrating distribution of θ 
</p>
<p>particles at five different thickness locations, from t/6 to t/2 in lot 930071 (722C-29), are shown 
</p>
<p>in Figure 15. It is apparent that the size of θ particles at t/6 (0.83 in) is much smaller and more 
</p>
<p>dispersed than that at t/3, near 3t/8 and near 2t/5 locations. The typical microstructure of AA2219 
</p>
<p>plate material (lot 930071) near 3t/8 (1.875 in from plate surface) and near 2t/5 (2.05 in from plate 
</p>
<p>surface) locations is presented in Figure 16. As shown, many θ particles are agglomerated along 
</p>
<p>the grain boundaries in these two locations. Both area percentage and size analysis for θ particles 
</p>
<p>suggest the weakest location is situated in the proximity of the 3t/8 (1.875 in from plate surface) 
</p>
<p>thickness location.2 The coarse and agglomerated θ particles are harder than α-Al matrix and will 
</p>
<p>be subjected to high stress concentration during tensile testing. θ particles were found to promote 
</p>
<p>crack initiation and propagation and play a significant role in the tensile fracture behavior of the 
</p>
<p>thick AA2219-T87 plates.2 The root cause of through-thickness edge effect can be attributed to the 
</p>
<p>inhomogeneous distribution of θ particles across the plate thickness. This finding explains why 
</p>
<p>inelastic strain and ultimate tensile strength decrease significantly in ST tensile specimens obtained 
</p>
<p>near 2t/5 and t/3 locations, as shown in Figure 5. </p>

<p>4.3.2 Effect of Rolling Temperature on Microstructure Evolution 
</p>
<p>AA2219-T87 plate production begins with homogenization of a direct-chill (DC) cast ingot 
</p>
<p>(~16 inches in height) at a temperature slightly below 1018 °F (548 °C) to reduce Cu segregation 
</p>
<p>and transform the coarse Al-Cu eutectic phase to α-Al and θ phase.2 The ingot is cooled naturally 
</p>
<p>in air after homogenization and quickly transported to a rolling mill. Hot-rolling starts when the 
</p>
<p>ingot temperature decreases to approximately 750 °F (399 °C). Precipitation of θ can occur at grain 
</p>
<p>boundaries during hot-rolling, which is very problematic for thick AA2219-T87 plate material. A 
</p>
<p>2219 time-temperature-precipitation (TTP) diagram8 was used to project through-thickness 
</p>
<p>microstructure evolution during hot-rolling, as shown in Figure 18. There are two C-curves that 
</p>
<p>show the critical time (tc) and temperature that θ phase begins to precipitate at grain boundaries 
</p>
<p>and in the α-Al matrix for AA2219 during heat treatment or thermo-mechanical processing. As 
</p>
<p>shown in Figure 18, the ingot cooling from homogenization to completion of hot-rolling proceeds 
</p>
<p>through a series of successive steps: a continuous cooling step from homogenization (the dotted 
</p>
<p>red curve) to the start of hot-rolling; a hot-rolling step (the solid red curves); and a continuous 
</p>
<p>cooling step to ambient temperature after hot-rolling (the dotted orange curves). The projected 
</p>
<p>cooling curves are based on the assumptions that it would take the ingot 10 to 15 minutes  
</p>
<p>(600–900 seconds) to cool down to 750 °F (399 °C) after homogenization and that hot-rolling lasts 
</p>
<p>for approximately 10 min (600 s). The estimated ingot cooling curve from homogenization (red 
</p>
<p>dotted curve), the isothermal hot-rolling curves (bold red lines), and the plate cooling curves after 
</p>
<p>hot-rolling (orange dotted curves) are superimposed on the TTP curve of 2219 to illustrate the 
</p>
<p>microstructure evolution that can occur from homogenization to completion of the hot-rolling step. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>16 
</p>
<p> 
Figure 18. A 2219 TTP diagram and tc for the precipitation of θ at grain boundaries and in the  
</p>
<p>α-Al matrix.8 Due to the inhomogeneous temperature distribution across the plate thickness, the 
</p>
<p>temperature can vary by as much as 60 °C in a thick aluminum plate from plate surface to plate 
</p>
<p>core.2 
</p>
<p>It has been reported that the through-thickness temperature of a 6-in thick AA7075 plate can vary 
</p>
<p>significantly up to 60 °C.2,5 Assuming the through-thickness temperature varies up to 60 °C when 
</p>
<p>hot-rolling a thick AA2219 plate at 750 °F (399 °C), the through-thickness temperature distribution 
</p>
<p>can vary significantly from the plate surface to the plate core, as shown in Figure 18. The entire 
</p>
<p>plate enters the matrix θ formation C-curve during cooling to 750 °F (from homogenization), as 
</p>
<p>shown in Figure 18. Precipitation of grain boundary (GB) θ is expected to occur near the plate core 
</p>
<p>when the hot-rolling temperature-time curve (the plate core curve) intercepts the C-curve of grain 
</p>
<p>boundary θ phase (at point S). Grain boundary θ phase can continue to precipitate at the plate core 
</p>
<p>during hot-rolling. After completion of hot-rolling, precipitation and growth of grain boundary θ 
</p>
<p>phase can continue at the plate core during natural cooling until the temperature drops below the 
</p>
<p>C-curve of grain boundary θ phase (at point F). In contrast, the plate surface temperature is 
</p>
<p>significantly lower than that at the plate mid-plane location, and the hot-rolling temperature time-
</p>
<p>curve never intercepts the C-curve of grain boundary θ phase during hot-rolling. Therefore, a 
</p>
<p>higher amount of grain boundary θ particles is expected to be present toward the plate mid-
</p>
<p>thickness location. The observed through-thickness edge effect can be partially attributed to 
</p>
<p>variation in through-thickness temperature distribution during hot-rolling that leads to 
</p>
<p>inhomogeneous distribution of θ particles across the plate thickness. 
</p>
<p>4.3.3 Effects of Rolling Strain and Temperature on Fragmentation of θ Particles 
</p>
<p>There is a significant difference in the size, volume fraction, and distribution of θ phase (Al2Cu) 
</p>
<p>at the edge (plate surface) and center (plate mid-plane) of a 5-in thick AA2219 plate.2 The 
</p>
<p>differences in volume fraction and size of θ phase between the edge and center of the plate can be 
</p>
<p>attributed to the variations in through-thickness rolling strain and temperature distribution. It has </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>17 
</p>
<p>been reported5 that there are considerable variations in temperature and effective strain at different 
</p>
<p>layers of a thick aluminum plate during the hot-rolling process, as shown in Figure 19. The 
</p>
<p>temperature and strain data shown in Figure 19 was generated using a 150-mm (5.9-in) thick 7050 
</p>
<p>plate. It can be seen from Figure 19(a) that the temperature history of plate core and surface is 
</p>
<p>quite different during hot-rolling. At the start of rolling, the surface temperature dropped rapidly 
</p>
<p>because the surface layer of the plate was in direct contact with the rollers, which increased heat 
</p>
<p>dissipation and resulted in a rapid temperature drop. In contrast to the surface behavior, the 
</p>
<p>temperature of the core area increased slightly during hot-rolling as additional heat is generated by 
</p>
<p>plastic deformation without immediate heat dissipation. 
</p>
<p> 
Figure 19. Distribution of (a) temperature and (b) effective strain at plate surface and core during 
</p>
<p>hot-rolling a 5.9-in thick 7050 plate.5 
</p>
<p>In addition to the variation in temperature between plate surface and core, the effective rolling 
</p>
<p>strain also varies through-thickness and affects fragmentation of θ particles in AA2219.5,9 Rolling 
</p>
<p>deformation cannot be uniformly distributed through the whole plate thickness, and the surface 
</p>
<p>layer is more deformed than the center layer. As shown in Figure 19(b), the effective strain 
</p>
<p>increases drastically at the plate surface when the rolling starts. The maximum effective strain 
</p>
<p>value is present on the surface. During hot-rolling of a thick AA2219 plate, rolling strain 
</p>
<p>incompatibility will develop at the interface of the θ particles and α-Al matrix because of their 
</p>
<p>differences in elastic constants and hardness, which causes stress concentration at the hard θ 
</p>
<p>particles.10 When the rolling temperature is high (e.g., exceeds 410 °C), the deformation resistance 
</p>
<p>of the material is reduced as the α-Al matrix is very soft. As a result, the rolling-induced stress is 
</p>
<p>not sufficient to break up the hard θ particles. When the plate is hot-rolled at a lower temperature 
</p>
<p>(e.g., 360 °C), stress concentration at the hard θ particles is expected to increase significantly 
</p>
<p>because of the increase in the effective strain and flow stress of the material when the plate is 
</p>
<p>colder. This explains why θ particles are more fragmented and dispersed toward the plate edge as 
</p>
<p>the plate surface layers are subjected to lower rolling temperature and higher rolling strain during 
</p>
<p>hot-rolling. In contrast, the hard θ particles tend to agglomerate near the plate center where the 
</p>
<p>temperature is higher and rolling stress/strain is too low to consistently break them up.   
</p>
<p>4.3.4 Effect of θ Particle Size and Shape on Cu Dissolution During Solution Treatment 
</p>
<p>The dispersed and fragmented θ particles, which have sharp corners and edges, can be more 
</p>
<p>thoroughly dissolved into Al-Cu solid solution during solution treatment because Cu diffusion is 
</p>
<p>facilitated by the increased total superficial area of θ particles.9 In contrast, the coarse and agglom-
</p>
<p>erated θ particles are harder to dissolve into Al-Cu solid solution than the finely dispersed θ </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>18 
</p>
<p>because the diffusion of Cu atoms through the coarse agglomerated θ particles is more restricted. 
</p>
<p>Toward the plate surface, where θ particles are more fragmented and dispersed, solution treatment 
</p>
<p>can take a significantly higher amount of Cu into the Al-Cu solid solution to maximize 
</p>
<p>precipitation hardening after artificial aging. θ particles are coarser and more agglomerated toward 
</p>
<p>the plate core, which is expected to lower Cu content in Al-Cu solid solution after solution 
</p>
<p>treatment. The strength of AA2219 is related to the size, distribution, and volume fraction of the 
</p>
<p>primary strengthening precipitate, θ'. The differences in Cu concentration (in Al-Cu solid solution) 
</p>
<p>and the degree of precipitation hardening contribute to the observed strength variations (edge 
</p>
<p>effects) across the plate width and thickness, as shown in Figure 2(a) and Figure 4(a). 
</p>
<p>4.3.5 Effect of Plate Thickness on Quench Cooling Rate 
</p>
<p>From the hardness profiles presented in Figure 13, it is apparent that the 5-in thick 2219-T87 plate 
</p>
<p>is quench sensitive. The quench cooling rate in a thick aluminum alloy plate is thickness-dependent 
</p>
<p>and is limited by the thermal conductivity of the material.11 The cooling rate at the mid-plane of a 
</p>
<p>thick plate is naturally slower than that near the surface areas. Figure 20 shows the average cooling 
</p>
<p>rates of aluminum alloy plates, with thicknesses ranging from 1.6 mm (0.06 in) to 250 mm (8 in), 
</p>
<p>achieved by quenching at three different water temperatures and in still air. The cooling rates 
</p>
<p>shown in Figure 20 are for the location most distant from the surface, which is the mid-plane 
</p>
<p>location in a plate or sheet. The dashed red line indicates the theoretical maximum cooling rate 
</p>
<p>that can be obtained at the plate mid-planes with different thicknesses. As shown in Figure 20, the 
</p>
<p>cooling rate at 400–290 °C (750–550 °F) at the mid-plane of a typical 127-mm (5-in) thick plate 
</p>
<p>is about 1.0 °C/s when immersing the plate into 82 °C water. In comparison, the cooling rate at the 
</p>
<p>near surface location of a 127-mm (5-in) thick plate can be estimated from the thin plate data to 
</p>
<p>increase to &gt; 100 °C/s under the same quenching condition, which is more than a 100-fold increase 
</p>
<p>in the quench cooling rate. Similar variation in quench cooling rate can be expected across the 
</p>
<p>plate width. The plate width edge is expected to have a higher cooling rate than the plate width 
</p>
<p>center because the width edge area is in direct contact with the quench medium. A higher quench 
</p>
<p>cooling rate at the width edge can make the material harder after artificial aging by reducing θ 
</p>
<p>precipitation and increasing the volume fraction of the primary strengthening precipitate, θ'. In 
</p>
<p>comparison, lower quench cooling rate at the width center softens the material due to precipitation 
</p>
<p>of higher volume fraction of θ phase that lowers the concentration of the primary strengthening 
</p>
<p>precipitate, θ', after artificial aging. </p>

<p>4.3.6 Effect of Quench Cooling Rate on Microstructure Evolution for AA2219 Plate 
</p>
<p>When AA2219 is held at the solution temperature (995 °F) to permit diffusion of Cu, most θ 
</p>
<p>particles will dissolve, and Cu will be taken into α-Al solid solution. AA2219 requires a very fast 
</p>
<p>quench cooling rate to maintain a supersaturated Al-Cu solid solution in α-Al matrix. It is difficult 
</p>
<p>to achieve the desired fast quench cooling rates in the plate core for thick plate products. The 
</p>
<p>atomic fraction of the Cu element that is bound in quench-induced θ precipitate reduces the amount 
</p>
<p>of Cu solute available for the formation of the strengthening precipitate, θ', during artificial aging. 
</p>
<p>Due to the combined effect of increased θ precipitation and reduced volume fraction of the primary 
</p>
<p>strengthening θ' precipitate, several mechanical properties, such as tensile strength and ductility, 
</p>
<p>are significantly affected. 
</p>
<p>A quench-sensitive alloy, such as AA2219, generally has the nose of its matrix θ C-curve (the blue 
</p>
<p>curve) shifted to the left (indicating very little time is required for θ precipitation to occur during 
</p>
<p>quench cooling), which is the primary root cause of quench sensitivity for AA2219, as shown in 
</p>
<p>Figure 21.8 A reasonable explanation for the high quench sensitivity of AA2219 is its high Cu 
</p>
<p>content, approximately 6.4% for thick plates. The maximum solubility of Cu in α-Al matrix is 
</p>
<p>approximately 5.2% Cu at 995 °F, as shown in Figure 22.8 Two schematic quench cooling curves 
</p>
<p>(faster cooling at plate surface and slower cooling at mid-plane) are superimposed on the TTP 
</p>
<p>diagram, as illustrated in Figure 21, to demonstrate the effects of quench cooling rate on θ 
</p>
<p>precipitation. The faster quench cooling curve misses the nose of θ phase C-curve, and θ does not 
</p>
<p>form during quenching. The slower quench cooling curve intercepts the C-curve at approximately 
</p>
<p>450 °C (at point S), which is the onset of θ precipitation during quench cooling. θ can grow 
</p>
<p>continuously until the material is cooled outside the θ forming C-curve at approximately 375 °C 
</p>
<p>(at point F). The quench-induced θ precipitate has very little hardening effect in itself,12,13 and the 
</p>
<p>precipitation of θ phase consumes Cu atoms in Al-Cu solid solution and reduces the amount of Cu 
</p>
<p>solute available for the formation of the strengthening precipitate, θ', during artificial aging. This 
</p>
<p>explains why abundant precipitation of θ phase is the primary root cause of quench sensitivity that 
</p>
<p>contributes to reduced hardness and strength at near mid-plane locations for thick AA2219 plates.14 </p>

<p>4.4 The Use of DSC for Edge Effect Screening 
</p>
<p>AA2219-T87 thick plates have significant variation in the size and volume fraction of θ phase 
</p>
<p>through the plate thickness. The use of quantitative θ particle size and area percentage analysis 
</p>
<p>successfully identified the weakest locations (across the plate thickness) of a couple of 5-in thick 
</p>
<p>AA2219 plates.2 Quantitative θ particle size and area percentage analysis require tedious 
</p>
<p>metallographic work using both optical microscopy and backscatter SEM. The local concentration 
</p>
<p>and size of the brittle θ (Al2Cu) particles were measured using the thresholding tool in Fiji (ImageJ) 
</p>
<p>analysis software.2 </p>
<p></p>
</div>

</p>
<p>problematic thick AA2219 plate lots in a timelier fashion. For AA2219, there is a strong endother-
</p>

</p>
<p>can be used to determine Al-Cu eutectic content in the sample.4 The area of Al-Cu eutectic melting 
</p>

</p>

<p>4.5 Edge Effect Root Cause Analysis Summary 
</p>
<p>Based on the tensile data, hardness profiles, and microstructure analysis, the key production steps 
</p>
<p>and microstructure evolutions that influence the observed edge effect for 5-in thick AA2219 plate 
</p>
<p>are summarized in Figure 27. The formation of θ phase starts when the Al-Cu eutectic phase, which 
</p>
<p>forms during the terminal stage of solidification in ingot casting, decomposes into α-Al and θ 
</p>
<p>during ingot homogenization. Large θ particles can fracture into small θ particles during hot-
</p>
<p>rolling, and the degree of θ fragmentation is rolling temperature and effective strain-dependent. θ 
</p>
<p>particles are more fragmented and dispersed toward the plate surface, as the plate edge is cooler 
</p>
<p>and has higher rolling strain than the plate center. In contrast, the hard and brittle θ particles tend 
</p>
<p>to grow and agglomerate along GBs near the plate center where the temperature is higher and 
</p>
<p>rolling stress/strain is too low to consistently break up the hard θ particles. AA2219 typically 
</p>
<p>contains up to 6.4 – 6.5 wt% of Cu, and Cu does not go completely into α-Al solid solution during 
</p>
<p>solution treatment at 995 °F. The solubility of Cu in α-Al matrix is approximately 5.2 wt% at </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>26 
</p>
<p>solution temperature of 995 °F (see Figure 22). Therefore, 1 – 2 wt% of undissolved θ particles 
</p>
<p>will remain in the α-Al matrix. If the alloy is cooled slowly during quenching (after solution 
</p>
<p>treatment), additional θ particles can form and the pre-existing θ particles have time to coarsen. 
</p>
<p>There will be inadequate time for additional θ particles to precipitate when the plate is cooled 
</p>
<p>rapidly during quenching after solution treatment. The precipitation and coarsening of θ phase 
</p>
<p>during quenching decreases Cu concentration in α-Al matrix, which lowers the degree of 
</p>
<p>precipitation hardening after artificial aging. Therefore, the key mechanical properties, such as 
</p>
<p>tensile strength and ductility, near the plate core are affected by the combined effect of increased 
</p>
<p>θ precipitation and reduced volume fraction of the primary strengthening precipitate, θ'. In 
</p>
<p>summary, the difference in the precipitate microstructure at the edge and center of the plate results 
</p>
<p>in the observed edge effect for 5-in thick AA2219 plate. 
</p>

</p>

<p>5.0 Summary and Conclusions 
</p>
<p>(1) Through-thickness edge effect can be attributed to three factors: (1) rolling temperature is lower 
</p>
<p>at t/6 (near surface) than at t/3 and 2t/5 (near mid-plane), which mitigates grain boundary θ 
</p>
<p>precipitation at t/6 (near surface); (2) more pronounced θ fragmentation occurs at t/6 (near surface) 
</p>
<p>than at 2t/5 (near mid-plane), as the effective rolling strain is higher at t/6 (near surface) than at 
</p>
<p>2t/5 (near mid-plane), which results in more Cu available for precipitaion hardening during aging; 
</p>
<p>(3) quench cooling rate is higher at t/6 (near surface), which inhibits precipitation of θ phase and 
</p>
<p>enhances Cu supersaturation in α-Al matrix during quench cooling. The increased Cu 
</p>
<p>supersaturation in α-Al matrix promotes θ' precipitation during artificial aging. As a result, the 
</p>
<p>volume fraction of θ' is higher at the edge than at the center of the thick plate, and the content of 
</p>
<p>brittle θ (Al2Cu) phase is lower at the edge (near surface) than at the center (near mid-plane). The 
</p>
<p>favorable microstructure evolution during hot-rolling and quench cooling leads to a noticeable 
</p>
<p>improvement in ductility and tensile strength toward the plate edge. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>27 
</p>
<p>(2) Through-width edge effect is primarily caused by quench sensitivity as evidenced by a distinct 
</p>
<p>through-width hardness gradient from plate width edge (higher hardness) to plate width center 
</p>
<p>(lower hardness). Higher quench cooling rate at the width edge strengthens the material by 
</p>
<p>increasing the supersaturation of Cu in Al-Cu solid solution and promoting precipitation of θ' 
</p>
<p>during artificial aging treatment. Ductility is also higher at the width edge, as precipitation and 
</p>
<p>coarsening of the brittle θ phase is suppressed due to faster quench cooling rate. In contrast, lower 
</p>
<p>quench cooling rate at the plate width center promotes θ precipitation and decreases the super-
</p>
<p>saturation of Cu in Al-Cu solid solution, which leads to reduced ductility and tensile strength (due 
</p>
<p>to reduced θ' precipitation) after artificial aging. The favorable microstructure evolution during 
</p>
<p>quench cooling leads to a noticeable improvement in ductility and tensile strength toward the plate 
</p>
<p>width edge. 
</p>
<p>(3) DSC is a viable time-saving screening method that can determine the weaker location through 
</p>
<p>the plate thickness. Based on the variation in mass fraction of Al-Cu eutectic phase, the weakest 
</p>
<p>location is projected to be between 3t/8 and t/3 locations, which is consistent with the results 
</p>
<p>obtained from quantitative size and area fraction analyses of θ particle using SEM and the 
</p>
<p>quantitative image analysis technique. 
</p>
