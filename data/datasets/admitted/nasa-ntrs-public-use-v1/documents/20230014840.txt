Linear Parameter-Varying Control Compatible with Excitation
for Real-Time Modeling



Luke J. Miller ∗



NASA Langley Research Center Hampton, Virginia, 23681



The Modeling and Control for Agile Aircraft Development project (MCAAD) seeks to
reduce the time spent on wind tunnel testing and computational fluid dynamic modeling when
developing control laws for new commercial and military aircraft. MCAAD uses advanced
dynamic modeling procedures coupled with real-time control design which can reduce the time
and cost spent on traditional aircraft development methods. This is done in-flight by using a
real-time modeling procedure where programmed test inputs (PTI) deflect the aircraft control
surfaces, and the responses are measured in order to create a dynamic model. This paper
presents an 𝐻∞-based linear parameter-varying (LPV) control law designed on a nonlinear
rigid-body dynamic model which commands control surface deflections at frequencies above
the PTI frequency band. Thus the controller will not suppress the response due to the PTI. The
LPV controller is compared with a previously designed Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion (NDI)
controller and the coherence between the commanded control surface deflections and the PTI is
plotted for both controllers. The LPV controller is shown to distort the PTI less than the NDI
controller.

II. Introduction
Stability and reference tracking are paramount concerns to a control system designer. However, in order to ensure



stability and tracking performance, an accurate model of the aircraft system must be available. One of the goals of the
NASA Modeling and Control for Agile Aircraft Development (MCAAD) project is to develop an aircraft model and
aircraft control system in real time when there is no prior knowledge of the aircraft dynamics. This real-time modeling
and control design procedure has the potential to greatly reduce the development time and cost of commercial and
military aircraft.



Real-time model identification and real-time control design has been considered before to study actuator failure
cases, as flown on the VISTA/F-16 aircraft [1]. Additionally, identification schemes using prior information to enhance
parameter estimates in a recursive least-squares formulation have been used [2]. Reconfigurable control allocation in
the presence of actuator failure has also been well studied [3] [4]. The NASA Learn-to-Fly (L2F) project explored
simultaneous real-time control design and real-time aerodynamic modeling aimed at reducing time spent on traditional
aircraft development methods. The Learn-to-Fly project used baseline Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion (NDI) control laws,
with adaptive L1 augmentation which was developed by Snyder et al. [5], [6], [7]. The control architecture used in L2F
is depicted in fig. 1. The real-time aerodynamic modeling under L2F was developed by Morelli [8].



Fig. 1 Control Architecture used in the NASA Learn-to-Fly project.



The L2F project successfully demonstrated the feasibility of simultaneous control design and real-time aerodynamic
modeling. However, the NDI control law incorporated the aerodynamic modeling parameters which are learned in



2











real-time and are subject to large initial error. This could cause the aircraft to behave in undesirable and unpredictable
ways.



This paper proposes a framework of an alternative solution to the L2F problem, performed under the NASA MCAAD
project and presents initial results, shown to be more compatible with excitation necessary for real-time modeling. This
solution seeks to remove the influence of the modeling results on the control system by incorporating the real-time
aerodynamic modeling results exclusively in the control allocator. Figure 2 shows the influence of the modeling on
the control system, experienced under the L2F project. Figure 3 shows the proposed removal of the influence of the
modeling on the control under the MCAAD project.

The full extent of the proposed control architecture is shown in fig. 4. Successfully removing the influence of the
aerodynamics on the control system will allow the control designer to only consider rigid-body equations of motion,
which will greatly simplify the controls problem. This paper shows that an 𝐻∞-based linear parameter-varying (LPV)
control law can be used to shape the response of the rigid-body equations of motion into a more compatible response
with excitation for real-time modeling than that of the NDI control law used in the L2F project while, establishing
uniform global exponential stability of the rigid body dynamics.



Fig. 4 Proposed comprehensive control architecture to be developed under NASA MCAAD project.



Successful development of the control architecture shown in fig. 4 will entail the implementation of closed-loop,
exponentially stabilizing control allocation laws. Johansen showed that this was possible through a control-Lyapunov



3











design approach which derived an optimal control allocation algorithm which guarantees uniform boundedness and
uniform global exponential convergence in conjunction with an exponentially stable trajectory-tracking controller [9].
The 𝐻∞-based LPV controller presented in this paper can serve as the exponentially stable trajectory tracking controller
necessary for the development of closed-loop stabilizing control allocators.



Under the proposed control architecture in fig. 4 the control allocation is designed based on aerodynamic modeling
parameters, which are learned in real-time and are subject to large initial error. Due to this error, the stabilizing allocator
might not realize the exact moment commanded by the LPV controller. This could cause undesirable and unpredictable
behavior in the aircraft. To address this problem, an adaptive control allocation element will be used to adjust control
surface deflection commands. A control-Lyapunov approach can be used to derive these adaptive control allocation
laws and is shown to prove closed-loop stability [10].



In this paper an LPV control design methodology is presented based on rigid-body equations of motion and is
shown to be more compatible with real-time modeling methods than the NDI control developed under the L2F project.
Additionally, this control design using a rigid-body model ensures robustness and exponential stability [11] which
Johansen shows is necessary for the development of any future stabilizing allocators [9]. This technique could also be
applied to aircraft models subject to more complex dynamic effects, such as slosh or flex modes. The LPV technique is
an alternative to the traditional gain scheduling process. The dynamic model and controller are still linear, but they
are dependent on time-varying parameters whose values can be measured, but are unknown in advance. A traditional
gain-scheduling approach involves linearizing dynamic models at many trim points and then designing linear time
invariant (LTI) controllers at each trim point. The global controller is then estimated by interpolating the gains of the
local controllers. This approach has been successfully implemented in many engineering applications but there is no
guarantee of exponential stability [12]. The LPV control strategy can show exponential stability for all possible values
of the parameters and can be implemented like a traditionally gain-scheduled control system [11] [13].



The LPV control strategy requires an affine dynamic system that must be converted into convex polytopic form
[11]. The rigid-body aircraft model is not affine and will result in an infinite number of Linear Matrix Inequalities
(LMIs) to determine the controller [11]. This difficulty is overcome using the Tensor Product (TP) model transformation
to convert the nonaffine system into convex polytopic form. There is then a linear control allocator generated from
a linearized system model produced by the real-time modeling procedure. The control allocator commands control
surface deflections such that the moment commanded by the controller is realized. The newly developed LPV control
law was tested on a nonlinear simulation of the NASA E1 aircraft, depicted in fig. 5.

The real-time modeling procedure is also described in detail in [8]. The PTI excitations, which are necessary for
real-time modeling, produce aircraft responses which the control system sees as disturbances that must be suppressed.
This creates a fundamental conflict. Good control tends to mitigate disturbances, such as those due to the PTI. However,
real time modeling needs to observe the aircraft response to the PTI in order to accurately construct global aerodynamic
models.

V. Linear Parameter-Varying Control Design
It is desired that the controller track the guidance commands, but also not suppress the response of the aircraft to the



PTI excitations. The guidance algorithms are described in detail in [18]. To design the LPV control loops to achieve
adequate tracking performance, there are two penalty filters in the inner loop,𝑊1,𝑖𝑛𝑛𝑒𝑟 and𝑊2,𝑖𝑛𝑛𝑒𝑟 , and one penalty
filter,𝑊1,𝑜𝑢𝑡𝑒𝑟 in the outer loop. The control architecture is built using the hinfgs MATLAB routine and shown in fig.
8.



Fig. 8 Detailed 𝐻∞ LPV Control Architecture



The inner loop controller is designed to penalize moment commands inside the PTI bandwidth, through minimization
of the output of𝑊2,𝑖𝑛𝑛𝑒𝑟 , 𝑍2,𝑖𝑛𝑛𝑒𝑟 . 𝑊2,𝑖𝑛𝑛𝑒𝑟 is chosen as a first-order high-pass Bessel filter with cutoff frequency, 𝜔𝑐,2

C. Control Allocation
Control allocation algorithms are tasked with determining aircraft control surface deflections to achieve the desired

problem as a constrained-optimization problem [19–25]. The allocator in this paper is taken from the L2F project, [5]
and is used for both the LPV and NDI control laws.

VII. Conclusions
In this work a two-loop LPV control law was designed for the NASA E1 aircraft. The synthesis of both control loops



requires an affine LPV model, which was generated from the non-affine kinematic-based E1 model using the TP method.
The derived two-loop LPV controller is tested on a Simulink® simulation of the NASA E1 aircraft, which compared the
performance of the LPV controller to a previously designed NDI controller. It was found that the LPV controller is able
to track guidance commands, but could exhibit slightly worse tracking performance than the NDI controller. Despite the
slightly worse tracking performance, the LPV controller exhibits desirable characteristics such as its invariance of the
real-time modeling parameters and its guarantee of exponential stability. Additionally, by commanding higher frequency
control surface commands, the LPV control law distorts the PTI less, which will minimize interaction between real-time
modeling and real-time control design.



Future work will entail development of nonlinear-stabilizing control allocation algorithms to and adaptive control
allocation algorithms.
