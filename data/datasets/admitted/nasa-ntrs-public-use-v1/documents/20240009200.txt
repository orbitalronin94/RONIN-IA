<p>Jettison Policy Requirements
The ISS Jettison Policy intends to quantify and control the risks of deploying and operating small satellites, not only to 
</p>
<p>ensure the safety of the humans flying in space, but also to preserve the orbital environment for world space activities 
</p>
<p>and enable the significant benefits brought by such utilization. 
</p>
<p>*Trackability: The jettison candidate(s) shall be trackable by the Space Surveillance Network (SSN).
</p>
<p>*Limit Generation of Orbital Debris: Candidates with stored energy systems  shall be passivated by End of Mission 
</p>
<p>(EOM) to prevent fragmentation or generation of additional orbital debris. If the jettison candidate cannot or will not be 
</p>
<p>passivated, the payload developer (PD) will provide analysis that the risk of fragmentation is acceptably low. For EVA 
</p>
<p>based jettisons, bundle multiple jettison candidates together when possible.
</p>
<p>*Limit Risk of Collision with ISS: All objects planned for jettison shall verify that they do not contact any ISS structure 
</p>
<p>during jettison. For candidates to be jettisoned from the ISS via EVA, analysis shall verify that the planned velocity 
</p>
<p>vector of the jettison candidate is the axis of an unobstructed cone of a 30° half-angle (minimum), and that the object is 
</p>
<p>within acceptable EVA control (i.e.,“handle-able”). For candidates to be jettisoned robotically, the clearance cone must 
</p>
<p>be larger than the worst case accuracy of the jettison mechanism, as defined by the robotics deploy mechanism system 
</p>
<p>owner and implementing ISS robotics team and safety team. 
</p>
<p>*Limit Risk of Re-Contact with the ISS: All objects planned for jettison shall verify safe relative motion with the ISS. 
</p>
<p>The jettison candidate shall clear the 200 meter ISS Keep Out Sphere (KOS) within 1 orbit and maintain a positive 
</p>
<p>departing rate while in the KOS. Nominal return time to the vicinity of ISS shall be no less than 30 days from the jettison 
</p>
<p>date; in the worst-case contingency jettison scenario, the jettisoned object’s return time to the vicinity of ISS shall be no 
</p>
<p>less than 10 days. ISS reboosts or avoidance maneuvers will not be considered in these analyses.
</p>
<p>*Limit Risk of Collision with ISS Visiting Vehicles: All objects planned for jettison from ISS Visiting Vehicles or their 
</p>
<p>associated launch vehicles shall verify that they do not pose an immediate collision risk with ISS. Candidates jettisoned 
</p>
<p>lower than ISS shall have an invariant apogee at least 5 km below ISS invariant perigee. Candidates jettisoned higher 
</p>
<p>than ISS shall have a Semi-Major Axis (SMA) of at least 45 km above the ISS SMA. 
</p>
<p>*Propulsive Jettison Candidate Criteria: Establish the proper flow of trajectory data between the US Space Force, 
</p>
<p>NASA, and the propulsive candidate’s mission operations team. 
</p>
<p>a. Establish data sharing process with 18th Space Defense Squadron &amp; ISS Trajectory Operations Officers (TOPO)
</p>
<p>b. Demonstrate that the satellite operators are Responsible and Safe Space Operators (RSSO) 
</p>
<p>c. Operate the satellite such that it cannot interfere with ISS operations.  
</p>

</p>
<p>Payload Developers
</p>
<p>Adaptability and Innovation
The ISS Program is committed to working with smallsat providers to 
</p>
<p>address their challenges and enable safe, accessible, innovative 
</p>
<p>missions. The Policy has grown with the industry, with each deploy 
</p>
<p>yielding hard-earned lessons learned that improve our process – not 
</p>
<p>only for the next deploy campaign, but with applicability and 
</p>
<p>adaptability for future applications in LEO and beyond. 
</p>
<p>- “Expedited Approval Criteria” are included to simplify and expedite 
</p>
<p>the analysis and approval process for candidates that pose a low 
</p>
<p>risk to the ISS and ISS Visiting Vehicles.
</p>
<p>- Introduced in Rev C (2021), RSSO requirements provide 
</p>
<p>developers of propulsive or complex satellites an opportunity to 
</p>
<p>demonstrate that they can operate independent of NASA guidance 
</p>
<p>without impacting ISS safety. 
</p>
<p>Jettison candidate = any object released from the ISS or an ISS Visiting 
</p>
<p>Vehicle while the vehicle is in free flight, as well as any deployables originating 
</p>
<p>from those objects. The ISS deploys dozens of CubeSats and other small 
</p>
<p>satellites into Low Earth Orbit (LEO) each year!
</p>
<p>In addition, other objects are jettisoned if they cannot be safely returned in a 
</p>
<p>Visiting Vehicle if they:
*Pose a safety issue for the ISS or for return 
</p>
<p>*Negatively impact ISS utilization, return manifests, or on-orbit stowage manifests.
</p>
<p>*Represent an Extravehicular Activity (EVA) timeline savings large enough to reduce the 
</p>
<p>sum of the risks of EVA exposure time and the orbital environment’s hazardous debris 
</p>
<p>population, compared to the sum of such risks without a jettison.
</p>

process for jettison candidates typically 
takes roughly 3-6 months, depending on 
the complexity of the jettison candidate.
