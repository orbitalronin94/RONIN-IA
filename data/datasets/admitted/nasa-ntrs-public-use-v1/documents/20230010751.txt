</p>
<p>Threat from Orbital Debris - Examples
</p>
<p>• Satellite operators, including the International Space Station (ISS) Program, 
</p>
<p>conduct collision avoidance maneuvers against the tracked objects on a regular 
</p>
<p>basis
</p>
<p>– ISS has conducted 35 collision avoidance maneuvers and 5 “shelter-in-Soyuz” since 1999
</p>
<p>– During shuttle operations, on average, two shuttle windows 
were replaced per mission
</p>
<p>• Impacts by small, untracked debris could be responsible for many satellite 
</p>
<p>anomalies
</p>
<p>– Collisions with large trackable objects account for less than 1% mission ending risk; risk 
comes from small mm size debris 
</p>
<p>– Lack of measurement data on mm-sized debris above 600 km altitude
</p>
<p>https://hvit.jsc.nasa.gov/impact-images/international-space-station.cfm
</p>

side radiator 
</p>

from MMOD
</p>

</p>

<p>7
</p>
<p>Hazards/Risks?
</p>
<p>• Debris left in orbits &gt; 600 km normally fall back to Earth within several years. At altitudes of 800 km, the time for 
</p>
<p>orbital decay is often measured in decades. Above 1,000 km, orbital debris will normally continue circling the Earth for 
</p>
<p>a century or more. 
</p>
<p>• The smaller the particle, the higher the potential for collision.
</p>
<p>• Kinetic Energy relation.
</p>
<p>– A 1 kg object in LEO involved in a collision with an object traveling at 10 km/s will have the 
same impact energy as a fully loaded 35,000 kg truck traveling at 190 km/h.</p>
