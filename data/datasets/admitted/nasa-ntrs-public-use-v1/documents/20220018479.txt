NASA’s Orion spacecraft is built to take humans farther than they’ve ever gone before. Orion will serve as the exploration vehicle that will carry the crew to lunar orbit, provide emergency abort capability, and return them safely from deep space return velocities. Orion will launch on NASA’s new heavy-lift rocket, the Space Launch System.

CREW MODULE
Orion is designed to accommodate 99% of the human population, which is a larger range than every other NASA or Department of Defense project past, present, or future.  The spacecraft can accommodate astronauts as small as a 4’10” tall female and as tall as a 6’5” male.
 
Orion’s crew module provides about 30 percent more cubic volume of space than the Apollo capsule.
 
HEATSHIELD
Orion’s heat shield withstands temperatures of about 5,000°F during re-entry.  Just how hot is 5,000°F?  Which is half the temperature of the Sun.
That is 2,000°F hotter than returning from the International Space Station
 
 
RETURN SPEED: WAY FASTER THAN A SPEEDING BULLET
Orion will return to Earth from the Moon at a speeds of about 25,000 mph. At this speed, Orion could travel from Los Angeles to New York City in 6 minutes, while a normal flight on commercial airlines takes 5.5 hours.
 
Orion will return to Earth at a speed of about 25,000mph from its missions to near the Moon. A spacecraft’s re-entry speed from a Mars mission would be about 27,000 mph – nearly 20 times faster than a supersonic jet can travel. (and more than 35 times faster than most speeding bullets)

Welding Orion’s seven large aluminum pieces, which began in September 2015, involved a meticulous process. Engineers prepared and outfitted each element with strain gauges and wiring to monitor the metal during the process. By the welding of the Artemis I pressure vessel, Lockheed has the welds down to only SEVEN welds. 
The pieces were joined using a state-of-the-art process called friction stir welding, which produces incredibly strong bonds by transforming metals from a solid into a plastic-like state and then using a rotating pin tool to soften, stir and forge a bond between two metal components to form a uniform welded joint, a vital requirement of next-generation space hardware.
MAF is home to the world’s largest tools for this type of friction stir welding. 
Decreased 30% of the crew module mass from its original size.
Orion will carry four astronauts to deep space.

The major join is complete in Vertical Assembly Center and will soon be mated with the engine section. 
The Vertical Assembly Center is the final major tool for welding hardware for the massive core stage of the Space Launch System.
The tool weighs three million pounds and is supported by 900 cubic yards of concrete and 100-feet-deep steel pilings to provide structural stability.
Designated a high-rise building by the International Building Code at approximately 7,500 square feet wide (?) and more than 170 feet tall, this integrated machine tool had to meet the construction standards for a skyscraper.
Approx. 1,000 tons of steel were used in its construction.
The MAF building containing the VAC is slightly taller than 200 feet. Despite this roof height, the overhead crane which lifts welded flight hardware clears the top of the tool by only ONE foot.
The VAC is among the largest and most complex Friction Stir Welding systems ever constructed. NASA engineers are not aware of another system in the world that rivals its size and capability.

Michoud: Building the Next Generation Rocket



Teams at NASA’s Michoud Assembly Facility in New Orleans are already building the core stage of NASA’s Space Launch System rocket for Artemis II, the first crewed mission to the Moon in NASA’s Artemis program, and Artemis III, the mission that will put the first woman and the first person of color on the Moon.
Production has also already begun on the core stage for Artemis IV as well as the fourth Orion, having successfully delivered the first through third Orion. 
Production has also begun on test articles for the Exploration Upper Stage (EUS) supporting the fourth flight. 

The Michoud Assembly Facility in New Orleans, a component facility managed by Marshall, has 3.8 million square feet of total infrastructure on 829-acres and is one of the few places SLS could be built.
Michoud is one of the largest manufacturing facilities in the world with the main manufacturing building occupying 2.2 million square feet of space, which is the size of 31 professional American football fields, and 43 acres under one roof.

Why is Nuclear Propulsion an Attractive Solution for a Mission to Mars?
Nuclear Thermal Propulsion (NTP) and Nuclear Electric Propulsion (NEP) allows for shorter total mission time and shorter trip time (Less exposure to galactic cosmic radiation and zero-g)
Nuclear allows mission robustness and potential abort scenarios
Fewer rocket launches can save operation time, money, and reduce risk
Current efforts in NTP are initial steps towards advanced space nuclear power and propulsion, which could eventually help enable exploration and development of the solar system

How Does Nuclear Thermal Propulsion (NTP) Work?
Propellant heated directly by a nuclear reactor and thermally expanded/accelerated through a nozzle (Low molecular weight propellant – typically Hydrogen)
Thrust directly related to thermal power of reactor:  100,000 N ≈ 450 MWth at 900 sec
Specific Impulse directly related to exhaust temperature: 830 - 1000 sec (2300 - 3100K)
Specific Impulse improvement over chemical rockets due to lower molecular weight of propellant (exhaust stream of O2/H2 engine runs much hotter than NTP)

Development Plan for NTP


Overall Nuclear Thermal Propulsion Strategy:
Develop a subscale nuclear engine that demonstrates a viable NTP propulsion system and is scalable to the Mars mission engine needs. Evolve to Mars engine system as a part of a Mars NP propulsive stage.
Develop a HALEU solid solution fuel form that can provide reactor exit temperatures > 2700 K (corresponds to > 900s Isp).
Design a reactor with an eye toward capability to be scaled to a size to support Mars mission engine needs. (Scaling is to be an engineering solution rather than further tech development.)
Design and develop a subscale engine (Key attribute goals: 10K-15K lbf thrust, 900s Isp)
Determine Demonstration option (either ground test or demonstration flight).

Conclusions: Why Nuclear, Why Now?
Space fission propulsion systems are game changing technologies for space exploration and cis-lunar mobility.

First generation NTP systems provide significant benefits to sustained human Mars exploration and other missions.
Imagine Earth-Mars transit times of 120 days; imagine 540 day total Mars mission times; imagine reduced crew health effects from cosmic radiation and exposure to microgravity; imagine robust architectures including abort capability. 

Advanced space fission power and propulsion systems will enable extremely ambitious space exploration and development.

Living and Working in Space



Marshall manages science operations around the clock on the International Space Station (ISS) from the Payload Operations Integration Center (pictured top right).
For more than 21 continuous years, we have supported science conducted on the station.
ISS serves as the world’s leading laboratory where researchers conduct cutting edge research and technology development that will enable human and robotic exploration of destinations beyond low-Earth orbit.

Marshall’s development of life support and research systems on ISS will benefit future human explorers who launch into deep space. 
Marshall contributes vital elements of the Space Station’s regenerative Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS), which provides water and oxygen for the crew, recycling wastewater into usable water and reducing supply costs. 
Marshall’s scientific hardware on ISS, including 11 science payload EXPRESS racks and two research gloveboxes provide a foundation for how science can be conducted on and around the Moon.

Marshall manages research into 3-D printing on ISS that will provide valuable information on how Additive Manufacturing technology works in space.
Marshall is partnering with industry and academia to mature concepts for habitats that will allow astronauts to live long-term on the lunar surface and ultimately to travel to Mars.
Through the NextSTEP effort, Marshall is working with commercial partners to mature technologies and concepts for a lunar Surface Habitat and Mars Transit Habitat.
Concepts developed through this program are also informing the Gateway lunar orbit facility and commercial LEO destinations.
