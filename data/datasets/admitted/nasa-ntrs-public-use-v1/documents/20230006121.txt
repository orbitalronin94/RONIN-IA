<p>Design Studies on Mechanically Geared, Magnetically 
</p>
<p>Geared, and Direct Drive Drivetrains for UAM Applications 
</p>
<p>Thomas Tallerico1, Jeffryes Chapman2, and Andrew Smith3 
NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, 44135 
</p>
<p>Urban air mobility vehicles require high reliability and high performance electric motor 
</p>
<p>drivetrains. In this paper, a design optimization study is carried out to compare mechanically 
</p>
<p>geared, magnetically geared, and direct drive motor drivetrain topologies’ achievable 
</p>
<p>performance in a 100 kW hover power quad rotor propulsor application. Design studies are 
</p>
<p>carried out accounting for a nominal mission profile and assuming constant propulsor rotor 
</p>
<p>RPMs of 400, 1000, 2000, and 3000 RPM. Results show that mechanically geared drives are 
</p>
<p>the lightest at all RPMs but their mass benefit over direct drive and magnetically geared 
</p>
<p>decays with increased rotor RPM. Direct drive is shown to always achieve the highest 
</p>
<p>efficiency due to its lower motor speed resulting in low electrical frequencies. Magnetically 
</p>
<p>geared drivetrains in the initial study are required to provide 50% torque margin and have 
</p>
<p>similar performance to direct drive drivetrains. It is shown that reducing this margin makes 
</p>
<p>magnetically geared drivetrains performance competitive with high efficiency mechanically 
</p>
<p>geared drivetrains and lighter than direct drive drivetrains.  
</p>
<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>Urban Air Mobility (UAM) requires high performance electric motor drivetrains to minimize the energy 
</p>
<p>consumption and cost of each flight. One of the fundamental questions in the design of an electric motor drivetrain is 
</p>
<p>whether or not to use a gearbox. Additionally, research groups at NASA [1], Texas A&amp;M [2], and The University of 
</p>
<p>Portland [3] [4] have proposed and developed magnetic gears for electric aircraft propulsion applications. This paper 
</p>
<p>presents a comparison of three electric aircraft drivetrain topologies (Mechanically Geared, Magnetically Geared, and 
</p>
<p>Direct Drive) within the context of a 100 kW hover power quadrotor UAM vehicle propulsor. 
</p>
<p>Past work by the University of Illinois studied mechanically geared vs direct drive drivetrains topologies for larger 
</p>
<p>fixed wing transport style aircraft [5]. Schweigert et al completed a detailed study on optimum machine speed and 
</p>
<p>gearbox design for electric cars [6]. Neither study targets UAM applications, mission profile considerations, or full 
</p>
<p>propulsion system design (gearbox, inverter, motor, and thermal management system).  
</p>
<p>In this paper, the UAM motor design tool developed in [7] and [8] is used to compare the different drivetrain 
</p>
<p>topologies. The target mission profile for the design is based on the NASA UAM concept vehicles studied in [9] [10] 
</p>
<p>and [11]. Prior studies by NASA on magnetically geared motors [12] [13] and [14] are used to down select magnetic 
</p>
<p>gear motor topology down to an outer stator magnetically geared motor (OSMGM). All three drive train topologies 
</p>
<p>are optimized for a 100 kW hover power propulsor at 400, 1000, 2000, and 3000 RPM. Results are compared in terms 
</p>
<p>of total propulsor mass and total propulsor mission efficiency. The results of this paper are only for a single set of 
</p>
<p>assumptions, power level, application, and technology and should not be considered generally applicable across the 
</p>
<p>full UAM design space.  
</p>
<p>Section II of this paper details design methodology and updates to the UAM motor design tool. Section III presents 
</p>
<p>results of the design study. Section IV provides a conclusion summarizing the results.  
</p>

</p>
<p>The design tools used in this paper corresponds to updated versions of the UAM motor drivetrain design tool 
</p>
<p>presented in [7]. The two flow diagrams in Figure 1 depict the two versions of the design tool used in this paper, one 
</p>
<p>for motor-based designs and one for OSMGM-based designs. The following sections first detail the updates to the 
</p>
<p>motor-based design tool and then the adaptation of the design tool to OSMGM-based designs.

<p>A. Updates to Motor Drivetrain Design Tool 
</p>
<p>1. Analytical Preoptimization 
</p>
<p>To reduce the computational cost of the detailed finite element analysis (FEA) based optimization, analytical pre-
</p>
<p>optimizations of gearbox, motor, inverter, and thermal management system (TMS) were incorporated into this version 
</p>
<p>of the motor drivetrain design tool. This analytical optimization uses the preoptimized mechanical gearbox 
</p>
<p>performance curves generated using the method described in [15], the analytical motor model from [8], the analytical 
</p>
<p>inverter design tool discussed in [7] and [16], and HEATSSPY [17] an open source thermal management system 
</p>
<p>design tool based on OPENMDAO. The analytical preoptimizations build a good initial population for the FEA based 
</p>
<p>tool. The FEA based optimization then filters out designs whose performance was overpredicted using analytical 
</p>
<p>equations before completing further optimization of the remaining designs.  
</p>
<p>2. Mechanical Gear Box Sizing  
</p>
<p>In [7], gearbox mass and efficiency were assumed to only depend on the gear ratio for a given power and output 
</p>
<p>speed. As an update to the tool, a gearbox specific torque index was added as a genetic optimization variable. Gearbox 
</p>
<p>designs were still pre-generated using the design tool described in [15]. The pareto fronts at each gear ratio analyzed 
</p>
<p>were then used to create a scattered interpolant function with inputs of gear ratio and specific torque index. The specific 
</p>
<p>torque index controls whether at a given gear ratio a high mass, high efficiency design or a low mass, low efficiency 
</p>
<p>design is selected. The result of this update is that the overall mass of the drivetrain in the mechanically geared results 
</p>
<p>do not purely trend with motor mass as they did in [7] and more significantly correlate with gearbox mass.  
</p>
<p>3. Rotor Loss at Each Mission Point 
</p>
<p>In past versions of the motor mission profile analysis, rotor magnet and iron loss were assumed to be constant 
</p>
<p>across the entire mission [7] [8]. To make some attempt to account for the effect of stator current on the magnitude of 
</p>
<p>rotor loss, max magnetic field in each component is calculated via static FEA for zero stator current and each mission 
</p>
<p>design point. The difference in max magnetic field for each stator current relative to no stator current is used to scale 
</p>
<p>the rotor and magnet losses calculated using the pseudo time stepping FEA at climb condition. The scaling is 
</p>
<p>completed using

</p>
<p>4. TMS Design Flow 
</p>
<p>In the previous version of the motor drivetrain design tool [7], the TMS liquid-to-air heat exchanger design was 
</p>
<p>completed once before passing the results to the transient thermal model of the motor and if that model resulted in a 
</p>
<p>failure of the design due to thermo-mechanical stress or winding thermochemical life, the design was assigned a fitness 
</p>
<p>of zero. In this updated version of the tool, a failure as a result of either inverter or motor transient thermal FEA results 
</p>
<p>in a redesign of the liquid-to-air heat exchanger with a lower target steady state temperature and another iteration of 
</p>
<p>the transient thermal models unless the target steady state temperature for the fluid has dropped below 50 C.  
</p>
<p>5. Inverter Transient  
</p>
<p>To raise the fidelity of the thermal analysis of the inverter in the design tool, a transient mission profile FEA 
</p>
<p>thermal analysis of the inverter was incorporated into the design flow. This allows for more accurate predictions of 
</p>
<p>inverter component temperature over the mission profile at a relatively small computational cost.  
</p>
<p>B. Adaptation for Direct Drive 
</p>
<p>For direct drive machines, three changes are made to the design tool. Firstly, the bearing code is changed from the 
</p>
<p>motor bearing code from [8] to the propellor shaft and bearing code from [15] so that both geared and direct drive 
</p>
<p>machines account for the bearings and shafts needed to react the loads of the propellor. Second, the assumed coolant 
</p>
<p>is changed from oil to a 50/50 PGW mixture since there is no gearbox requiring oil lubrication. Third, rotor pole count 
</p>
<p>is not an optimization variable for the direct drive case. Since RPM is assumed to be constant in these studies, electrical 
</p>
<p>frequency is used to define rotor pole count in the optimization. 
</p>

<p>C. Adaptation to OSMGM design Tool 
</p>
<p>1. Optimization Variables 
</p>
<p>For the OSMGM design tool, 12 genetic optimization variables are used. Table 1 lists the variables. Sun gear radius 
</p>
<p>instead of tip speed is used to control the diameter of the gear, because of magnetic gear’s high mass sensitivity to 
</p>
<p>radius [18]. Both a second stage concentric magnetic gear (CMG) gear ratio, if applicable, and the OSMGM’s gear 
</p>
<p>ratio are used to control the rotational speed of the sun gear and modulator of the OSMGM. Electrical frequency in 
</p>
<p>turn sets the sun gear pole count. Sun magnet, ring magnet, and modulator thickness are all controlled at the top level 
</p>
<p>of the optimization. OSMGM stator mass replaces motor mass since the stack length and mass of the magnetic gear 
</p>
<p>portion of the OSMGM is set by the required torque. Using stator mass as a genetic optimization variable allows the 
</p>
<p>stator to be designed to maximize efficiency and thermal performance within a fitness evaluation. Both ring magnet 
</p>
<p>and sun magnet temperatures are allowed to be optimized by the code. Internal airgap cooling and the added windage 
</p>
<p>losses to maintain magnet temperatures are accounted for as described in Section III B 4. 
</p>

<p>2. CMG Gearbox Stage 
</p>
<p>If a concentric magnetic gear stage is used before the OSMGM in a 2-stage drivetrain, a simple one-dimensional 
</p>
<p>correlation for gearbox mass and efficiency as a function of gear ratio is used to include CMG mass and efficiency in 
</p>
<p>the performance prediction for the drivetrain. A two-dimensional one like the one used for mechanical gearboxes is 
</p>
<p>not used due to the higher mass and higher efficiency of the magnetic gears per Section III-A-2 making it logical to 
</p>
<p>prioritize minimizing the mass of the magnetic gearbox at the cost of efficiency in these design studies.  
</p>
<p>The data used to create the correlation for gearbox mass and efficiency as a function of gear ratio are generated 
</p>
<p>using a genetic optimization of a concentric magnetic gear. The design flow within the genetic optimization fitness 
</p>
<p>evaluation is based off of the design code used in previous magnetic gear sizing studies [19]. Different from [19], the 
</p>
<p>code makes an approximate efficiency estimate for the gear based on static FEA (Figure 2) and ring gear back iron is 
</p>
<p>included in the geometry of the concentric magnetic gear to allow the use of a metal housing around the gear. The 
</p>
<p>bearings and shafts of the concentric magnetic gear are sized using the code from [15] so that propellor loads and 
</p>
<p>moments are accounted for in both mechanical and magnetic gearbox designs.  
</p>
<p>The loss estimation methodologies used in [14] and [12] are used to estimate modulator iron, ring gear magnet, 
</p>
<p>ring gear iron, and windage losses. Some estimates of the difference between these loss calculations and loss 
</p>
<p>calculations that used pseudo time stepping FEA can be found in [12] [13] [14]. Sun gear magnetic loss was neglected 
</p>
<p>in past work. As a simple approximation in the paper, sun gear magnet loss is estimated as 
</p>
<p>𝑆𝑢𝑛𝐿𝑜𝑠𝑠 = 𝑅𝑖𝑛𝑔𝐿𝑜𝑠𝑠 ∗ (𝐺𝑅 − 1)2 
</p>
<p>The magnets are assumed to be samarium cobalt operating at 160 C. All magnets are assumed to be composed of 
</p>
<p>1 mm laminations to suppress eddy current losses in the magnets. In the magnetic simulations of the gears, a 10% 
</p>
<p>reduction is applied to the remanent flux density of the magnets to account for loss of magnet fill due to the use of 
</p>
<p>laminations. </p>

<p>3. OSMGM Sizing 
</p>
<p>Static 2D FEA simulations are used to calculate the torque per meter of the magnetic gear portion of the OSMGM 
</p>
<p>and the torque per meter per amp of stator current of the motor portion of the OSMGM. An example geometry and 
</p>
<p>result for this simulation is shown in Figure 3. The torque-per-meter of the gear portion is used to define its stack 
</p>
<p>length based on the required torque. 3D leakage is accounted for in the magnetic gear torque prediction and in the 
</p>
<p>motor torque calculation using a correlation based on the data found in [20] for magnetic gear 2D to 3D torque 
</p>
<p>reductions.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Figure 3 Geometry and Example Result for Static 2D OSMGM FEA 
</p>
<p>The stator stack length is sized by extrapolating from the 2D FEA the stator and its heat sink performance vs stator 
</p>
<p>radial thickness, stator back iron thickness, stator tooth thickness, and heat sink fin height. Stack length for set 
</p>
<p>combinations of these variables is back calculated such that the stator mass is roughly equal to the stator mass selected 
</p>
<p>by the genetic optimizer for a given fitness evaluation. Losses and an analytical prediction of stator thermal 
</p>
<p>performance are calculated for each case. A stator design is then selected based on maximizing the thermal 
</p>
<p>performance of the OSMGM. 
</p>
<p>A parametric stator electrical current sweep for the selected geometry is carried out in static 2D FEA. Required 
</p>
<p>stator electrical current for each mission design point is defined. Static FEA is then carried out at each of these set 
</p>
<p>electrical current points to calculate both the fields in all components and the inductance of the stator coil. The field 
</p>
<p>data is used to create magnetic loss estimates for the various components at each mission design point using the 
</p>
<p>methodology from [13]. To capture an estimate of the sun gear iron and magnet loss caused by the ring gear, a static 
</p>
<p>FEA simulation is carried out with the sun gear magnetization set to zero. The field data in the sun magnets and the 
</p>
<p>sun gear back iron from this simulation is used to estimate the losses caused by ring gear magnetic flux. Stator </p>
<p></p>
</div>

</p>
<p>electrical current caused losses in the sun gear are neglected assuming that the ring gear generates the bulk of the loss 
</p>
<p>given its relative magnetic strength and high electrical frequency relative to the sun gear [18].   
</p>

<p>4. OSMGM Airgap Cooling Flow 
</p>
<p>In [14] [13] [12], the modulator of the magnetically geared motor was consistently found to reach high 
</p>
<p>temperatures when high fidelity thermal analysis was completed. To compensate for this and to allow magnet 
</p>
<p>temperature to be an optimization variable, air gap cooling flow was added to the machine designs. A parametric 
</p>
<p>sweep of volumetric flow rates was carried out in 3D thermal FEA to define the required volumetric flow rate of 
</p>
<p>coolant required to maintain target magnet and modulator temperatures in the OSMGM designs. The geometry of this 
</p>
<p>model is shown in Figure 4. Like in [12] and [14], the internal air is modeled as a solid body with high thermal 
</p>
<p>conductivity and thermal contact boundary conditions are used to represent the convective heat transfer between the 
</p>
<p>OSMGM components and the internal air.  
</p>
<p>From the analysis, magnet, and iron temperatures are extracted as a function of airgap flow and a required 
</p>
<p>volumetric flow rate of air is defined. Windage calculations are completed for this required axial flow rate of air and 
</p>
<p>incorporated into efficiency estimates for the machine. All subsequent thermal simulations of the machine include 
</p>
<p>boundary conditions with heat transfer coefficients corresponding to the required airgap cooling flow rate. Magnet 
</p>
<p>and Iron temperatures are checked after thermal transient FEA to verify that temperature limits for their respective 
</p>
<p>materials have not been exceeded.  
</p>

<p>III. Results 
</p>
<p>Design studies were completed for the NASA RVLT six passenger quad rotor reference vehicle. The assumed 
</p>
<p>mission profile for this vehicle is defined in Table 2. This is the same mission as was used in [7] and [8]. Design 
</p>
<p>optimizations are carried out for each drive topology at 400, 1000, 2000, and 3000 RPM. All designs are assumed to 
</p>
<p>operate at constant RPM throughout the mission profile. All magnetic gears are sized for 150% the peak nominal 
</p>
<p>mission power so that slip, and corresponding loss of control can be avoided. 50% margin may be a conservative 
</p>
<p>estimate in the general studies presented here. More complete knowledge of a vehicle and its required control response 
</p>
<p>could raise or lower this margin. Section III-A presents the mechanical gearbox and magnetic gearbox optimization 
</p>
<p>results used in subsequent optimizations of geared drivetrains. Section III-B presents results for full drivetrains at each 
</p>
<p>RPM. Section III-C presents results for reduced airgap and reduced margin magnetically geared drives and a 
</p>
<p>discussion of what would be needed to make the magnetically geared drives competitive with direct drive and 
</p>
<p>mechanically geared drives.

<p>A. Gearbox Results 
</p>
<p>1. Mechanical Gearboxes 
</p>
<p>Mechanical gearbox preoptimizations were carried out 400, 100, 2000, and 3000 RPM. In each case the gears were 
</p>
<p>sized for a life of 5000 hours with 99% reliability at peak mission torque (climb condition – 140 kW). Optimizations 
</p>
<p>were carried out for 3 types of gearboxes: 1 stage planetary, 2 stage planetary, and a planetary output stage plus a spur 
</p>
<p>gear input stage. The results of the optimizations are shown in Appendix A. Figures 5 thru 8 show the outputs of the 
</p>
<p>gearbox scattered interpolant functions at each RPM. Minimum gearbox mass is shown to decay with rotor RPM in 
</p>
<p>Figures 5-8 but with marginal returns corresponding to gearbox specific torque decaying with reduced gearbox torque 
</p>
<p>[15] [21]. Gear efficiency at a given motor RPM is shown to increase with propulsor rotor speed due to higher rotor 
</p>
<p>RPM resulting in less load on the gears and lower gear ratios. Gear mass is shown to stay relatively constant at gear 
</p>
<p>ratios greater than 5 in Figures 5-8 due to 2 stage gearboxes becoming optimum at those gear ratios and the second 
</p>
<p>stage being lower mass and having minimum impact on the overall gearbox mass.

<p>

<p>2. Magnetic Gearboxes. 
</p>
<p>Second stage magnetic gearbox optimizations were only completed for a 400 RPM propulsor rotor speed assuming 
</p>
<p>an OSMGM on its own, without a second stage CMG, would be optimum at rotor speeds at or above 1000 RPM since 
</p>
<p>optimum concentric magnetic gear (CMG) gear ratio is typically 5 or 6 [18]. CMGs were first optimized for a nominal 
</p>
<p>1 mm mechanical airgap and a 150% torque margin and then optimized with a 0.5 mm airgap and a 150% torque 
</p>
<p>margin. Appendix B provides the pareto front data at each gear ratio for these optimizations. There is some difficulty 
</p>
<p>in equating mechanical gearboxes designed for a life at peak nominal mission torque and magnetic gearboxes without 
</p>
<p>knowing the required control response for the aircraft and what the absolute maximum required torque is. Here 50% 
</p>
<p>is assumed to be a conservative estimate of what would be required 
</p>
<p>Figure 9 shows a mass comparison of the lightest magnetic gearboxes with both 1 mm and 0.5 mm airgaps 
</p>
<p>compared to the lightest mechanical gearboxes with 99% efficiency. Magnetic gears are shown to be 10 to 15 kg 
</p>
<p>heavier at a gear ratio of 3 depending on airgap than mechanical gears. The CMGs are shown to increase more 
</p>
<p>significantly in mass with gear ratio then the mechanical gears, but that is in part due to the CMGs only being single 
</p>
<p>stage gearboxes while the mechanical gearboxes are 2 stages above a gear ratio of 5. The 0.5 mm airgap designs 
</p>
<p>increase less significantly in mass with increased gear ratio due to the smaller airgap resulting in less pole-to-pole  
</p>
<p>leakage flux when ring gear pole count gets larger and pole to pole distance gets smaller.  
</p>
<p>Figures 10 and 11 show the curves used to define second stage magnetic gearbox mass and efficiency at 400 rpm 
</p>
<p>with 1.0 mm and 0.5 mm airgaps. The lightest design at each gear ratio from the data in Appendix B is used assuming 
</p>
<p>magnetic gears need to prioritize mass over their mechanical counterparts to be competitive. At low gear ratios, 
</p>
<p>magnetic gears are shown to be able to achieve efficiencies greater than 99.5% in both Figure 10 and 11 as well as the 
</p>
<p>appendix data at this RPM. This high efficiency is due to the low electrical frequency of the components relative to 
</p>
<p>one another at 400 RPM. Figure 12 shows how efficiency of the CMGs trend with the electrical frequency of the sun 
</p>
<p>gear relative to the ring gear.  
</p>

<p>B. Drivetrain Comparison  
</p>
<p>Figures 13 thru 16 show the best design of each topology at 400, 1000, 2000, and 3000 RPM rotor speed.  In all 
</p>
<p>cases, the mechanically geared drive is shown to be the lightest. Mechanically geared drivetrains’ mass benefit relative 
</p>
<p>to the direct drive and magnetically geared motor topologies is shown to reduce with increased RPM. Figure 17 shows 
</p>
<p>a mass comparison of each drivetrain topology’s minimum mass design. Mechanically geared motor drivetrains are 
</p>
<p>shown to have a 30 kg mass benefit at 400 RPM that decays to less than 5 kg at 3000 RPM. All three drives have 
</p>
<p>essentially the same mass at 3000 RPM. This reduction in gearbox benefit with increased rotor RPM trend is primarily 
</p>
<p>the result of gearbox specific torque decaying with reduced torque as shown in the data in Appendix A. Figure 18 
</p>
<p>shows how overall mechanically geared drivetrain mass primarily trends with the mass of the gearbox for all best 
</p>
<p>designs at all RPM. It also shows that the mass and performance of the other components in the drivetrain (inverter, 
</p>
<p>motor, and TMS) stay roughly constant with changes in rotor RPM as would be suspected. This result verifies that, in 
</p>
<p>the future, only a single full mechanically geared drivetrain study for a given application needs to be completed and 
</p>
<p>then the results can be extrapolated to other rotor RPM’s by updating the gearbox mass and efficiency  based on 
</p>
<p>reoptimizing the gearbox for different rotor speeds.

<p>IV. Conclusions and Future Work 
</p>
<p>In this paper, drivetrain topologies were explored for a 100 kW hover power quadrotor propulsor application. 
</p>
<p>Mechanically geared, direct drive, and magnetically geared drivetrain topologies were compared by optimizing them 
</p>
<p>for a given mission profile at different rotor RPM. Across the design space mechanically geared drives are shown to 
</p>
<p>achieve the lowest mass while direct drive drivetrains achieve the highest efficiencies. The magnetically geared 
</p>
<p>topologies were nominally not competitive in the design space, but that was in large part due to the assumption of a 
</p>
<p>50% torque margin requirement on nominal mission power for magnetic gears. Reduction of this margin to 10% or 
</p>
<p>the used of 0.5 mm airgaps in the magnetically geared drives did make them competitive in the design space. It should 
</p>
<p>be noted that these studies were completed for a specific power level, a specific application, and with a specific 
</p>
<p>assumed set of technologies and correspondingly do not provide general results that are applicable to all applications 
</p>
<p>and technologies. Future work in this area will: 
</p>
<p>1. Incorporate reliability models in the design optimization  
</p>
<p>2. Integration of explicit control response requirements to eliminate torque margin assumptions 
</p>
<p>3. Expand studies to different applications, technologies, and power levels 
</p>
<p>4. Explore other possible methods of improving magnetic gear performance beyond just reducing airgap 
</p>
