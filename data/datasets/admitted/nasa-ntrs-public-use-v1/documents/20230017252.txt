<p>Surface Instability of Liquid Propellants in 
</p>
<p>Microgravity During Pulsed Settling 
</p>
<p>Operations  
</p>
<p>Bryan D. Hoffman1, Marco D. Sansone1, and Jacob M. Brodnick2 
NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, Alabama, USA 
</p>
<p>Pulsing reaction control system (RCS) thrusters, vent valves, or other propulsion devices 
</p>
<p>can preserve propellant resources in space, but this operation also effectively introduces a 
</p>
<p>vibration to the vehicle. When the vibration is perpendicular to the liquid propellant surface, 
</p>
<p>Faraday waves may be generated at the liquid-vapor interface. These Faraday instabilities 
</p>
<p>can perturb or break up the liquid surface of cryogenic tanks, leading to inefficiencies in 
</p>
<p>thermal management or even ullage collapse. Drawing from theory and experiments, an 
</p>
<p>engineering model defining the allowable design regions for pulsed settling in microgravity 
</p>
<p>was assembled and verified with computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations. A 
</p>
<p>traditional settling metric, the Bond number, was also overlaid in the model to indicate which 
</p>
<p>duty cycles were insufficient to overcome surface tension and aggregate propellant. Mission 
</p>
<p>planners and engineers can consult the tool to rapidly evaluate the stability of a liquid-vapor 
</p>
<p>interface given the pulse frequency and the excitation acceleration. Expressions developed for 
</p>
<p>Faraday waves induced by a sinusoidal forcing input at standard gravity were found to 
</p>
<p>provide excellent predictive capabilities for pulsed, or rectangular, waveforms in the absence 
</p>
<p>of a consistent gravitational acceleration. This study extends the usage of these equations to 
</p>
<p>an alternative forcing function and microgravity environments for the purpose of estimating 
</p>
<p>natural frequencies, surface mode shapes, surface wave amplitudes, and the onset of droplet 
</p>
<p>ejection. CFD simulations with the Loci/STREAM-VoF (Volume of Fluid) solver were initially 
</p>
<p>validated against experimental results in standard gravity. Discrete points on the design map 
</p>
<p>were then investigated with CFD and confirmed that the engineering model reliably indicates 
</p>
<p>surface stability and most Faraday wave characteristics without requiring higher-fidelity 
</p>
<p>tools. The engineering model is highly extensible and can be adapted for various propellant 
</p>
<p>fill fractions, fluid properties, and tank sizes. 
</p>

<p>II. Introduction 
</p>
<p>In microgravity, liquid propellants tend to easily distribute throughout a tank, or unsettle, unless consistent 
</p>
<p>acceleration is applied. This behavior can be problematic for propulsion systems or propellant transfer between tanks 
</p>
<p>due to the risk of vapor ingestion in feed lines or liquid ingestion in vent lines. Unsettling can also submerge ullage 
</p>
<p>gas diffusers, hindering performance of pressurization systems not designed for that mode of operation. Moreover, 
</p>
<p>unsettling can lead to unintentional mixing between a cryogenic liquid and a relatively warm vapor volume above it, 
</p>
<p>known as the ullage. This mixing generates heat and mass transfer between the two phases and can lead to ullage 
</p>
<p>collapse, or the decline of pressure towards saturated conditions.  
</p>
<p>In architectures without propellant management devices, typical of tanks in large-scale orbital rockets, the 
</p>
<p>acceleration applied to aggregate liquid is commonly produced by reaction control system (RCS) thrusters. These 
</p>
<p>thrusters are allotted a budget of propellant mass for the purpose of settling cryogens. For thrusters with a fixed force 
</p>
<p>output, the amount of propellant mass expended and the average acceleration can be reduced by pulsing, or introducing 
</p>
<p>a time delay between thruster firings. Consequently, a range of average accelerations can be achieved by considering 
</p>
<p>the vehicle mass, the RCS thrust output, the pulse duration, and the duty cycle, or the ratio of the pulse duration to the 
</p>
<p>pulse period. Propulsion engineers are responsible for evaluating these parameters and selecting RCS hardware which 
</p>
<p>satisfy objectives for many mission phases, including for liquid aggregation.  
</p>
<p>A side effect of pulsing, however, is that a vibration is effectively introduced. Instabilities in liquids within a 
</p>
<p>vibrating container are well known. The polymathic physicist and chemist, Michael Faraday, recorded the first 
</p>
<p>observations in an appendix to his article to the Royal Society of London in 1831 [1]. He documented the observation 
</p>
<p>that liquid on a vibrating plate displayed regular patterns. Notably, he described changes in the behavior of these 
</p>
<p>patterns due to liquid properties, liquid depth, frequency, and force, but not to parameters such as surface size. He 
</p>
<p>even observed the primary ½-subharmonic response of the surface waves. As illustrated in Fig. 1, when the container 
</p>
<p>goes down, the liquid inertia tends to create a surface deformation which disappears when the surface comes back up, 
</p>
<p>completing the excitation period (𝑇). The flow induced from the destruction of the previous waves drives the following 
</p>
<p>waves, whose maxima occur at the location of the prior waves’ minima (and vice versa), making the surface wave 
</p>
<p>period (𝑇0) equal to two excitation periods. At high enough forcing inputs or low enough frequencies, the patterns 
</p>
<p>became chaotic, and splashing occurred. Droplet ejection could be problematic in space applications where ullage 
</p>
<p>collapse is a concern. Unlike experiments done in earth’s gravity well, the only significant acceleration propellants 
</p>
<p>experience in space may be from the vehicle’s thrusters. At very low frequencies, liquids inclined to wet surfaces may 
</p>
<p>climb the walls between pulses, exaggerating the unsettling effects. Therefore, it is necessary to understand the 
</p>
<p>constraints imposed on RCS thrusters by surface instabilities to adequately design settling operations.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fig. 1 For an excitation perpendicular to the liquid-vapor interface, the most visible liquid surface response 
</p>
<p>is at half the excitation frequency, known as the ½-subharmonic [2]. </p>

<p>III. Engineering Model 
</p>
<p>A. Theory 
</p>
<p>The Bond number (𝐵𝑜) is a well-known metric used to define the relative magnitudes of gravitational acceleration 
</p>
<p>(𝑔) and surface tension (𝜎) given the tank radius (𝑅), and the liquid density (𝜌). By selecting a target Bond number, 
</p>
<p>shown in Eq. (1), the metric provides a constraint on the minimum average acceleration designers must meet to 
</p>
<p>overcome surface tension in microgravity and aggregate liquid reliably. An analysis by Hochstein et al. in 1992 used 
</p>
<p>a 2D computational model with a Volume of Fluid (VoF) approach to evaluate the impact of Bond number on the 
</p>
<p>settling function, a correlation enabling prediction of pulsed settling performance for a wide range of tank sizes [3]. 
</p>
<p>They found that reorientation performance, or the efficiency of the settling operation, is similar for different pulse 
</p>
<p>patterns when the Bond number is greater than 10. As the Bond number was increased from 10 to 100, the model 
</p>
<p>results showed a very narrow range of variation in the settling function, indicating that there was a very strong 
</p>
<p>correlation between average acceleration and the vehicle velocity increment (delta-v), which is proportional to the 
</p>
<p>propellant used to accomplish the maneuver.

<p>Experiments have demonstrated that capillary waves experience predictable droplet ejection independent of the 
</p>
<p>gravitational acceleration. This feature has obvious benefits in low-gravity applications, where the surface tension 
</p>
<p>term would dominate. An example of this behavior is shown in Fig. 3. The sinusoidal excitation acceleration at which 
</p>
<p>droplet ejection occurs (𝑎𝑑) was empirically determined by Goodridge et al. [8]. Using ethanol, distilled water, and 
</p>
<p>glycerin-water solutions, they recorded the acceleration which produced two ejected droplets within a ten second 
</p>
<p>period. According to their research, the threshold is non-hysteretic for capillary waves, but it does vary by 5-10% for 
</p>
<p>gravity waves, depending on whether it is approached from higher or lower accelerations [9]. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Fig. 3 Droplet-ejecting waves in distilled water at 30 Hz [9]. 
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>maximum of 0.73 for capillary waves, exceeding the value of 0.142 for gravity waves [10]. Wave crests become self-
</p>
<p>intersecting at higher ratios, trapping vapor in the troughs. From observations, the wave amplitude for pure capillary 
</p>

<p>C. Design Map 
</p>
<p>The practical result of the collection of theory, empirical results, and analytical relationships is the pulsed 
</p>
<p>settling design map in Fig. 8. The map is based on the generic propellant tank and fluid properties presented in 
</p>
<p>section V. With this engineering model, designers can determine whether the operating condition that they have 
</p>
<p>selected is stable, subject to surface waves at the liquid-vapor interface, or unstable by plotting the pulse frequency 
</p>
<p>against the sinusoidal excitation acceleration. For convenience, the duty cycle can be overlaid on the y-axis using the 
</p>
<p>conversion in Eq. (21). The map assumes that a single pulse frequency is maintained and that the operation is 
</p>
<p>performed for a long period of time relative to the pulse period.  
</p>
<p>Since extreme ranges in duty cycle and frequency are possible, the map is plotted in a log-log scale. Outlined in 
</p>
<p>Fig. 7, the excitation acceleration is a function of the duty cycle and is also symmetric about the 50% value, so the 
</p>
<p>plot is split with the y-axis logarithmically approaching 100% duty cycle at the top and 0% duty cycle at the bottom. 
</p>
<p>It is expected that very low duty cycles are more common than very high ones. This is not only because a common 
</p>
<p>objective of pulsing is to save as much propellant mass as possible, but also because the areas of highest confidence 
</p>
<p>in the equations are where surface tension is much stronger than the average acceleration, falling to the right of the 
</p>
<p>gravity-capillary crossover curve.  
</p>
<p>The engineering model is divided by color into three regions based on the Faraday wave response. The red, 
</p>
<p>unstable region is characterized by liquid breakup and droplet ejection from the surface, with the boundary 
</p>
<p>determined by Eq. (15). To the right of this boundary – the yellow region – surface waves are predicted to occur at 
</p>
<p>the liquid surface. Wave amplitudes are relatively high near the droplet ejection threshold and decrease to the right, 
</p>
<p>as pulse frequencies increase. The transition from surface waves to a stable, unperturbed surface – the green region – 
</p>
<p>is delineated by the Faraday wave onset formula in Eq. (2).  </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>9 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The gravity-capillary crossover from Eq. (10) is also highlighted, marking the transition from gravity-dominated 
</p>
<p>waves (left) to waves dominated by surface tension (right). Since duty cycle and the average acceleration (effective 
</p>
<p>gravity) are coupled, it runs parallel to the droplet ejection threshold for most of the low duty cycles before deviating 
</p>
<p>around 50%. The deviation occurs because the average acceleration continues to go up with duty cycle while the 
</p>
<p>excitation acceleration trends back down. Note that the droplet ejection threshold is plotted up to 100% even though 
</p>
<p>it lies entirely in the gravity wave regime above 50%. The data points in Fig. 4 indicate there is some applicability of 
</p>
<p>this equation between the gravity-capillary breaking line and the crossover line. This relationship will break down 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

<p>A cross-hatched region at the bottom of the map shows the duty cycles at which the Bond number criterion has 
</p>

</p>
<p>number metric provides an understanding of whether liquid can be brought from an unsettled state to a settled state. 
</p>
<p>If the Bond number is too low, the duty cycle must be increased to overcome surface tension and move the liquid. 
</p>
<p>Ideally, 𝐵𝑜 ≫ 1, and the selection of the Bond number was discussed in the Theory portion of this section. A limit 
</p>

</p>
<p>have shown this value to prevent complete wetting at high fill levels. If a range of duty cycles are achievable after 
</p>
<p>consulting the design map, the time it takes to settle the propellant for a single operating condition can be predicted 
</p>
<p>with computational fluid dynamics (CFD). A tradeoff between the total time it takes to aggregate the liquid and the 
</p>
<p>amount of time the propulsive system is operating (e.g., RCS thrusters, propulsive vent system) can then be 
</p>
<p>evaluated for other duty cycles [12]. Designing a settling scheme or selecting hardware to operate in the stable 
</p>
<p>region is not always possible (nor essential, since wave amplitudes may be very low compared with the tank scale). 
</p>
<p>In cases where surface waves are predicted, the wavenumber/mode shape and wave amplitude can be estimated. 
</p>
<p>Verification and discussion of these predictions is addressed in section V.

<p>D. Surface Modal Responses 
</p>
<p>Surface waves produced by pulsed settling were evaluated for consistency with analytical equations. For a circular 
</p>
<p>cylinder, the wavenumber can be calculated with Eq. (13) while Eq. (12) can be used for a rectangular tank. Since the 
</p>
<p>wave frequency of the ½-subharmonic is half the pulse frequency, Eq. (8) is combined with the wavenumber equation 
</p>
<p>appropriate for the tank to estimate the mode shape. Two-dimensional rectangular tanks only have a single node count. 
</p>
<p>Cylindrical mode shapes, on the other hand, are expressed as (𝑛, 𝑚) pairs indicating the number of nodal diameters 
</p>
<p>and circles, respectively. Imaging of the first 50 modes in a cylindrical tank were documented by Shao, et al. [15].   
</p>
<p>Surface shapes may take various forms over time, exemplified by the sequence in Fig. 20. At the pulsing inception, 
</p>
<p>harmonic edge waves are propagated towards the center according to a (0, 21) or (0, 22) pattern for a driving frequency 
</p>
<p>of 0.3 Hz at 90% duty cycle. However, these waves yield to the dominant ½-subharmonic waves in part b) of the 
</p>
<p>image. A (0, 6) mode takes over as the most visible response, although a distinct high-order circumferential mode is 
</p>
<p>also observed at the same time. As the simulation continues to evolve in part c), mode mixing begins to distort and 
</p>
<p>warp the shapes into a mosaic of interfering patterns. 
</p>

</p>

<p>The estimation of wave amplitude may be valuable in a cryogenic tank for heat transfer modeling. The impact of 
</p>
<p>lateral slosh wave amplitude on pressure and temperature were explored by Ludwig, Dreyer, and Hopfinger [16] via 
</p>
<p>a sloshing Reynolds number and Nusselt number. Although it is not the same manner of motion, Faraday waves and 
</p>
<p>their disruption of the thermal stratification around the interface could potentially be modeled in kind.  </p>

</p>
<p>amplitude of the sinusoidal displacement function. For the sinusoidal acceleration in Eq. (20), the double integral is 
</p>
<p>the sinusoidal displacement (𝑎𝑠𝑖𝑛𝑒/𝜔
2). Goodridge observed that capillary waves respond linearly according to Eq. 
</p>
<p>(14). Using this relationship for wave amplitude entails that the ratio of wave amplitude to wavelength (rearrange Eq. 
</p>
<p>(8) and Eq. (11)) is unity at the droplet ejection threshold, consistent with their initial assumptions. Jiang, Perlin, and 
</p>
<p>Schultz also documented a linear movement of wave amplitude with the sinusoidal displacement amplitude, albeit at 
</p>
<p>a much lower rate [17]. Once droplet ejection began, they measured wave amplitudes at substantially higher values.

<p>Wave amplitudes in the propellant tank were measured by the maximum and minimum values of the VoF = 0.5 
</p>
<p>(cells with 50% liquid in them) iso-surface within the inner 80% radius of the tank. Using a clipped radius removes 
</p>
<p>the meniscus from the calculation, which can extend much higher than the waves oscillate. The range is then divided 
</p>
<p>in half to obtain the amplitude of the surface waves. As Fig. 7 showed, the wave amplitudes can vary with time, so an 
</p>
<p>average of the last twenty peak amplitudes was taken. A composite of the 3D cylindrical tank and 2D rectangular tank 
</p>
<p>cases is given in Fig. 23. Regression lines are overlaid with the y-intercept forced through the (0, 0) point. The data

</p>
<p>et al. were referring to breaking or non-breaking capillary waves when their observations confirmed a slope of 47.

<p>VI. Conclusion 
</p>
<p>An engineering model for assessing the stability of a liquid surface in microgravity which is subject to pulsed 
</p>
<p>accelerations perpendicular to the interface was compiled and verified with CFD. Validation of the CFD solver against 
</p>
<p>experimental data in standard gravity provided confidence in the tool’s ability to forecast surface behavior in 
</p>
<p>conditions where experimental data is sparse. The engineering model relies on empirical and theoretical formulae 
</p>
<p>developed for sinusoidal forcing. Consequently, a method for converting the pulsed forcing waveform into an 
</p>
<p>equivalent sinusoidal wave amplitude was proposed. Several methods were considered, included conserving the 
</p>
<p>average power of the wave, but the recommended method of using the first harmonic resulted in good agreement. 
</p>
<p>Predictions made using the pulsed settling engineering model are in good agreement with CFD solutions showing 
</p>
<p>droplet breakup and surface wave characteristics such as the subharmonic frequency response and mode shape. Wave 
</p>
<p>amplitude is more complex to determine accurately, but scaled functions can serve as rules of thumb if estimates are 
</p>
<p>needed. The Faraday wave onset criterion was not scrutinized closely like other characteristics presented here. 
</p>
<p>Computationally, very small cell sizes are required to resolve the waves at those frequencies and acceleration levels, 
</p>
<p>requiring larger meshes and time. Practically, the risk of ullage collapse should be very low around this boundary, so 
</p>
<p>the threshold should largely serve as a helpful target for designers who are deciding how much margin to build into 
</p>
<p>their systems. </p>
