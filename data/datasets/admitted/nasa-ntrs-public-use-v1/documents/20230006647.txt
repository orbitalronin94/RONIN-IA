The National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) aims to land crew on the lunar surface to 
establish a sustainable presence and develop operational concepts for future long-duration missions. New 
technologies will be necessary to extend planning and execution capabilities for lunar surface activities. NASA’s 
Joint Augmented Reality Visual Informatics System (Joint AR) is one such technology. Joint AR is a suit-
mounted augmented reality (AR) display and computes system which facilitates unprecedented information 
exchange and data visualization capabilities between mission support operators and suited crew. This paper 
describes challenges associated with developing AR technology for an envisioned work domain by applying a 
sociotechnical lens to the iterative testing and development of novel AR technology through virtual reality (VR). 
A VR testbed was established to simulate a representative lunar surface environment, enabling a series of three 
human-in-the-loop (HITL) tests evaluating AR navigation interfaces for exploration extravehicular activity 
(xEVA). Our findings identify several considerations for future Joint AR design and testing efforts, including 
challenges with data overload, attentional demands, and environment-related perceptual challenges. Trade-
offs and potential approaches are discussed to mitigate these challenges and improve future Joint AR testing 
fidelity.

I. Introduction 
ASA’s Artemis missions seek to establish a sustainable human presence on the moon and test future concepts 
for increasingly Earth-independent planetary exploration. One primary goal of future lunar missions is increased 



crew autonomy, meaning independence from Earth-based support in favor of crew self-reliance for operational 
decision-making using local assets and resources. Notably, this goal constitutes a substantial transformation of the 
work domain, as existing and historical extravehicular activities are heavily supported by ground-based mission-
support personnel, who hold virtually all authority and responsibility for operational decision-making.1 Achieving 
increased crew autonomy will require new technologies and operational concepts to facilitate information exchange 
and support intra-crew decision-making during exploration extravehicular activity (xEVA) and other activities. 
 One technology addressing this gap is the Joint Augmented Reality Visual Informatics System (Joint AR), a suit-
mounted AR display and compute system enabling new information exchange capabilities between suited crew and 
mission support operators.2 Joint AR provides a visual medium for exchanging and visualizing mission-relevant data, 
with envisioned applications including life-support consumable monitoring, dynamic procedure updates, and 
navigation support during xEVA. While new technologies such as Joint AR will be necessary to support this 
transformation of work, technology alone will be insufficient to cope with the demands of crew-based operational 
decision-making. As a result, future mission concepts and systems must be developed with intimate knowledge of 
these new demands, explicitly supporting new capabilities and coordinative roles for both crew and ground-based 
mission support operators. Notably, these capabilities and roles are one piece of a broader sociotechnical system, 
wherein practitioners coordinate (e.g., sharing information, recruiting resources, delegating tasks), use technological 
tools (e.g., to monitor the state of the environment), and apply their expertise adaptively to ensure mission success.3,4 
However, it is difficult to assess how a technological tool may fit into an emerging work domain.5–7 Interdisciplinary 
testing methods are necessary to explore developing technology within a complex, uncertain, and rapidly evolving 
work domain. 
 This paper addresses challenges associated with envisioning technology for an emerging work domain, using VR 
to explore AR support interfaces for lunar surface navigation. An iterative sequence of three human-in-the-loop 
(HITL) experiments tasked participants to use a suit-mounted AR display to navigate to predetermined waypoints on 
a representative lunar surface environment. These tests occurred in tandem with Joint AR system development and 
were performed relatively early to support technology exploration. For this reason, testing simultaneously informed 
feature capabilities for future evaluation. Opportunistic, bootstrapping knowledge captures techniques drawn from 
ethnographic research4,8 and cognitive systems engineering methods9,10 were employed in testbed creation and analysis 
of experiment results, prioritizing external validity, functional prototypes, and meaningful insights over internal 
validity or statistical significance. VR was chosen as a sandbox to 1) obtain higher ecological validity of the experience 
of navigating a distant planetary body and 2) simulate and explore technologies and system architectures which do 
not yet exist (and/or are prohibitively expensive to develop in the real-world). By leveraging VR to explore notional 
AR navigation tools in a constrained lunar xEVA use case, challenges, strengths, and limitations are identified, early 
in the development cycle to help inform further maturation of the real-world Joint AR system.

II. Background 



A. Joint AR 
Joint AR is a spacesuit-compatible augmented reality display and controls system enabling novel data visualization 



and coordination capabilities during xEVA. Anticipated benefits include novel communication modalities between 
crew and ground support operators, improved intra-crew decision making for future long-distance human exploration 
missions, and asynchronous coordination with ground-based operators. As future Artemis xEVA workflows leverage 
new technologies, multiple data streams, Joint AR aims to provide the ability to superimpose information and visual 
cues upon the environment. As one of multiple tech-centric initiatives to reach stated goals of increased crew 
autonomy, Joint AR is uniquely situated in its ability to increase the capacity of crew to make informed decisions 
during future xEVA.2 



Although the hardware and optical requirements of Joint AR are one driving factor of its development, Joint AR 
must also be cognizant of broader work system it exists within, including multiple spatially and temporally distributed 
agents with common goals (i.e., suited crew and ground-based mission support operators) as well as a highly 
constrained, uncertain, and complex environment. While registered AR visualizations are expected to be a major 
strength of AR technology for future xEVA, little is understood surrounding desirable mechanisms for decision-
support and potential coordination between suited crew and ground. 



B. Augmented Reality within Virtual Reality 
Overview and Prior Applications 
 Simulations are commonly employed to assess the effectiveness of a new technology. Domains including air traffic 
control, military operations, and space operations commonly empoy this technique. These human-in-the-loop tests 
(HITL) seek to assess human behavior and interactions with the environment by placing participants in central roles 
within the staged environment. Staged worlds can provide valuable situational knowledge and context, prioritizing 
external validity and real-world applicability. While simplistic staged worlds are somewhat contrived, high-fidelity 
simulations can be used to approximate contextually rich challenges and work scenarios, testing the robustness and 
resilience of the system. By creating a staged world simulation early into the technology development cycle, 
assumptions about the system and broader work environment are often revealed, promoting positive iterations and 
questions for further research.5,11 



Virtual reality (VR) is a term describing a wide variety of immersive, computer simulated 3D environments. 
Computer Aided Design (CAD) software is a frequently used to design 3D worlds, allowing a nearly infinite 
sandbox.12 VR has been deployed in a multitude of domains including healthcare/surgery training, air traffic control, 
emergency response, industrial process control, and space operations.1,13–16 Applications of VR at NASA JSC date as 
early as the 1990s, with later applications including crew training for STS-61 deployment of the Hubble Space 
Telescope, involving complex human-robot choreography including an extravehicular crew member and Shuttle 
robotic arm.12 VR is a highly visual experience and applications frequently include a variety of sensory modalities to 
increase immersion. This makes VR well suited to replicate the experience of traversing unique lunar landscapes with 
high contrast lighting conditions. Augmented reality (AR) is related technology allowing digital content to be 
superimposed upon the environment. AR has been deployed as a training tool for deep-sea diving navigation,17 surgery 
and medical displays,18 military operations,19 and driver heads-up-displays20. 



Fully configurable VR environments allow simulation of AR displays by superimposing digital content on the 
environment. One advantage of using VR to simulate AR technology is the ability to quickly test theoretical and 
envisioned systems without undergoing traditional technology research and development processes. While AR 
technologies at a minimum involve producing digital objects in real world (or virtual) scenes, “registered” AR 
describes an advanced application of AR where sensor systems are used to sense the surrounding environment and 
precisely align virtual assets to the environment in contextually meaningful ways. For example, light detection and 
ranging (LiDAR) sensors can be used to gain a high-resolution digital model of the real world and, if paired with 
computer vision, enable object recognition capabilities and precise registration of digital content with a real world 
object. A registered AR system could provide information about distances from mission-relevant locations or provide 
suggestions about which terrain may be hazardous to navigate upon which precisely located digital assets can be 
overlaid in the real world. In a VR environment, registered AR components are easily simulated, since the controlled 
nature of the scenes can be directly mapped to inform data shown on AR display elements.

Joint AR Virtual Testbed Overview 
 The Joint AR Virtual Testbed was developed with Unreal Engine 5 (UE5), with custom functionalities 



implemented for testing AR in VR. This included 1) incorporating dynamic eye adaptation by subjectively matching 
real-world legibility on lit surfaces, 2) leveraging the Scientific Hybrid Reality Environment (SHyRE) project’s high-
resolution, scientifically relevant scene21,22 augmented with a lunar South pole lighting environment and 3) a Hard 
Upper Torso reference design including representative helmet lighting and helmet bubble. Assets chosen for 
incorporation into the virtual testbed are all elements which affect the legibility or content of the AR experience. 
Testbed equipment included the VIVE Pro 2 Virtual Reality Headset, two VIVE Lightbox Sensors, two VIVE Pro 
Controllers, and a PC with NVIDIA GeForce 2080 Ti GPU. Additionally, the UE5 engine had data output capabilities, 
automatically saving each experiment instance into a comma separated values file with timestamp, location 
coordinates, and other data relevant for analysis purposes. 
 The display engine being developed for the real-world AR system, The Space Technology Application Rendering 
Kit (STARK), was integrated with UE5 to enable real-time rapid prototyping of the real user interface without 
requiring access to real-world optical hardware technology.2 The STARK engine rendered its content live on the 
desktop in a separate window, while an open source UE5 plugin projected content onto the virtual helmet bubble. 
While this application ran on a powerful Windows PC, the display engine can also cross-compile to an embedded 
platform. This means that UIs created for the testbed are generated using a display engine specifically designed for 
the performance-restricted lunar environment. Figure 1 shows a first-person perspective view of the STARK 
displaying a UI superimposed upon the VR environment.

Three HITL tests were conducted, beginning with an initial version (1.0) followed by two iterations (1.5, 2.0).

incremental approach prioritized opportunistic knowledge capture and rapid iteration over strict experimental control 
and internal validity. In other words, discovering the proper measures, metrics, and work contexts was part of the 
experimental process and intended outcomes. Broad goals included building the capacity to make comparisons and 
identify areas for further specification, with three specific HITL test configurations performed over ten months, each 
designed to enable opportunistic, bootstrapping knowledge capture techniques through rapid development of testable

Environment 
 The experiment’s VR environment was representative of lunar surface conditions, leveraging the SHyRE terrain 
to provide a geologically and illumination-relevant backdrop for evaluations, as both the shadows and highlights 
impact display legibility. The terrain was paired with a realistically low sun angle casting shadows equivalent to 
expected lunar conditions for upcoming Artemis endeavors. Destinations were selected based on their diversity of 
terrain, distance, and directional challenge from the lander. Notably, surnames were used for destination titles during 
the experiment, but changed to alphabetic monikers for presentation in this paper. Mission elements such as slope of 
terrain and varied traverse speed were excluded from this analysis, but closely observed to inform future traverse 
simulation. 



 While some sloped areas of terrain would pose challenges in real xEVA navigation scenarios, physical hazards 
were not included in the scope of this work. As a result, no penalties were incurred for walking over sloped terrain 
that may be undesirable for real xEVA activities. This was ultimately chosen to maintain focus on the effectiveness 
of the presented AR displays, with plans for future testing to include negative penalties for traversing hazardous terrain 
such as slowed movement speed. 



D. Simple Navigation (1.0 and 1.5) 
Version-Specific Goals 



Simple Navigation (Simple Nav)’s primary goal was testing if participants could use an AR display for a minimum 
viable ‘point-to-point’ navigation system in a representative a lunar-surface environment. This took place through two 
versions, (1.0) and (1.5). Version 1.0 aimed to deliver a proof of concept for using AR for navigation in a basic xEVA 
VR environment. Additionally, 1.0 sought to identify potential bugs and explore the effectiveness of the Compass user 
interface through quantitative and qualitative data capture. Version 1.5 offered improvements to bugs identified in 1.0, 
fixing UI visibility issues faced by participants in certain conditions. 1.5 shared primary goals with 1.0, with the 
additional goal of a smoother experience and capturing more conclusive results. 
 
Experimental Design 
 The simple navigation experiment featured a within-subjects design, with the key independent variable being 
traverse destination. The experiment featured four different destinations ranging from 140 to 157 meters from the 
starting position. These destinations were presented in random order based a 4x4 Latin square. Destinations were 
selected to provide directional variation and terrain. Due to varying participant counts for each experimental version, 
not all Latin square conditions were tested with an equal number of participants. 



Simple navigation (1.0 and 1.5) employed identical metrics, with dependent variables including completion time 
to reach destination and final distance from destination. Participant traverses were timed from start to finish, which 
the participant indicated by pressing and holding triggers on the controller until haptic feedback was generated. The 
UE5 engine generated data on the exact location the participants finished at, as well as their path from the starting 
point to the goal. A derived measure of mission success/failure was also measured, considered the mission successful 
if participants completed the task within 2 meters of the destination point. Participant traverse paths were also captured 
through the UE5 engine, allowing later analysis of how efficient the traverse path was from start point to goal. 



Qualitative data was collected through self-surveys administered at the conclusion of the last run. Participants were 
asked to provide information on how easy/difficult they felt the navigation was, as well as questions such as: 



• Did you feel that the navigation user interface helped guide you to the task? (Yes or no and why?) 
• Were there situations when the Nav UI was confusing or unhelpful towards your destination? When? 
• Was the Nav UI useful for different destinations? Which destination? 



 
Features and UI 
 Simple Navigation utilized the same features and UI assets between version 1.0 and 1.5, consisting of three 
dynamic components: bearing, distance to goal in meters, and navigation crossbar/chevron. Bearing showed the 
participants orientation 0-360 degrees, updating as they changed directions. Together, these components are referred 
to as the ‘Compass’.

Test Methodology and Procedure 
 Simple Navigation (1.0 and 1.5) tasked participants using the Compass tool (including a 0-360 degree bearing, 



crossbar and chevron, and distance in meters) to navigate from a lander to one destination. Before entering the VR 
environment, participants were familiarized with the controller and headset, including adjustment straps to ensure 
proper fit. Upon entering the environment, participants were trained to successfully use controllers for movement and 
verified the contents of heads-in-display (HUD) were appearing correctly through reading them aloud. Participants 
completed two practice traverses to further familiarize themselves with the Compass display. Participants were also 
trained to press and held both triggers on the HTC VIVE controller to indicate the start and conclusion of each run. 



After completing training cases, participants were given an opportunity to ask clarifying questions before 
beginning the four measurement runs. For measurement runs, participants were loaded into a starting point positioned 
next to a lunar lander. They were instructed to press and hold both triggers and begin the task when ready. Upon 
completion of one traverse, participants were loaded into the next version of the simulation, starting at the same point, 
but with a different destination. Measurement runs for Navigation 1.0 had no visible markers or names for participants, 
but are displayed as Mercury, Gemini, Apollo, and Voyager in the presented analysis. 



E. Waypoint Navigation (2.0) 
Experimental Design 



Participants performed up to six total experimental runs, with three training runs (one for each tool variation) 
beforehand. Dependent variables included accuracy to waypoints and completion time for both task segments and the 
entire sequence. 
 
Features and UI 



The navigation UI for version 2.0 had key differences including the introduction of registered AR features and 
varied combinations of UI features for certain goals. The registered AR feature included a suggested footpath towards 
the objective, a flag with objective title indicating the destination, and a disc with 2-meter circumference highlighting 
the objective area.

Test Methodology and Procedure 
Waypoint Navigation introduces independent variables and experimental procedure. The key independent 



variables for this version were destination and tools used. The experiment involved three tool use variations, also 
shown in the Waypoint Navigation (2.0) Features and UI section above: 



• AR navigation system only (referred to as AR system) 
• Compass only (navigation bar, chevron, bearing, and arrows) 
• Both (AR navigation system and Compass) 



 Participants used each UI configuration one at a time to assist in navigating from a lunar lander to multiple 
sequential destinations. After completing two training runs, participants were introduced to three UI variations within 
the VR environment: the AR only, the Compass, and Both. Within each variation, there were two different length 
runs, a short sequence of three waypoints and a longer sequence with five waypoints. In total, each participant went 
through two training and six complete runs. The paths and destinations were sampled based on uniqueness and variety, 
with Sun angle alternating between each cardinal direction (NSEW) to test multiple lighting conditions. No waypoints 
were shared between the runs. The order of the runs was determined by a Latin square matrix to randomize runs 
between participants.

V. Discussion 



H. Overview 
 This research used VR to test multiple registered AR interface concepts for xEVA navigation. The paper showcases 
iterative testing of interaction technology early in the development cycle, gaining opportunistic insights and empirical 
evidence to inform future development efforts. While multiple UI design concepts were tested, variations appear to 
have negligible impacts on overall performance, as a large majority of participants were able to successfully reach the 
destination despite the UI variation used. Participant success was generally anticipated due to the simplicity of the 
task, future testing may require more nuanced measures of success. Additional metrics could include more 
sophisticated success measures than requiring proximity within 2 meters and could use top-down path characteristics 
(as shown by Figure 7) to evaluate performance. Efficiency and success avoiding non-traversable terrain could be 
additional measures for a more advanced navigation study. Likewise, future measures may be more closely tied to 
operational goals rather than specific destination points. 
 Quantitative results from version 2.0 indicated similar outcomes to 1.5, showing that the navigation systems tested 
were mostly effective at allowing participants to reach the destination. Qualitative results from Waypoint Navigation 
(2.0) yielded interesting insights into particularly challenging aspects of developing registered AR data visualizations. 
One of the most interesting findings was that participants found the number of flags, markers, and on-screen items for 
the ‘AR only’ and ‘Both’ systems to be cumbersome. For example, named markers sometimes overlapped with each 
other and became difficult to read. This was exacerbated when participants used the AR and Compass (both) UI. This 
finding highlighted that future interface testing could explore using a variety of visual properties to convey meaning, 
such as opacity and color. 
 Other qualitative results (see Table 3, Table 4) related to important design tradeoffs, such as a widely favored 
feature of the AR system being the dotted-line footpath superimposed on the ground, and the Compass feature being 
perceived as a familiar and reliable tool for navigation. This indicates the need and value of simple, functional 
navigation tools as a gestalt to more complex, algorithmically generated footpaths projected by the AR system. This 
is important as providing functional and reliable alternatives to sophisticated navigation techniques reduces the 
likelihood of overreliance on complex sensor suites and algorithms and that may fail in challenging, off-nominal 
scenarios. 



I. Key Observations and Challenges 
 Display-related perceptual challenges (for example: attentional demand and salience of display) were observed in 
the results. One recurring challenge of using the AR system was data overload in the form of display clutter. Figure 3 
showcases an example of this, combining the AR and Compass UI configurations. Aside from the known redundancy 
of meters from distance being indicated in multiple places, one issue identified by participants was waypoints 
appearing the same visually regardless of if they had been visited or not. This made orienteering slightly more 
challenging when in the middle of multiple sequential waypoints. Additionally, iteration between Simple Navigation

versions (1.0 and 1.5) introduced an interesting element of design and deployment for lunar AR displays: visual 
redundancy to help mitigate high contrast lighting conditions. 
 While high visual contrast of the lunar surface has been speculated to pose visibility risks for an AR display as 
discussed in previous Joint AR literature2, the results empirically validated this concern in the VR environment through 
qualitative survey results and direct observations. During the 1.0 test, participant AR displays were reported as difficult 
to see when superimposed upon a brightly lit background or other occlusion. This was identified to be primarily due 
to a bug with UE5 eye adaptation in certain sun angle conditions. However, even when this bug did not occur, the 
high contrast environment of the lunar surface could make the display difficult to read in certain surroundings. When 
participants experienced this they often adapted by utilizing elements of the display that were not visually obscured 
due to the high contrast background. This behavior suggests that redundant visual cues at multiple positions can help 
deliver important information even when all parts of the UI are not visible. Specific visual features used by participants 
in fashion way included the navigation bar and chevron, which had their brightness increased when facing the correct 
direction. 
 Another interesting finding was that participants using the Compass UI noted it as useful for initial orienteering. 
This makes sense, as the Compass supported directional (left to right) awareness relative to the goal. Once oriented 
correctly, however, participants claimed the Compass was less useful for precise wayfinding. One interesting pattern 
was the Compass causing confusion when participants went several meters past the goal point on accident, as the 
Compass rapidly turned around to direct them back towards the goal. 
 While the constrained VR environment allowed high-fidelity simulation of registered AR capabilities, real world 
operations equivalent of the presented experiences with registered AR displays would depend on data gathered by 
sensor systems and interpreted by computer algorithms. One assumption of the VR scenario is that this collection and 
interpretation of data demonstrated perfect accuracy in registered AR content. This is a generous representation of 
performance compared to the real world, as sensor platforms using computer vision algorithms tend to underperform 
when deployed in field settings.23 The implementation of registered AR features in this paper did not make any 
presumption of real-world sensing capabilities. This highlights a need for caution surrounding assumptions of future 
technological capabilities. Further research could seek to assess how the Joint AR system influences decision making 
when certain cues are missed, incomplete, or wrong. Testing how prototyped systems respond to these challenging 
cases will support developing a system capable of accommodating a variety of nominal and off nominal xEVA 
scenarios. 
 
Design Considerations for Future xEVA AR Displays 
 The study’s findings highlight the benefits of testing interaction technology early in the development cycle. Using 
VR to test AR technology allowed for rapid prototyping and evaluation. The findings also identified potential risks of 
data overload when using registered AR displays, illustrating a need for thorough and traceable representational design 
processes for interface components. Due to the iterative nature and necessity of regular prototyping when developing 
a first-of-a-kind system such as Joint AR, clear articulation of assumptions for test elements and UI design will 
reinforce the interconnected and interdependent nature of the xEVA system that tools are being created to support. 
Techniques from CSE could support development of rigid, traceable engineering design specifications for UI.9,10,24 
 Improvements for future iterations could include varied opacity or color for specific waypoints, potentially 
changing as the task is incrementally completed. For example, an upcoming waypoint could be labeled orange, rather 
than the final goal, and previously visited waypoints could be set to 50% opacity, making them less salient. Specific 
operational demands could influence the strategy deployed for this, as the previously described example would need 
altered for an xEVA that involved returning to a previously visited destination. 



VI. Conclusion 
A series of HITL experiments were conducted to explore AR heads-in-displays with dynamic content during a 



simulated lunar surface EVA operations. A fully immersive VR representation of the lunar landscape provided a 
configurable environment to test potential system configurations and features. Results indicated that AR navigation 
support displays improved performance during lunar navigation tasks. Broader design approaches were presented as 
an approach to coping with complexity inherent in developing novel technology for envisioned work domains. 



Joint AR will be necessary to improve operational performance amid data overload conditions for future xEVA 
but there still remains significant design challenges and pitfalls to create an effective, powerful visualization tool. 
However, to avoid pitfalls, further specification of engineering design processes for attentional demands and 
perceptual cues must be carefully developed. This work offers potential at presenting a rare opportunity to create a
