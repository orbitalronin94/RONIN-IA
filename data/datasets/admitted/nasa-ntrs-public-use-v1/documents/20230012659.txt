<p>In-Space Transportation Sensitivity to Roundtrip Mission
</p>
<p>Duration and Mars Vicinity Stay Time
</p>
<p>Patrick R. Chai∗, Andrew J. Sais†, Nicholas M. Gaug‡
</p>
<p>NASA Langley Research Center, Hampton, VA, 23601 USA
</p>
<p>Min Qu§

</p>
<p>The National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s Exploration Systems Development Mis-
</p>
<p>sion Directorate has been developing architecture concepts for human missions to Mars in
</p>
<p>alignment with the agency’s Moon-to-Mars Strategy &amp; Objectives. One of the key components
</p>
<p>of a human Mars mission is the in-space transportation system that delivers crew and cargo to
</p>
<p>Mars vicinity and returns the crew safely back to Earth. The Mars Architecture Team within
</p>
<p>ESDMD’s Strategy and Architecture Office has been evaluating multiple in-space transporta-
</p>
<p>tion options to satisfy this functional need. The first step of this evaluation is to understand
</p>
<p>key architectural drivers for in-space transportation systems. Lunar and Mars missions have
</p>
<p>unique challenges, and systems designed for one may not be directly applicable to the other as
</p>
<p>they have different energy and mission needs. In addition, the time and distance for a Mars
</p>
<p>mission will be drastically different from the historical and current programs, requiring a new
</p>
<p>paradigm for mission architects. This paper investigates the impact of mission duration on
</p>
<p>a variety of different transportation options. As mission duration decreases, the total energy
</p>
<p>required to perform a roundtrip Mars mission increases exponentially, significantly increasing
</p>
<p>the required Earth departure mass. Sensitivity analyses of various in-space transportation
</p>
<p>system concepts were shown in this paper to begin mapping out the in-space transportation
</p>
<p>trade space. Each of the transportation systems has its own strengths and weaknesses, es-
</p>
<p>pecially in the context of the continuous mission duration and vicinity stay time trade space.
</p>
<p>Although mission duration and Mars vicinity stay time may be key architecture drivers from
</p>
<p>an in-space transportation perspective, the decision of acceptable mission duration cannot be
</p>
<p>made solely from this perspective. Mission duration also has tangible impacts to crew health
</p>
<p>and performance, as well as system reliability and other key architecture considerations. The
</p>
<p>integrated nature of the architecture decision road map will be the primary focus of the Mars
</p>
<p>Architecture Team in the coming analysis cycle. This in-space transportation analysis will serve
</p>
<p>as the first guidepost of this critical activity that will guide the Moon-to-Mars Architecture into
</p>
<p>the humans to Mars segment.
</p>

</p>

</p>

<p>both lunar and Mars missions and to develop functional needs to enable an integrated end-to-end mission architecture.
</p>

</p>

<p>one part of the architecture will ripple through other parts of the architecture, it is critically important that the agency

<p>One of the key components of a human Mars mission is the in-space transportation system that delivers crew and
</p>
<p>cargo to Mars vicinity and returns the crew safely back to Earth, as specified by multiple Moon-to-Mars Objectives
</p>
<p>and Recurring Tenets[1]. The Mars Architecture Team within ESDMD’s Strategy and Architecture Office has been
</p>
<p>evaluating four in-space transportation options to satisfy this functional need: 1) Hybrid Nuclear Electric Propulsion
</p>
<p>(NEP)/ Chemical Propulsion, 2) Nuclear Thermal Propulsion (NTP), 3) Hybrid Solar Electric Propulsion (SEP)/
</p>
<p>Chemical Propulsion, and 4) All-Chemical Propulsion. To address the question “which transportation option is best?”
</p>
<p>the architecture team needs to understand the performance characteristics—and limitations—for each transportation
</p>
<p>option. Each option could be considered “better” than the others, depending on the primary selection criteria and the
</p>
<p>order of the integrated decisions that are made.
</p>
<p>II. Interplanetary Transits between Earth and Mars
In human Mars mission architecture discussions it is helpful to keep in mind that distances traveled will be at a
</p>
<p>scale far beyond the entirety of human space flight experience to date. A single roundtrip journey between Earth and
</p>
<p>Mars will put about 1.8 to 2 billion kilometers on a Mars transportation system’s odometer, regardless of departure
</p>
<p>opportunity or trajectory. To put this distance in context, for the recent Artemis I mission between Earth and the Moon,
</p>
<p>the Orion spacecraft traveled only 2.2 million kilometers[2], or roughly 1/1000th the distance for a roundtrip Mars
</p>
<p>mission. The distance that the Moon circles Earth only varies by about 43,000 kilometers over time, so it always takes
</p>
<p>about the same amount of energy to travel to the Moon and back no matter the mission cadence. By contrast, the
</p>
<p>distance between Earth and Mars can vary by as much as 340 million kilometers as the two planets orbit the Sun. The
</p>
<p>closest Mars ever approaches Earth is 54.6 million kilometers; at their farthest, over 400 million kilometers of deep
</p>
<p>space separates the two planets.
</p>
<p>In addition, lunar and Mars missions have unique challenges, and systems designed for one destination may not be
</p>
<p>directly applicable to the other as they have different energy and mission needs. Missions to Mars have higher energy
</p>
<p>need, require much longer system service life, and have more stringent departure window constraints. This higher
</p>
<p>energy need is evident in Fig. 1, which shows an Earth-Moon-Mars energy map for missions to the surface of the Moon

<p>To that end, recent analysis was designed to explore the pros and cons of different transportation system options
</p>
<p>across a wider range of mission profiles than previously considered[3]. The initial metric of interest for recent
</p>
<p>assessments was total roundtrip mission duration, due to the significant duration-related flow-down impacts to crew
</p>
<p>health and performance[4], technology investment, development timelines, and cost. Interplanetary transportation
</p>
<p>energy is a function of many variables, including the distance and relative velocity of the planets as well as the mission
</p>
<p>duration and orbital stay time. This relationship between the energy required as a function of total mission duration
</p>
<p>and Mars vicinity stay time is shown in Fig. 2. For this particular analysis, Lunar Distance High Earth Orbit (LDHEO)
</p>
<p>is used as the starting point for Earth departure staging for Mars missions, and a 5-sol orbit is used for Mars arrival
</p>
<p>staging and departure, more energy will be needed if departing and arriving at lower altitude orbits.
</p>
<p>Historically, Mars mission duration has been framed as a binary choice. On one end of the spectrum, to minimize
</p>
<p>the total energy required to achieve the roundtrip mission, mission planning has selected optimal planetary departure
</p>
<p>and arrival timing to maximize the benefit of the natural relative position and velocity between the planets. This
</p>
<p>results in what are typically known as minimum energy long-stay missions (or conjunction class), where both the
</p>
<p>Earth-to-Mars and Mars-to-Earth trajectories are minimum-energy in nature. These types of missions are generally ∼3
</p>
<p>years in total roundtrip duration and require a Mars vicinity stay time on the order of 300 to 500 days while awaiting
</p>
<p>the next optimal planetary alignment for the return journey.
</p>
<p>To achieve shorter duration roundtrip missions to Mars, less-energy-efficient trajectories must be utilized. Unlike
</p>
<p>the minimum-energy conjunction-class mission, where the Mars stay time is dictated by the waiting period for the
</p>
<p>optimal return trajectory, shorter roundtrip missions do not have built-in constraints for Mars stay time. This design
</p>
<p>parameter becomes a key driving factor in interplanetary mission planning. Shorter mission duration also results in
</p>
<p>shorter stay time at Mars. Examples of these shorter roundtrip missions to Mars are typically known as short-stay
</p>
<p>(or opposition class) missions. These types of missions are generally ∼2 years in total roundtrip duration, with
</p>
<p>relatively short Mars vicinity stay time (weeks to a month or two), and may potentially require additional planetary
</p>
<p>flyby maneuvers to help reduce the total energy cost.
</p>

<p>important to note that roundtrip missions to Mars are not limited to these two options. The energy versus time trade
</p>
<p>for a roundtrip mission to Mars is a continuum, but the relationship is exponential in nature: as the mission duration
</p>
<p>is shortened, the energy required to achieve the roundtrip mission increases exponentially, as shown in Fig. 2. This

<p>III. Analysis of In-Space Transportation Concepts
Solving the complete end-to-end trajectory and system optimization for human Mars missions with any transporta-
</p>
<p>tion system is a complex endeavor. Typically, the trajectory optimization of a traditional high thrust transportation
</p>
<p>system is independent of the vehicle sizing optimization of the transportation vehicle. For a hybrid high/low thrust
</p>
<p>system, because the thrust delivered by the electric propulsion system is so low, the trajectory optimization is highly
</p>
<p>coupled with the vehicle sizing and optimization. This requires the trajectory optimization to be solved simultaneously
</p>
<p>with the vehicle sizing and closure. Previous publications[5, 6] detail the integrated analysis method developed to solve
</p>
<p>this complex problem. Using the Copernicus Trajectory Design and Optimization System[7] and a series of internally
</p>
<p>developed plug-ins, the end-to-end trajectory can be modeled and coupled with system sizing to perform optimization
</p>
<p>of the trajectory. For the high thrust systems, even though the trajectory and the sizing are decoupled, there is still
</p>
<p>benefit from utilizing an integrated optimization framework as it enables more rapid analysis and broader trade space
</p>
<p>exploration.
</p>
<p>For this paper, the integrated trajectory analysis framework is used to evaluate the sensitivity of both high and
</p>
<p>low thrust trajectories to mission duration and Mars stay time. For the hybrid analysis, multiple power levels for both
</p>
<p>SEP[8–12] and NEP[6, 13] based hybrid systems were used to help better understand both the vehicle trade space and
</p>
<p>the mission duration / Mars vicinity stay time trade space, as discussed in Ref. [3]. Similarly, for the NTP system, the
</p>
<p>integrated trajectory analysis framework was utilized to evaluate the performance of the system for varying mission
</p>
<p>duration and stay time constraints. An all-chemical propulsion system was not part of this trade analysis and remains
</p>
<p>forward work, but the expectation is that the trends will be similar to the NTP systems, but with significantly higher
</p>
<p>sensitivities due to the lower performance of the engine. In addition, for this analysis, only the “all-up” mission
</p>
<p>architecture was considered, where the crewed vehicle leaves Earth with all of the propellant and systems necessary to
</p>
<p>complete the roundtrip mission. The mass sensitivity does not include any of the Mars surface elements nor the cargo
</p>
<p>necessary to deliver those elements. The conceptual in-space transportation options are depicted in Fig. 3.
</p>
<p>For the purpose of these analyses, the roundtrip mission duration is defined only as the interplanetary portion,
</p>
<p>from the Earth departure trans-Mars injection maneuver to the Earth arrival orbit insertion maneuvers. Additional
</p>
<p>time that the crew will be in space will be dictated by operational needs such as phasing, rendezvous, and docking

<p>The figure also provides the breakdown of the integrated transportation system mass into distinct pieces: the
</p>
<p>habitation mass, which includes the transit habitation system[14] and the logistics outfitting required for the mission;
</p>
<p>the transportation system dry mass; and the propellant mass required to complete the roundtrip mission. For this
</p>
<p>analysis, habitation system dry mass was held constant, with the habitation concept sized and designed for the longer
</p>
<p>duration roundtrip mission and only the logistics and outfitting as the primary variable for mission duration sensitivity.
</p>
<p>Thus for this fixed duration analysis (with the exception of the 880-day mission for the 700 kW-class SEP), the habitat
</p>
<p>mass does not change. The majority of the mass difference between the concepts is in the form of the transportation
</p>
<p>system dry mass and the propellant mass.
</p>
<p>For the three NEP-based hybrid transportation systems, the variation in the propulsion systems mass is the primary
</p>
<p>driver. Higher power NEP systems require larger and more powerful reactors, which also means more complex and
</p>
<p>massive systems are needed to support the power conversion and waste heat rejection. Although a higher power NEP
</p>
<p>system does have a slight benefit in the reactor’s mass efficiency due to component scalability, the integration of the
</p>
<p>support systems to be more mass efficient remains a significant challenge. A lower power NEP system can alleviate
</p>
<p>some of these challenges, but there is likely a diminishing return as power is reduced, as each system has minimum
</p>
<p>mass and volume requirements.
</p>

<p>VI. Summary
As mission duration decreases, the total energy required to perform a roundtrip Mars mission increases exponentially,
</p>
<p>significantly increasing the required Earth departure mass. For high thrust propulsion systems (NTP and all-chemical
</p>
<p>propulsion), the total mass required is at the mercy of the exponential nature of the rocket equation. For the hybrid
</p>
<p>systems (SEP and NEP), there is a limitation on how much energy the low thrust system can produce for any given
</p>
<p>power level. Thus, to enable higher energy missions, either the chemical part of the hybrid system must be more
</p>
<p>heavily utilized or the power level must be increased at the cost of higher system dry mass.
</p>
<p>Sensitivity analyses of various in-space transportation system concepts were shown in this paper to begin mapping
</p>
<p>out the in-space transportation trade space. Each of the transportation systems has its own strengths and weaknesses,
</p>
<p>especially in the context of the continuous mission duration and vicinity stay time trade space. One of the many
</p>
<p>challenges associated with the transportation system decision is the temptation to down-select a single transportation
</p>
<p>system without fully appreciating the integrated trade space and impact of that decision. The two nuclear transportation
</p>
<p>options provide significant benefits for the higher energy missions due to their higher performance but carry the
</p>
<p>penalties of higher system dry mass and additional development timeline and cost. These drawbacks can be substantial
</p>
<p>if the desired mission is a longer duration, lower energy mission. Similarly, forcing the higher energy missions on
</p>
<p>the lower performance, but less complex, non-nuclear options means that their inherent weakness is amplified to
</p>
<p>result in significantly challenging architectural concepts. Having the knowledge and understanding of the integrated
</p>
<p>transportation trade space is paramount in selecting the appropriate transportation system for the appropriate desired
</p>
<p>mission.
</p>
<p>Most importantly, although mission duration and Mars vicinity stay time may be key architecture drivers from
</p>
<p>an in-space transportation perspective, the decision of acceptable mission duration cannot be made solely from this
</p>
<p>10</p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>perspective. Mission duration also has tangible impacts to crew health and performance, as well as system reliability
</p>
<p>and other key architecture considerations. Mars vicinity stay time directly impacts the time available for Mars surface
</p>
<p>operations and the time available to perform key operations in Mars orbit. The integrated nature of the architecture
</p>
<p>decision road map will be the primary focus of the Mars Architecture Team in the coming analysis cycle. This in-space
</p>
<p>transportation analysis will serve as the first guide post of this critical activity that will guide the Moon-to-Mars
</p>
<p>Architecture into the humans to Mars segment.
