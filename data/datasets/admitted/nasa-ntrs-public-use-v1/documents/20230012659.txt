The National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s Exploration Systems Development Mis-



sion Directorate has been developing architecture concepts for human missions to Mars in



alignment with the agency’s Moon-to-Mars Strategy & Objectives. One of the key components



of a human Mars mission is the in-space transportation system that delivers crew and cargo to



Mars vicinity and returns the crew safely back to Earth. The Mars Architecture Team within



ESDMD’s Strategy and Architecture Office has been evaluating multiple in-space transporta-



tion options to satisfy this functional need. The first step of this evaluation is to understand



key architectural drivers for in-space transportation systems. Lunar and Mars missions have



unique challenges, and systems designed for one may not be directly applicable to the other as



they have different energy and mission needs. In addition, the time and distance for a Mars



mission will be drastically different from the historical and current programs, requiring a new



paradigm for mission architects. This paper investigates the impact of mission duration on



a variety of different transportation options. As mission duration decreases, the total energy



required to perform a roundtrip Mars mission increases exponentially, significantly increasing



the required Earth departure mass. Sensitivity analyses of various in-space transportation



system concepts were shown in this paper to begin mapping out the in-space transportation



trade space. Each of the transportation systems has its own strengths and weaknesses, es-



pecially in the context of the continuous mission duration and vicinity stay time trade space.



Although mission duration and Mars vicinity stay time may be key architecture drivers from



an in-space transportation perspective, the decision of acceptable mission duration cannot be



made solely from this perspective. Mission duration also has tangible impacts to crew health



and performance, as well as system reliability and other key architecture considerations. The



integrated nature of the architecture decision road map will be the primary focus of the Mars



Architecture Team in the coming analysis cycle. This in-space transportation analysis will serve



as the first guidepost of this critical activity that will guide the Moon-to-Mars Architecture into



the humans to Mars segment.

One of the key components of a human Mars mission is the in-space transportation system that delivers crew and



cargo to Mars vicinity and returns the crew safely back to Earth, as specified by multiple Moon-to-Mars Objectives



and Recurring Tenets[1]. The Mars Architecture Team within ESDMD’s Strategy and Architecture Office has been



evaluating four in-space transportation options to satisfy this functional need: 1) Hybrid Nuclear Electric Propulsion



(NEP)/ Chemical Propulsion, 2) Nuclear Thermal Propulsion (NTP), 3) Hybrid Solar Electric Propulsion (SEP)/



Chemical Propulsion, and 4) All-Chemical Propulsion. To address the question “which transportation option is best?”



the architecture team needs to understand the performance characteristics—and limitations—for each transportation



option. Each option could be considered “better” than the others, depending on the primary selection criteria and the



order of the integrated decisions that are made.



II. Interplanetary Transits between Earth and Mars
In human Mars mission architecture discussions it is helpful to keep in mind that distances traveled will be at a



scale far beyond the entirety of human space flight experience to date. A single roundtrip journey between Earth and



Mars will put about 1.8 to 2 billion kilometers on a Mars transportation system’s odometer, regardless of departure



opportunity or trajectory. To put this distance in context, for the recent Artemis I mission between Earth and the Moon,



the Orion spacecraft traveled only 2.2 million kilometers[2], or roughly 1/1000th the distance for a roundtrip Mars



mission. The distance that the Moon circles Earth only varies by about 43,000 kilometers over time, so it always takes



about the same amount of energy to travel to the Moon and back no matter the mission cadence. By contrast, the



distance between Earth and Mars can vary by as much as 340 million kilometers as the two planets orbit the Sun. The



closest Mars ever approaches Earth is 54.6 million kilometers; at their farthest, over 400 million kilometers of deep



space separates the two planets.



In addition, lunar and Mars missions have unique challenges, and systems designed for one destination may not be



directly applicable to the other as they have different energy and mission needs. Missions to Mars have higher energy



need, require much longer system service life, and have more stringent departure window constraints. This higher



energy need is evident in Fig. 1, which shows an Earth-Moon-Mars energy map for missions to the surface of the Moon

To that end, recent analysis was designed to explore the pros and cons of different transportation system options



across a wider range of mission profiles than previously considered[3]. The initial metric of interest for recent



assessments was total roundtrip mission duration, due to the significant duration-related flow-down impacts to crew



health and performance[4], technology investment, development timelines, and cost. Interplanetary transportation



energy is a function of many variables, including the distance and relative velocity of the planets as well as the mission



duration and orbital stay time. This relationship between the energy required as a function of total mission duration



and Mars vicinity stay time is shown in Fig. 2. For this particular analysis, Lunar Distance High Earth Orbit (LDHEO)



is used as the starting point for Earth departure staging for Mars missions, and a 5-sol orbit is used for Mars arrival



staging and departure, more energy will be needed if departing and arriving at lower altitude orbits.



Historically, Mars mission duration has been framed as a binary choice. On one end of the spectrum, to minimize



the total energy required to achieve the roundtrip mission, mission planning has selected optimal planetary departure



and arrival timing to maximize the benefit of the natural relative position and velocity between the planets. This



results in what are typically known as minimum energy long-stay missions (or conjunction class), where both the



Earth-to-Mars and Mars-to-Earth trajectories are minimum-energy in nature. These types of missions are generally ∼3



years in total roundtrip duration and require a Mars vicinity stay time on the order of 300 to 500 days while awaiting



the next optimal planetary alignment for the return journey.



To achieve shorter duration roundtrip missions to Mars, less-energy-efficient trajectories must be utilized. Unlike



the minimum-energy conjunction-class mission, where the Mars stay time is dictated by the waiting period for the



optimal return trajectory, shorter roundtrip missions do not have built-in constraints for Mars stay time. This design



parameter becomes a key driving factor in interplanetary mission planning. Shorter mission duration also results in



shorter stay time at Mars. Examples of these shorter roundtrip missions to Mars are typically known as short-stay



(or opposition class) missions. These types of missions are generally ∼2 years in total roundtrip duration, with



relatively short Mars vicinity stay time (weeks to a month or two), and may potentially require additional planetary



flyby maneuvers to help reduce the total energy cost.

III. Analysis of In-Space Transportation Concepts
Solving the complete end-to-end trajectory and system optimization for human Mars missions with any transporta-



tion system is a complex endeavor. Typically, the trajectory optimization of a traditional high thrust transportation



system is independent of the vehicle sizing optimization of the transportation vehicle. For a hybrid high/low thrust



system, because the thrust delivered by the electric propulsion system is so low, the trajectory optimization is highly



coupled with the vehicle sizing and optimization. This requires the trajectory optimization to be solved simultaneously



with the vehicle sizing and closure. Previous publications[5, 6] detail the integrated analysis method developed to solve



this complex problem. Using the Copernicus Trajectory Design and Optimization System[7] and a series of internally



developed plug-ins, the end-to-end trajectory can be modeled and coupled with system sizing to perform optimization



of the trajectory. For the high thrust systems, even though the trajectory and the sizing are decoupled, there is still



benefit from utilizing an integrated optimization framework as it enables more rapid analysis and broader trade space



exploration.



For this paper, the integrated trajectory analysis framework is used to evaluate the sensitivity of both high and



low thrust trajectories to mission duration and Mars stay time. For the hybrid analysis, multiple power levels for both



SEP[8–12] and NEP[6, 13] based hybrid systems were used to help better understand both the vehicle trade space and



the mission duration / Mars vicinity stay time trade space, as discussed in Ref. [3]. Similarly, for the NTP system, the



integrated trajectory analysis framework was utilized to evaluate the performance of the system for varying mission



duration and stay time constraints. An all-chemical propulsion system was not part of this trade analysis and remains



forward work, but the expectation is that the trends will be similar to the NTP systems, but with significantly higher



sensitivities due to the lower performance of the engine. In addition, for this analysis, only the “all-up” mission



architecture was considered, where the crewed vehicle leaves Earth with all of the propellant and systems necessary to



complete the roundtrip mission. The mass sensitivity does not include any of the Mars surface elements nor the cargo



necessary to deliver those elements. The conceptual in-space transportation options are depicted in Fig. 3.



For the purpose of these analyses, the roundtrip mission duration is defined only as the interplanetary portion,



from the Earth departure trans-Mars injection maneuver to the Earth arrival orbit insertion maneuvers. Additional



time that the crew will be in space will be dictated by operational needs such as phasing, rendezvous, and docking

For the three NEP-based hybrid transportation systems, the variation in the propulsion systems mass is the primary



driver. Higher power NEP systems require larger and more powerful reactors, which also means more complex and



massive systems are needed to support the power conversion and waste heat rejection. Although a higher power NEP



system does have a slight benefit in the reactor’s mass efficiency due to component scalability, the integration of the



support systems to be more mass efficient remains a significant challenge. A lower power NEP system can alleviate



some of these challenges, but there is likely a diminishing return as power is reduced, as each system has minimum



mass and volume requirements.



Another option is the SEP-based hybrid system that does not require a nuclear reactor nor a radiator to support



power generation, instead the power generation comes from the solar arrays, which have significantly lighter system dry



mass. However, the primary challenge with a SEP-based hybrid system comes in the form of the inherent inefficiency

similar to the NEP to SEP comparison, it does not carry the penalty of the onboard nuclear system. Analysis of an



all-chemical propulsion system in this context of an equitable comparison to the other three transportation architectures



remains ongoing and will be completed in the near future.



IV. Roundtrip Mars Mission Duration Sensitivity
Figure 5 shows the sensitivity of the Earth departure mass as function of the roundtrip mission duration for a



notional 1.9 MW-class NEP/Chemical hybrid propulsion system[13]. The plot shows normalized sensitivity to a



moderate duration, 850-day roundtrip mission with a minimum Mars vicinity stay time of 50 sols (∼51.4 days). Similar



to the trend shown in Fig. 2, the mass sensitivity increases exponentially as the mission duration decreases, since



the Earth departure mass required follows similar trends as the energy required for roundtrip missions. The one key



difference is that the mass sensitivity does not scale linearly with energy.



For the 1.9 MW-class NEP/Chemical hybrid propulsion system, the moderate 850-day roundtrip mission represents



a relatively benign part of the Earth departure mass sensitivity, but a shorter 760-day roundtrip mission begins to



exhibit significant exponential mass growth behavior. As the energy required increases for shorter duration missions,



the low thrust system (with constant power) is limited in its ability to perform the necessary thrusting to achieve the



energy need, thus the much less efficient high thrust chemical propulsion system must make up the difference. With a



fixed power level, the electric thrusters’ overall energy output is constrained by how much time is available for thrusting,

Figure 6 shows the Earth departure mass sensitivity for three different conceptual NEP/Chemical hybrid propulsion



systems with three different power levels. The 3.8 MW-class NEP/Chemical concept was derived directly from the 1.9



MW-class concept, where the power generation and distribution system was essentially doubled to increase the overall



power level. This comes at a cost of some potential system efficiencies and results in a slight mass penalty for the



overall system. As discussed in Ref. [3], as power level for the NEP concept increases, significant challenge arise with



the development, integration, and deployment of the radiators that are necessary to reject the waste heat generated by



the high power reactors. As shown in Fig. 6, both the 1.9 and 3.8 MW-class NEP/Chemical hybrid propulsion systems



are simply too large for these mission durations and payload masses to overcome the penalty associated with carrying



the higher power systems. However, the overall mass sensitivity (slope of the curve) is slightly lower for the higher



powered systems for much shorter mission durations, showing the potential performance for a more power optimized



system.



On the other end of the spectrum, a lower power, 750 kW-class NEP/Chemical hybrid propulsion system provides



a more mass optimal solution for the overall trade space. This concept was also derived from the 1.9 MW-class



concept[13], using a similar design but removing a portion of the radiator panels and other related systems to meet



the heat rejection need for the lower power system. The reduction in the propulsion system mass directly resulted in a



reduction in overall mass for much of the trade space for this example analysis. The lack of power does manifest itself



in much higher mass sensitivity for the shorter duration missions as shown in the figure.



Comparing these three NEP/Chemical hybrid concepts, the significant difference is the overall system dry mass



delta, resulting in variation of the propellant demands for each mission duration. The overall mass sensitivity (the slopes



of the curves) is not appreciably different between the three concepts, especially for mission durations greater than 800



days. For missions shorter than 800 days, there can be significant mass discriminators between the three concepts;



additional analysis and concept refinement is required to fully evaluate the differences between the performance of the



three concepts. Power and mass optimization for different roundtrip duration missions remain a key trade for hybrid



transportation systems.

Finally, Fig. 7 shows the additional comparison between the NEP/Chemical, SEP/Chemical, and NTP systems



for Earth departure mass sensitivity to mission duration. The NTP system’s mass sensitivity to mission duration is



fundamentally different than the hybrid systems. Although the NEP and SEP systems can scale relatively smoothly



based on energy needs, the NTP system’s scaling is discrete in nature due to the drop tanks used for the NTP concepts



to accommodate the high volume requirement of hydrogen propellant. In comparison, the NEP and SEP concepts



utilizes much denser liquid oxygen and liquid methane as its primary propellant for its chemical propulsion system.



These hydrogen drop tanks are discrete elements, and thus as propellant demand increases, there are discontinuity in



the overall system mass due to the addition of discrete drop tanks. This discretization manifests itself in the figure

mission durations between 830 and 900 days, the NTP concept represents the least mass optimal solution. However, for



mission durations shorter than 760 days, the NTP system is lighter than all of the concepts shown and shows slightly



lower mass sensitivity (slope of the curve) than the other options. This shows the potential benefit the NTP concept



and the capability for the high performance of the NTP system to perform these short duration, high energy missions.



The comparison between all of the options shows one key finding. Each propulsion technology concept has its



own strengths and weaknesses that could be exploited and optimized depending on the desired mission duration. The



SEP-based hybrid system represents the lowest mass and lowest performance concept that can be optimized for the



long duration, minimum energy missions to produce the most mass efficient solutions, but it is not well suited for



higher energy missions. The NEP-based hybrid system can perform well for the lower energy missions but carries



the cost of a higher dry mass. However, the NEP system shines for the moderate duration and energy missions in the



middle of the mission duration trade space. Here the NEP system can provide the power that the SEP system is lacking



and provides consistent performance for a variety of moderate duration and energy missions. The NTP system, with



its inherent high thrust, high performance nature, provides significant benefit for the extremely high energy and short



duration missions.

system for 790-day and 850-day roundtrip missions. As the figure shows, a shorter stay time at Mars results in lower



mass solutions, and it provides the electric thruster more time in interplanetary space for thrusting when the roundtrip



duration is held constant. Longer Mars vicinity stay time reduces the time for thrusting, thus requiring more chemical



propulsion to make up the energy deficit resulting in higher mass. The other key attribute is that this sensitivity to



Mars vicinity stay time is higher for shorter total duration missions. Shorter missions already require more energy than



longer missions, thus restricting the limited time that the electric thrusters have for thrusting even further drives up the



mass sensitivity.

VI. Summary
As mission duration decreases, the total energy required to perform a roundtrip Mars mission increases exponentially,



significantly increasing the required Earth departure mass. For high thrust propulsion systems (NTP and all-chemical



propulsion), the total mass required is at the mercy of the exponential nature of the rocket equation. For the hybrid



systems (SEP and NEP), there is a limitation on how much energy the low thrust system can produce for any given



power level. Thus, to enable higher energy missions, either the chemical part of the hybrid system must be more



heavily utilized or the power level must be increased at the cost of higher system dry mass.



Sensitivity analyses of various in-space transportation system concepts were shown in this paper to begin mapping



out the in-space transportation trade space. Each of the transportation systems has its own strengths and weaknesses,



especially in the context of the continuous mission duration and vicinity stay time trade space. One of the many



challenges associated with the transportation system decision is the temptation to down-select a single transportation



system without fully appreciating the integrated trade space and impact of that decision. The two nuclear transportation



options provide significant benefits for the higher energy missions due to their higher performance but carry the



penalties of higher system dry mass and additional development timeline and cost. These drawbacks can be substantial



if the desired mission is a longer duration, lower energy mission. Similarly, forcing the higher energy missions on



the lower performance, but less complex, non-nuclear options means that their inherent weakness is amplified to



result in significantly challenging architectural concepts. Having the knowledge and understanding of the integrated



transportation trade space is paramount in selecting the appropriate transportation system for the appropriate desired



mission.



Most importantly, although mission duration and Mars vicinity stay time may be key architecture drivers from



an in-space transportation perspective, the decision of acceptable mission duration cannot be made solely from this



10











perspective. Mission duration also has tangible impacts to crew health and performance, as well as system reliability



and other key architecture considerations. Mars vicinity stay time directly impacts the time available for Mars surface



operations and the time available to perform key operations in Mars orbit. The integrated nature of the architecture



decision road map will be the primary focus of the Mars Architecture Team in the coming analysis cycle. This in-space



transportation analysis will serve as the first guide post of this critical activity that will guide the Moon-to-Mars



Architecture into the humans to Mars segment.
