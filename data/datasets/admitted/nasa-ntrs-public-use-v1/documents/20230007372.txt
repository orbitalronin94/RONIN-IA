<p>Novel Microgreen Crop Testing for Space 
</p>
<p>LaShelle E. Spencer1, Jennifer L. Gooden2, Aaron B. Curry3 
</p>
<p>Amentum Services, Kennedy Space Center, FL, 32899, USA 
</p>
<p>Matthew W. Romeyn4, Raymond M. Wheeler5 
</p>
<p>NASA, Kennedy Space Center, FL, 32899, USA 
</p>
<p>and 
</p>
<p>Takiyah A. Sirmons6 
</p>
<p>Leidos Technologies, Johnson Space Center, TX 77058, USA 
</p>
<p>Long-duration missions beyond low-Earth orbit will encounter challenges in maintaining 
</p>
<p>adequate nutrition and crew acceptability in the food system. In situ production of fresh 
</p>
<p>produce can supplement nutrient deficiencies in the prepackaged diet. Currently, there are a 
</p>
<p>relatively small number of crops that can be reliably grown in space for space crop production 
</p>
<p>efforts. An intriguing area of new investigation involves novel types of microgreens that have 
</p>
<p>the potential to be sources of calories, fat, carbohydrates, and protein. These sources of 
</p>
<p>nutrition are not obtainable in significant quantities with current pick and eat crops. Many 
</p>
<p>microgreen cultivars are also sources of nutrients of interest, such as Vitamins B1, C, and K, 
</p>
<p>and elements such as potassium. Microgreens should be selected to address specific nutritional 
</p>
<p>deficits, as identified by NASA’s Human Research Program, with an emphasis on having a 
</p>
<p>diversity of crops to meet nutritional requirements and crew acceptability. To achieve this, 
</p>
<p>the concept of Crop Readiness Level (CRL) has been developed to gauge readiness of crops 
</p>
<p>for spaceflight applications. CRL includes assessing environmental compatibility, food safety 
</p>
<p>considerations, relevant nutritional analysis, and sensory analysis. Recent testing at Kennedy 
</p>
<p>Space Center has focused on advancing the CRL of a variety of novel microgreens. These 
</p>
<p>varieties were grown under 150 µmol m-2 s-1 PPFD from LED lights, 3000 ppm CO2, and 23°C 
</p>
<p>to simulate an ISS environment. Crops were harvested and yield was assessed. Then, baseline 
</p>
<p>microbiological and nutritional analysis (Vitamins B1, C, K; mineral analysis; proximate 
</p>
<p>analysis) and sensory evaluation were performed. These baseline data are essential to selecting 
</p>
<p>candidate crops for future missions and assessing crop production hardware and changes in 
</p>
<p>environmental conditions on future crop performance and nutritional quality. 
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>s we move toward long-duration, exploration class missions, the capability to grow nutritious, palatable food for 
</p>
<p>crew consumption during spaceflight becomes increasingly important.  Critical nutrients have been proven to 
</p>
<p>degrade over time in pre-packaged, thermo-stabilized meals, to the extent that they will not meet the shelf-life 
</p>
<p>requirements of long-duration mission scenarios1-2. Supplementing these nutrients through pharmaceutical methods 
</p>
<p>has not been recommended due to concerns with possible side effects, faster degradation of nutrients in supplement 
</p>
<p>form, crew compliance, and lack of synergistic benefits associated with the natural combination of phytochemicals in 
</p>
<p>whole-food delivery. Implementation of a pick-and-eat bioregenerative produce system in spaceflight has tremendous 
</p>
<p>potential to supplement nutrition over time, while supporting crew psychosocial health through the introduction of 
</p>
<p>fresh foods that increase the variety, texture, flavor, and color attributes of the food system3-4. To date, the testing of 
</p>
<p>edible crops for space flight has been limited largely to leafy green species. A common approach to these studies is to 
</p>
<p>simulate the types of environments that might occur in International Space Station (ISS) plant chambers like Veggie, 
</p>
<p>for example, elevated CO2, LED lighting, and typical ISS cabin temperatures and humidity. Regardless of the plant 
</p>
<p>growth chambers used for ISS and future vegetable production systems, growth volume and shoot/canopy height will 
</p>
<p>be limited. For example, shoot height available for the Veggie and Advanced Plant Habitat (APH) growth chamber 
</p>
<p>systems onboard the ISS are 47 cm and 45 cm, respectively5.   
</p>
<p>A next step for supplementing food crops for space might be the inclusion of small, quick-growing plants such as 
</p>
<p>microgreens. Microgreens are edible seedlings grown in a lawn and harvested after the cotyledons emerge (or soon 
</p>
<p>after true leaves emerge), generally 7-14 days after planting. Most varieties of microgreens are approximately 2.5-7.5 
</p>
<p>cm tall.  They come in a variety of colors, aromatic flavors, and concentrated nutritional profiles. They have 
</p>
<p>historically been used in the restaurant industry to embellish food, and are commonly consumed in sandwiches, soups, 
</p>
<p>and salads6. Recently, microgreens have gained increasing popularity as food ingredients because of their high nutrient 
</p>
<p>content. Although the exact content can vary, most types tend to be rich in iron, potassium, and magnesium7-9.  Sun et 
</p>
<p>al (2013) reported that, when comparing microgreens to more mature greens, the nutrient levels in microgreens can 
</p>
<p>be up to nine times higher than those found in mature greens10. Microgreens are ideally suited for cultivation in indoor 
</p>
<p>environments due to their low light and water requirements11. For this reason, they are being evaluated for biological 
</p>
<p>performance and suitability under ISS-like environmental conditions, as well as being tested for nutritional content 
</p>
<p>and acceptability.  
</p>
<p>For these studies, we wanted to 1) assess the morphology, yield, and viability of crops grown in mission relevant 
</p>
<p>environmental conditions, 2) evaluate organoleptic acceptability, and 3) determine nutritional value of several 
</p>
<p>microgreen cultivars. These findings can help us advance these new species and cultivars through a Crop Readiness 
</p>
<p>Level (CRL) evaluation for eventual spaceflight applications.12 
</p>
<p>II. Materials and Methods 
</p>
<p>A. Microgreen Cultivation System 
</p>
<p>A custom, two-tiered microgreen cultivation system was utilized 
</p>
<p>(Figure 1).  Each tray (17 x 28 inches) contained three troughs (5.52 
</p>
<p>x 28 inches). Trays were fed via a custom 40 L nutrient solution tank 
</p>
<p>constructed of black polycarbonate. Each tray was provided solution, 
</p>
<p>which was recirculated utilizing external pumps (Little Giant, 3-
</p>
<p>MDX PN2P579). Pumps were controlled via OPTO22 software 
</p>
<p>customized to provide up to eight irrigation events with user-selected 
</p>
<p>times and durations.  
</p>
<p>B. Seeds 
</p>
<p>A complete list of all cultivars screened can be found in Table 5. 
</p>
<p>Seeds were sourced from commercially well-known vendors such as 
</p>
<p>Johnny’s Selected Seeds (Winslow, ME) and True Leaf 
</p>
<p>Market/Kitazawa Seed (Salt Lake City, UT). For these tests, seeds 
</p>
<p>labeled “for microgreens” were sought after first, with standard seed 
</p>
<p>types selected in the absence of microgreen seed.   
</p>
<p>Prior to planting, the weight of the seed needed to plant a 4 x 4 in 
</p>
<p>area was weighed out and broadcast within that area.

<p>C. Environmental Conditions  
</p>
<p>The microgreen cultivation system was installed in a reach-in environmental growth chamber (Percival E41L2, 
</p>
<p>Perry, Iowa) located in the Space Station Processing Facility at NASA Kennedy Space Center, Florida (located at sea 
</p>
<p>level with atmospheric pressure of ~101 kPa). The chamber air temperature, relative humidity (RH), and CO2 setpoints 
</p>
<p>were maintained at 23 °C, 50%, and 3000 ppm, respectively, for all tests. These set points were chosen to simulate an 
</p>
<p>environment similar to that of the ISS cabin air. 
</p>
<p>Un-sanitized seeds were sown on a sterilized hemp mat. The system was automatically irrigated several times daily 
</p>
<p>with a modified 0.5 X Hoagland’s nutrient solution, pH 5.8-6.5 and 1200 µS cm-1 electrical conductivity (EC). Trays 
</p>
<p>were covered with a transparent lid for 2-3 days to promote germination.  
</p>
<p>Lighting was provided by OSRAM LED grow lights (Phytofy RL, Munich, Germany). The photosynthetic photon 
</p>
<p>flux density (PPFD) target was 150 μmol m−2 s−1, averaged over the entire canopy, with a 16-h (16 h light/8 h dark) 
</p>
<p>photoperiod. Spectral quality consisted of 23% blue (400-500 nm), 27% green (500-600 nm), and 50% red (600-700 
</p>
<p>nm), using a combination of 450 nm, white (2700 K), 630 nm, and 660 nm LEDs. To minimize lighting hotspots and 
</p>
<p>improve light distribution, a light diffusion acrylic panel (Curbell Plastics, Largo, FL) was installed over the surface 
</p>
<p>of the LED fixture. 
</p>
<p>D. Crop Assessments 
</p>
<p>General appearance was monitored qualitatively throughout the 10 day experiment.  
</p>
<p>At 10-14 days after planting (DAP), the microgreens were harvested. Average canopy height (collected at three 
</p>
<p>random locations), total harvest fresh mass, and presence of true leaves were recorded. Three subsamples were 
</p>
<p>collected and oven dried at 70 ⁰C for 72 hours, then weighed to collect dry mass.  
</p>
<p>E. Nutritional Analysis 
</p>
<p>Microgreens were harvested and frozen at -80 ⁰C until they were shipped to a commercial laboratory (Eurofins 
</p>
<p>Nutritional Analysis Center, Des Moines, IA), where they were analyzed for proximate composition (ash, calories, 
</p>
<p>carbohydrates, fat, moisture, protein), mineral content (calcium, iron, magnesium, phosphorous, potassium), and 
</p>
<p>vitamin content (B1, C, K).  
</p>
<p>F. Sensory Analysis 
</p>
<p>Microgreens were shipped live (unharvested and still attached to hemp mat) in coolers containing cold packs 
</p>
<p>overnight from Kennedy Space Center (KSC) to Johnson Space Center (JSC), where formal sensory evaluation was 
</p>
<p>conducted using the untrained volunteers at the Space Food Systems Laboratory sensory evaluation center. Each 
</p>
<p>sample was rated on appearance, color, aroma, flavor, texture, and overall acceptability on a 9-point hedonic scale, 
</p>
<p>where 1 corresponds to “dislike extremely” and 9 corresponds to “like extremely”. Just About Right (JAR) measured 
</p>
<p>the appropriateness of tenderness, bitterness, crispiness, nuttiness, sweetness, and aromatics on a 5-point scale. 
</p>
<p>Example: 1=not tender at all, 2=not tender enough, 3=just about right, 4=somewhat too tender, 5=much too tender.  
</p>
<p> Due to ongoing issues with the COVID-19 pandemic, there were occasions where only an informal testing could 
</p>
<p>be completed. These data were collected in a laboratory setting with n &lt; 10.  
</p>

<p>III. Results 
</p>
<p>G. Harvest Metrics and Crop Morphology  
</p>
<p>Under these environmental conditions and 
</p>
<p>when grown in our system, harvesting at 10 DAP 
</p>
<p>was adequate for the majority of the crops. At 10 
</p>
<p>DAP, the majority of the seeds had germinated, a 
</p>
<p>sufficient lawn had formed and true leaves were not 
</p>
<p>present.  One caveat is that the pea cultivars could 
</p>
<p>have been harvested at 7 or 8 days, which would 
</p>
<p>have produced a more tender, less fibrous product. 
</p>
<p>While we were ultimately successful, 
</p>
<p>cantaloupe microgreens were extremely difficult to 
</p>
<p>grow in this system. The vining nature of this crop 
</p>
<p>prevented adequate root penetration into the hemp 
</p>
<p>mat, often requiring the seeds to be weighed down 
</p>
<p>during the germination phase. Even when grown in 
</p>
<p>other media types (i.e. arcillite), roots grew up and 
</p>
<p>out, along the surface of the media. It appears that 
</p>
<p>this seed type is more easily cultivated in soil-based media, planted deeply (Figure 2). 
</p>
<p>Horticulturally, the easiest crops to grow were Brassicas (mustard family). Aspects that contributed to this ease 
</p>
<p>included high and quick germination rates, seeds that did not harbor damaging endophytic microorganisms, an early 
</p>
<p>root system which could easily penetrate the hemp matting, and a plant height that allowed easy separation from the 
</p>
<p>hemp mat at harvest. Varieties with endophytic fungus issues included orach and sunflower cultivars. The fungus from 
</p>
<p>orach was so invasive that the newly germinated seedlings were overcome, and mostly failed to thrive (Figure 3). 
</p>
<p>Sunflower, on the other hand, often exhibited fungus on the seed coat that didn’t seem to affect plant growth overmuch, 
</p>
<p>but was considered unsightly and would be problematic for consumption (Figure 4a).  Several varieties, such as alfalfa 
</p>
<p>and wasabi arugula, were so short that they could not be easily harvested (Figure 5). The average heights of these 
</p>
<p>plants were 19 and 20 mm, respectively. Additionally, the seeds of wasabi 
</p>
<p>arugula were very small and hard to handle and sow. 
</p>
<p>Another issue often observed with large-seeded microgreens was the 
</p>
<p>seedling’s inability to shed its seed coat. Buckwheat shoot seeds maintained 
</p>
<p>the hard, black seed coat, whereas buckwheat groats were hulled. Groats are 
</p>
<p>the hulled kernels of cereal grains. Because of this, microgreens from groats 
</p>
<p>were devoid of the seed coat (Figure 4, b/c), and would be more favorable for 
</p>
<p>consumption.  
</p>
<p>The shungiku varieties proved to be the lowest yielding, due to low 
</p>
<p>germination rates. High yields were observed from trays of buckwheat 
</p>
<p>shoots/groats (356.3 g and 303.7 g), mung beans (339.4 g), and ‘Mammoth 
</p>
<p>Melting Sugar’ peas (338.8 g). It should be noted that these are all large seed 
</p>
<p>types, requiring between 56 to 133 g of seeds per tray to achieve these yields 
</p>
<p>(Table 5).  
</p>
<p>Good germination and seedling establishment, absence of excessive 
</p>
<p>fungus, and yield were all factors when choosing cultivars to down select.  
</p>
<p>Images of down selected microgreens can be seen in Figure 6 and Figure 7. 
</p>
<p>Cultivar names, planting density, and resulting yields can be found in Table 5, where down selected cultivars used 
</p>
<p>during additional testing are also highlighted.  
</p>

<p>I. Organoleptic Results 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Sensory panelists were presented with washed samples and asked to rate their taste using a 9-point hedonic scale, 
</p>
<p>and a “just about right” (JAR) scale that measures the appropriateness of the level of a specific attribute. The results 
</p>
<p>of the panel assessments and standard deviations are shown in Table 3 and Table 4.   
</p>
<p>Fifteen of the eighteen microgreens received an overall rating of 6.0 or higher, which is considered good.  
</p>
<p>Cantaloupe received the highest overall score (7.67), with several tasters commenting on its sweetness and that it 
</p>
<p>smelled and tasted just like mature cantaloupe fruit. Brassicas ‘White Stem’ pac choi (7.40), wasabi mustard (7.36), 
</p>
<p>and rutabaga (7.31) achieved the next highest overall ratings after cantaloupe.  ‘White Stem’ pac choi was enjoyed 
</p>

<p>Discussion 
</p>
<p>Of the 18 cultivars down selected for nutritional and organoleptic analysis, only two (shiso and chia) would be 
</p>
<p>withheld from future testing due to poor palatability. All other microgreens were well received by tasters. Issues with 
</p>
<p>product quality often reduced palatability, rightfully so. The shelf life of microgreens is a major concern, even for 
</p>
<p>commercial producers who have explored modified packaging strategies, temperature control, and calcium treatments 
</p>
<p>to extend shelf life18. It should be noted that, while none of these strategies were utilized here, every effort was made 
</p>
<p>to supply a high-quality product (shipping live and cold). Food safety testing, which was conducted on product that 
</p>
<p>was freshly harvested, showed that the samples had higher microbial counts in comparison to other leafy greens grown 
</p>
<p>in controlled environments at KSC. Decreasing product quality, such as wilting, dehydrating, and decay, raises an 
</p>
<p>even higher concern for foodborne illness. Various strategies have since been explored to reduce the microbial and 
</p>
<p>fungal load on microgreen products. Testing of cultivation under a blue-rich light spectrum, which decreases product 
</p>
<p>height prior to harvest, and plant growth hardware that separates the root system from the foliage have proven to have 
</p>
<p>some effect on the microbial load19. If used in conjunction with Prosan, a vegetable sanitizer that has been used by 
</p>
<p>ISS crew members to sanitize produce grown in the Veggie facility, these strategies could provide a product with 
</p>
<p>lower microbial and fungal loads. Microgreens are nutritional powerhouses. If chosen and cultivated correctly, they 
</p>
<p>have the ability to supply substantial amounts of protein, vitamin C, B1, and K.  
</p>
<p>This raises the question of how many microgreens to produce at any given time in a space mission scenario. We 
</p>
<p>suggest to grow and harvest single serve portions for consumption.  Testing reported here advanced these microgreen 
</p>
<p>crops to CRL 3. Additional testing reported by Hummerick et. al (2022), moves these crops to CRL 4.  To further 
</p>
<p>advance along the CRL, future testing should be in flight-forward hardware and include further food safety testing in 
</p>
<p>this hardware.
