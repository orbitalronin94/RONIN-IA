<p>New X-ray Detectors to Provide Unprecedented 
</p>
<p>Vision of the Invisible Universe  
</p>
<p>Project: Advanced Magnetic Microcalorimeter development  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Snapshot: A new class of X-ray detector with unprecedented energy resolution and array size could help 
</p>
<p>transform our understanding of the cosmos through unparalleled vision into the otherwise invisible 
</p>
<p>universe. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Very detailed information is now available from ultraviolet, optical, and submillimeter observations of 
</p>
<p>the stellar, dust, and cold gas content of galaxies, and yet there is a dearth of understanding about the 
</p>
<p>mechanisms that formed these galaxies. To truly understand how galaxies form, X-ray observations from 
</p>
<p>high energy resolution imaging spectrometers are needed to see the cores of the galaxies themselves.  
</p>
<p>New large-area, high-angular-resolution, imaging X-ray spectrometers will expose the essential drivers 
</p>
<p>of galaxy evolution, which leave imprints in the warm-to-hot plasma that cosmologists believe exists in 
</p>
<p>the spaces between galaxies. These intergalactic spaces contain 40–50% of the 'normal matter' in the 
</p>
<p>universe and extend well beyond the currently visible size of galaxies.  
</p>
<p>A class of X-ray spectrometers called microcalorimeters operate at a very low temperature—a few tens 
</p>
<p>of milli-Kelvin above absolute zero. Over the past five years, the X-ray Microcalorimeter Group at NASA’s 
</p>
<p>Goddard Space Flight Center (GSFC), the Advanced Imager Technology Group at Massachusetts Institute 
</p>
<p>of Technology’s Lincoln Laboratory (MIT/LL), and the Quantum Sensors Group at the National Institute 
</p>
<p>  
</p>
<p>Figure 1: Prototype 100,000-pixel magnetic calorimeter array developed 
</p>
<p>via collaboration between NASA GSFC and MIT Lincoln Laboratory.  
</p>
<p>(Image credit: NASA GSFC) </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>   
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>   
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>of Standards and Technology (NIST) in Boulder, Colorado have been collaborating on the development 
</p>
<p>of an ambitious new X-ray camera with unprecedented imaging and spectroscopic capabilities.  
</p>
<p>This camera is based on a new type of X-ray microcalorimeter called a magnetic microcalorimeter. This 
</p>
<p>NASA/MIT/NIST effort significantly extends the capabilities of the technology. For example, the X-Ray 
</p>
<p>Imaging and Spectroscopy Mission (XRISM) mission, which is a collaboration between JAXA and NASA 
</p>
<p>scheduled to launch in 2023, consists of a microcalorimeter array with 36 pixels. An ESA flagship mission 
</p>
<p>currently in formulation (the Advanced Telescope for High Energy Astrophysics, or ATHENA) will have a 
</p>
<p>microcalorimeter array with about two-thousand pixels. The arrays under development by the 
</p>
<p>NASA/MIT/NIST collaboration have around one-hundred thousand pixels or more, reaching the angular 
</p>
<p>scales and array sizes normally only associated with charged-coupled device (CCD) cameras.  
</p>
<p>Figure 2 shows one of the 100,000-pixel arrays the team has developed.  The pixels are designed to have 
</p>
<p>energy resolution that is about two orders of magnitude greater than that of an X-ray CCD camera. This 
</p>
<p>exquisite high-energy resolution is critical to measure the abundances, temperatures, densities, and 
</p>
<p>velocities of astrophysical plasmas. Such measurements will expose the essential drivers of galaxy 
</p>
<p>evolution that are hidden in the plasmas of the universe.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>When an incoming X-ray hits the microcalorimeter’s absorber, its energy is converted into heat, which is 
</p>
<p>measured by a thermometer. The temperature rise is directly proportional to the X-ray’s energy. The 
</p>
<p>thermometers employed with magnetic microcalorimeters use paramagnetism to enable high-precision 
</p>
<p>temperature sensing. In a paramagnet, the magnetization is inversely proportional to temperature, 
</p>
<p>making it very sensitive to small changes at the low temperatures (at or below 50 milli-Kelvin) at which 
</p>
<p>these devices operate.  
</p>
<p>In addition to single-pixel sensors, it is possible to design position-sensitive magnetic microcalorimeters 
</p>
<p>in which a sensor is attached to multiple X-ray absorbers with different thermal conductance strengths. 
</p>
<p>The unique temporal response of the different pixels to X-ray events allows the pixel event location to 
</p>
<p>be distinguished. Due to the many heads in the sensor, this kind of thermal multiplexing device is often 
</p>
<p>called a `Hydra,’ after the multi-headed serpentine water monster in Greek and Roman mythology. An 
</p>
<p>example of a magnetic calorimeter `Hydra’ sensor is shown in Figure 3.  
</p>
<p>  
</p>
<p>Figure 2: Zoom-in of prototype 100,000 -pixel magnetic calorimeter array showing the three different 
</p>
<p>pixel types in this array. (Image credit: NASA GSFC) 
</p>

<p> 
</p>
<p>The main factors limiting the development of microcalorimeter arrays with the desired size and angular 
</p>
<p>resolution (the distance from the center of one pixel to the next, or “pitch”) are the challenges involved 
</p>
<p>in fabricating high-density, high-yield, microstrip superconducting wiring to connect all the pixels in the 
</p>
<p>array. The major innovation employed to overcome this difficulty is to incorporate many layers of buried 
</p>
<p>wiring underneath the top surface of detector chips on which the microcalorimeter arrays are then 
</p>
<p>fabricated. Through an investment in technology for superconducting electronics, MIT/LL developed a 
</p>
<p>process that allows over eight layers of superconducting wiring with high yield. The array shown in 
</p>
<p>Figure 2 uses four layers of superconducting wiring, and the next generation of devices currently in 
</p>
<p>fabrication utilizes seven layers of buried wiring.  By combining this buried wiring process with the 25-
</p>
<p>pixel, ‘thermally multiplexed,’ microcalorimeters developed at NASA GSFC, the team has been able to 
</p>
<p>produce large-format arrays of wires with pitch as fine as 25 microns.  
</p>

</p>
<p>different thermal links that go to 25 different contact points from which 25 independent X-ray 
</p>
<p>absorbers can be suspended on top of the sensor, as shown in the electron micrographs (center, 
</p>

<p> 
</p>
<p>The final key development needed to make this detector suitable for future astrophysics missions is the 
</p>
<p>multiplexed read-out needed for such large arrays of pixels. With NASA funding, NIST is developing a 
</p>
<p>microwave-multiplexer superconducting quantum interference device (μ-MUX SQUID) read-out in a 
</p>
<p>form-factor suitable for direct integration with this detector. Four two-dimensional chips carrying this 
</p>
<p>read-out will be bump-bonded to the four large green rectangular areas in the outer regions of the 
</p>
<p>detector shown in Figure 1. NIST has recently demonstrated low-noise μ-MUX SQUIDs, in small one-
</p>
<p>dimensional resonator arrays suitable for the new magnetic calorimeters. These μ-MUX SQUIDs 
</p>
<p>measured magnetic flux noise that corresponds to just 20 quanta (or photons) at signal frequencies, 
</p>
<p>meeting the challenging design requirements. In the near future, two-dimensional versions of this read-
</p>
<p>out will be bump-bonded to the detector shown in Figure 1, and the team hopes to demonstrate a 
</p>
<p>ground-breaking new astrophysics instrumentation capability. 
</p>
<p>NASA’s Astrophysics Division has invested in magnetic calorimeter development at GSFC since 2008, 
</p>
<p>most recently in this collaborative effort with MIT/LL. This type of device is under consideration for use 
</p>
<p>in several potential future missions. 
</p>

<p>Growing Yeast on the Moon to Study Radiation 
</p>
<p>Risks to Human Explorers 
</p>
<p>Project: Lunar Explorer Instrument for space biology Applications (LEIA) 
</p>
<p>Snapshot: An autonomous microfluidic culturing system with CubeSat heritage teams up with two state-
</p>
<p>of-the-art radiation detectors to measure how biology responds to the radiation and reduced gravity 
</p>
<p>environment on the lunar surface. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>A LEIA card stack: a fluidic card with microwells for yeast growth, sandwiched by the electronics that 
</p>
<p>control temperature and optics. 
</p>
<p>Humans are returning to the Moon for the first time in decades, and we intend to stay. NASA’s Artemis 
</p>
<p>missions are preparing for a sustained human presence on and around the Moon, with the ultimate goal 
</p>
<p>of sending humans to Mars. These deep-space destinations present health risks to astronauts that are 
</p>
<p>qualitatively and quantitatively different from those associated with stays on the International Space 
</p>
<p>Station (ISS). One of the most significant risks for astronauts venturing outside the protection of Earth’s 
</p>
<p>magnetic field is ionizing radiation: Galactic Cosmic Rays (GCR), Solar Particle Events (SPE), and the 
</p>
<p>“albedo” radiation produced by the interaction of space radiation with the lunar surface. Exposure to 
</p>
<p>ionizing radiation can result in an increased risk of cancer, cardiovascular disease, and neurological 
</p>
<p>impairment.  
</p>
<p>The Lunar Explorer Instrument for space biology Applications (LEIA) aims to provide empirical data to 
</p>
<p>help define these risks by measuring the biological effects of lunar radiation and reduced gravity using a 
</p>
<p>well-studied microbe whose DNA has a lot in common with ours: baker’s yeast. Remarkably, yeast and 
</p>
<p>humans have hundreds of similar genes, including those important to DNA damage responses, so 
</p>
<p>understanding the effects of stress on yeast in space will help us develop countermeasures to protect 
</p>
<p>humans. As an added dividend, LEIA will obtain precise measurements of the radiation environment on 
</p>
<p>the lunar surface at the South Pole, near the anticipated landing site for Artemis III—the mission that 
</p>
<p>will return humans to the Moon’s surface. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>   
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>   
</p>
<p>

<p>

<p> 
</p>
<p>Drawings of the hardware components comprising LEIA—the ARES/Biosensor subassembly and the 
</p>
<p>Mini-FND. The image at the top on the right depicts how these two components are connected to form 
</p>
<p>LEIA. (ACRONYM KEY: MLI = Multi-Layer Insulation, EPS = Electrical Power System, EMI = 
</p>
<p>Electromagnetic Interference) 
</p>
<p>LEIA is a suite of instruments that work together to characterize the lunar radiation environment and 
</p>
<p>the biological response to it. The biological instrument, the BioSensor, is based on the BioSentinel 
</p>
<p>CubeSat developed at Ames Research Center as part of the Artemis I mission. The BioSensor has an 
</p>
<p>autonomous microfluidic system in which yeast cells, kept in a dry dormant state for transit, are 
</p>
<p>activated by adding a liquid culture medium to stimulate growth and metabolism over several days. The 
</p>
<p>LEIA version of the BioSensor has 16 fluidic cards, each with 16 culture microwells, so the instrument 
</p>
<p>can accommodate 256 samples and calibration standards. Each well is equipped with three Light-
</p>
<p>Emitting Diode (LED) lights, as well as a photodiode sensor to measure cell growth via optical density 
</p>
<p>and metabolic activity using a color-changing oxidation-reduction (redox) indicator dye. Chronic, 
</p>
<p>sublethal radiation damage to cells is a significant concern for astronaut health. The BioSensor detects 
</p>
<p>these effects through changes in rates of metabolism and cell growth in the yeast. 
</p>

<p>Two radiation detectors will accompany the BioSensor as part of the LEIA suite. The Airborne 
</p>
<p>Radiological Enhanced-sensor System (ARES), a radiation detector slated for use on the Artemis II and III 
</p>
<p>missions, will measure time-resolved dose, dose rate, and energy deposition by charged particles from 
</p>
<p>the solar wind and GCR. The Mini-Fast Neutron Detector (Mini-FND) will measure high-energy neutrons, 
</p>
<p>which are a substantial component of lunar surface radiation and a significant risk to quantify for 
</p>
<p>crewed missions. The Mini-FND is a miniaturized version of the International Space Station (ISS) 
</p>
<p>Radiation Assessment Detector (ISS-RAD) FND. ARES was developed at NASA Johnson Space Center 
</p>
<p>(JSC), and the Mini-FND is being developed by the Southwest Research Institute (SwRI) in Boulder, CO. 
</p>
<p>LEIA technology has roots in instruments with extensive flight heritage, yet several developments 
</p>
<p>distinguish it from its predecessors. One example is the introduction of a “late-load” capability, enabling 
</p>
<p>investigators to integrate the fluidic cards containing the yeast cells into the BioSensor at the end of the 
</p>
<p>payload assembly. Late load allows fresh yeast cells to be flown, which mitigates the cell damage that 
</p>
<p>occurs when yeast cells are stored in suboptimal environmental conditions during integrated lander 
</p>
<p>testing and potential launch delays. This improvement ensures more direct measurements of the effects 
</p>
<p>of the lunar surface environment. </p>
<p></p>

</p>
<p>The LEIA yeast strains, currently being developed by Principal Investigators (PIs) Mark Settles and Sergio 
</p>
<p>Santa Maria at NASA Ames Research Center (ARC), represent a biotechnological development central to 
</p>
<p>achieving the scientific aims of the mission. The LEIA experiments will include both strains flown on 
</p>
<p>BioSentinel and additional strains engineered with modifications to DNA repair pathways, to provide 
</p>
<p>data on how different components of cellular machinery interact to fix radiation-induced damage. In 
</p>
<p>addition, LEIA will include yeast strains from the BioNutrients experiment, a project to develop microbial 
</p>
<p>food sources that could provide astronauts with nutritional supplements produced on-demand during 
</p>
<p>long-duration missions. Specifically, LEIA will study whether yeast engineered to produce antioxidants 
</p>
<p>such as beta-carotene can maintain antioxidant production in the lunar environment. LEIA will also test 
</p>
<p>yeast strains that include genes introduced to increase radiation resistance. Extensive research is 
</p>
<p>underway to select flight strains that are robust enough to withstand pre-flight storage, launch, and 
</p>
<p>transit, but sensitive enough to provide a measurable biological response to the lunar environment. 
</p>
<p>LEIA is supported by a Payloads and Research Investigation on the Surface of the Moon (PRISM) Grant 
</p>
<p>and is scheduled for delivery to the Moon by the Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program in 
</p>
<p>2026. 
</p>

<p>HOTTech Attempts to Tackle Venus 
</p>
<p>Specialized test rig determines how new technologies fare in extreme 
</p>
<p>conditions 
</p>
<p>Project:  Hot Operating Temperature Technology (HOTTech) Program; NASA Glenn Extreme 
</p>
<p>Environment Rig (GEER) 
</p>
<p>Snapshot:  Projects in NASA’s HOTTech Program are developing technologies that will operate on the 
</p>
<p>surface of Venus for at least 60 days. Since previous Venus landers have only survived for several hours, 
</p>
<p>long-duration operation represents a paradigm shift in landed spacecraft technology. The GEER chamber 
</p>
<p>at NASA Glenn Research Center simulates Venus conditions and acts as a testbed for materials, 
</p>
<p>electronics, sensors, and power technologies for future long-duration exploration. 
</p>
<p> 
 
</p>
<p>As far as solar system exploration goes, the surface of Venus is an inhospitable place for technology. The 
</p>
<p>average temperature is almost 900F, high enough to melt metals like lead; the atmospheric pressure is 
</p>
<p>over 90 times that of Earth; and the atmosphere contains highly corrosive chemicals. Previous Venus 
</p>
<p>landers subjected to these harsh environmental conditions have only survived for several hours. To 
</p>
<p>overcome this challenge, the HOTTech Program is developing core technologies including electronics, 
</p>
<p>batteries, power systems, and actuators that will be able to operate on the Venus surface for at least 60 
</p>
<p>days. 
</p>
<p>The teams working on these technologies have been iteratively building and testing in laboratories 
</p>
<p>throughout the US over the last several years, but eventually the big question is: How would these 
</p>
<p>   
</p>

<p>technologies fare on Venus? Enter the Glenn Extreme Environment Rig (GEER), a nearly 20-ton chamber 
</p>
<p>at the NASA Glenn Research Center designed to simulate the conditions on the surface of Venus. The 
</p>
<p>chamber is massive to safely contain the Venus pressure. The GEER end cap, which slides opens on 
</p>
<p>railroad-like rails to enable set up of experiments, is subject to two million pounds of force as the Venus 
</p>
<p>surface pressure is simulated. Heaters keep the chamber and everything inside at the extreme 
</p>
<p>temperature of almost 900F. Furthermore, GEER can insert and monitor corrosive gases in the chamber 
</p>
<p>and ‘boost’ them as needed to match conditions expected on the Venus surface (these gases may react 
</p>
<p>with the technologies being tested!). Finally, the GEER team has incorporated electrical feedthroughs to 
</p>
<p>allow power and electrical signals to be transferred between the HOTTech technology inside the 
</p>
<p>chamber and external test equipment.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Several HOTTech project teams formed a community of practice to collaborate with each other and the 
</p>
<p>GEER team to improve and optimize their technologies. The goal is to eventually combine successful 
</p>
<p>individual components into increasingly capable systems. The technologies listed below were recently 
</p>
<p>tested together in GEER to learn how they might fare during long-term operations on Venus:

<p>The GEER test of these technologies ran from December 2022 through February 2023. Although the 
</p>
<p>technologists are still performing post-test analyses to evaluate how their technologies performed, 
</p>
<p>some encouraging initial results have already been noted.   
</p>
<p>Gaskets are critical components that seal the various mechanical and electrical systems on a spacecraft 
</p>
<p>or lander, preventing the ingress of dust, gas, and other contaminants. While developing a power 
</p>
<p>system for a future Venus lander, the Penn State team identified vermiculite as a potential gasket 
</p>
<p>material capable of surviving the extreme Venusian conditions. “Vermiculite is a mineral with excellent 
</p>
<p>thermal stability, a low coefficient of thermal expansion, and a great resistance to most corrosive 
</p>
<p>environments” said Dr. Christopher J. Greer, Assistant Research Professor Mechanical Engineering, Penn 
</p>
<p>State University. The GEER tests determined the ability of this vermiculite sealing material to hold back 
</p>
<p>the high pressure and temperature and withstand the corrosive conditions on Venus. 
</p>
<p>In a collaborative effort, three different groups tested electronic devices packaged by a fourth group in 
</p>
<p>this recent GEER test. One of those devices was a diamond diode provided by Dr. Nemanich, a member 
</p>
<p>of the Arizona State University team. He explains “Each hour, the computer-controlled system turned 
</p>
<p>the diamond diode on and recorded the current density (vs. voltage) in the forward and reverse 
</p>
<p>directions. The figure below presents a composite plot of all measurements during the 30 days. 
</p>
<p>Throughout the test, the diode showed a high rectification ratio (forward vs reverse current density), 
</p>
<p>which is consistent with the theoretical simulations.” This GEER test represents the first demonstration 
</p>
<p>of diamond-based electronics operating in simulated Venus surface conditions for an extended time, 
</p>
<p>and provides a foundation for a new type of electronics for future missions 
</p>

<p>A notable “first” achieved in the GEER test was the successful operation of electronic memory, which 
</p>
<p>allows scientific data to be stored until it can be transmitted using silicon carbide (SiC) electronics. The 
</p>
<p>picture below shows the pristine condition of the memory chip and packaging after the GEER test was 
</p>
<p>completed, as well as a snapshot of the electrical performance during the test. Dr. Phil Neudeck, NASA 
</p>
<p>GRC, remarked, “Even though this early prototype chip is just 16 bits, it nevertheless represents the first 
</p>
<p>random access memory ever to demonstrate successful operation for days while exposed to the 
</p>
<p>insanely harsh temperature, pressure, and reactive chemical environment found on the Venus surface. 
</p>
<p>The chip outlasted the Venus environment test duration. Such capability represents another step in the 
</p>
<p>march towards opening the Venus surface to the kind of prolonged robotic exploration that has proven 
</p>
<p>so scientifically fruitful on the surface of Mars.”   
</p>

<p>Other technologies were also tested in this latest GEER run. Results are still being analyzed and will be 
</p>
<p>the subject of future presentations and publications. As a whole, a wide range of technologies and 
</p>
<p>materials of differing levels of maturity were, in some cases, given their first GEER-simulated Venus 
</p>
<p>surface exposure. Some technologies gained a new level of demonstrated maturity, while developers of 
</p>
<p>others gained valuable lessoned learned about the harsh environment on the surface of Venus. This 
</p>
<p>collaborative GEER testing effort provided a foundation for future technology advancements that will 
</p>
<p>enable extended missions on the surface of Venus.
