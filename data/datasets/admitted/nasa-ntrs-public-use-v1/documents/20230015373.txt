<div class="slide-content"><p>*</p>
<p>Introduction: Varying Parameters</p>
<ul>	<li>Vehicle parameters are estimated during the design phases</li>
	<li>Uncertainties are relatively large</li>
	<li>Many of these parameters will be known better for a specific rocket</li>
	<li>We weigh the components for example</li>
	<li>Some things are better known on flight day</li>
	<li>Estimate of the winds for example</li>
	<li>Known parameters can be used to design the flight day trajectory</li>
	<li>There remains uncertainty on flight day </li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>*</p>
<p>Varying Vehicle Parameters</p>
<ul>	<li>Four types of variations</li>
	<li>1.  Uncertainty during the design phases</li>
	<li>How will the engines perform?</li>
	<li>What are the aerodynamic parameters?</li>
	<li>Uncertainty normally decreases with design maturity</li>
	<li>2.  Know about for the particular assembled vehicle</li>
	<li>Each manufactured element differs in weight and performance (thrust, Isp, solid burn rate), get estimates of these (for example, if the engine is tested prior to flight)</li>
	<li>Axial force coefficient will be known better by the time the vehicle flies (wind tunnel testing)</li>
	<li>Need to be able to meet requirements and design limits with these assembled vehicles, even early in the design phase</li>
	<li>Design the trajectory in simulation with these as “knowns”</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>*</p>
<p>Varying Vehicle Parameters</p>
<ul>	<li>Four types of variations</li>
	<li>3.  Know about on launch day</li>
	<li>Know for day of launch trajectory design and go/no go decision</li>
	<li>Temperature estimate (solid rocket motor)</li>
	<li>Propellant loading estimate</li>
	<li>Wind estimate</li>
	<li>4.  Don’t know on launch day</li>
	<li>Remaining uncertainties in all parameters (engine performance, aerodynamics, propellant loading, and more detailed parameters such as actuator modeling)</li>
	<li>If we throw all these into the same Monte Carlo simulation, we won’t get the right answer for anything (mission success, flight performance reserve, maximum dynamic pressure, thermal indicators, maximum acceleration, ….)</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Vehicle Models vs Flight Day Unknowns</p>
<p>All elements in Monte Carlo run</p>

	<li>Assume both pre-flight known items and flight day unknowns have the same standard deviation and are characterized by Normal distributions</li>
	<li>This example assumes you need to meet mission success requirements when launching a 3 sigma vehicle model</li>
</ul>

<div class="slide-content"><p>Some Vehicle Parameters of Interest</p>
<ul>	<li>Heavy/slow vehicle</li>
	<li>Lowest payload capability (or lowest remaining propellant)</li>
	<li>Longest time to clear the tower, most liftoff drift</li>
	<li>Most lofting, so may be worst abort conditions</li>
	<li>Light/fast vehicle</li>
	<li>Highest acceleration</li>
	<li>Highest dynamic pressure</li>
	<li>Highest bending loads</li>
	<li>Max temperatures</li>
	<li>Hybrid</li>
	<li>For some parameters, a different model might be appropriate</li>
	<li>For example, a booster stage with high performance and a high mass upper stage and payload might give driving compression loads</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="slide-content"><p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<p>Steps for the Vehicle Models</p>
<ul>	<li>1. Determine the partial derivatives of the desired varying outputs (z) with respect to each of the parameters that can vary (xi) but will be known for a given assembled vehicle (for example, the partial of dynamic pressure with respect to burn rate).</li>
	<li>Use the standard deviation for each parameter, from the uncertainty portion that will be known when the vehicle is assembled</li>
	<li>Generate the standard deviation for the composite impact of all the parameters</li>
<p></p>
<p></p>
<p></p>
<p></p>
	<li>2. Determine the target (z) values of each parameter (e.g. dynamic pressure, heat rate, acceleration, liftoff thrust/weight ratio, low fuel, etc.)</li>
	<li>Assuming the composite behaves as a Gaussian (Normal) distribution, we have</li>
</ul>
<p>					z = n sz</p>
<p>	where n is the desired sigma level, e.g. n = 3 corresponds to 99.865%. We typically use a 90% vehicle model so that we don’t have too much conservatism (3 sigma vehicle with 3 sigma flight day variations).</p>
<ul>	<li>3. Determine the vehicle model that uses the total set of parameters and maximizes the statistical chance of ending up with that vehicle.</li>
	<li>Constrained numerical optimization </li>
</ul>
<p>Target z values are the constraints (could be multiple targets for a vehicle model, e.g. all light/fast target params)</p>
<p>xi values are the variables to be chosen</p>
<p>Maximize the joint probability of the xi from the xi distributions (can be Normal, Uniform, etc.)</p>
<p>The more influential parameters will vary more</p>
<p>Some parameters will have big impacts, others not so much</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>*</p>
<p>Monte Carlo Procedure</p>
<ul>	<li>A wind estimate, a propellant temperature estimate (for solid motors), and liquid propellant loading estimates are known when the launch go/no go decision is made </li>
	<li>Procedure</li>
	<li>Define the vehicle model to match the z targets</li>
	<li>Run a Monte Carlo simulation to generate the desired percentage of natural environments case </li>
	<li>e.g. suppose we want to launch in a 95% bad temperature/wind combination</li>
	<li>Run Monte Carlo simulation for the remaining flight day uncertainties</li>
	<li>Includes remaining uncertainty in all the parameters used to define the vehicle model</li>
	<li>Includes remaining environmental uncertainties (e.g. winds)</li>
	<li>Do this for each stressing parameter, launch month, and mission scenario</li>
	<li>Show requirements are met for these cases</li>
	<li>Determine parameters such as Flight Performance Reserve required for mission success, as well as design-driving values.</li>
	<li>Typically hundreds of values of interest</li>
	<li></li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<p>Flight Control Parameters</p>
<ul>	<li>Aleatory uncertainty:  it really varies from flight to flight.  Example:  Winds, solid burn rate</li>
	<li>Epistemic uncertainty:  there is really only (approximately) a single value, but we don’t know it.  </li>
	<li>The vehicle parameters we have already separated are mathematically epistemic</li>
	<li>We don’t know them now in general, but will estimate the single value prior to flight.  So we need to cover for the variations in the single value.</li>
	<li>What about other epistemic values?</li>
	<li>e.g. aerodynamic moment coefficients, slosh mode frequency, first vibration mode. </li>
	<li>These remaining epistemic parameters are primarily impacts to flight control</li>
	<li>Flight control effects are highly nonlinear, so it would not be appropriate to just examine the bounds, or specific values (e.g. build a 90% vehicle model), of these parameters and assume all combinations would be covered</li>
	<li>Not feasible to run a Monte Carlo simulation with varying flight control parameters for each sample of flight day winds and vehicle models</li>
	<li>A reasonable approach seems to be:</li>
	<li>Monte Carlo run with all parameters included to determine the effects of parameter interactions (use the Monte Carlo runs we already have)</li>
	<li>Sensitivity studies with individual parameters to see where the system is sensitive and to ensure robustness</li>
	<li>Margin on flight control stability results</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>

<p>Requirements and Design Values</p>
<ul>	<li>Typical requirements statements specify probabilistic success percentages</li>
	<li>For example, 99.73% with 10% consumer risk (or 90% confidence)</li>
	<li>Or we want to design to a certain percentage worst case</li>
	<li>For example, 99.865% high maximum acceleration</li>
	<li>It doesn’t make sense to say 100%</li>
	<li>So, how do you capture a certain percentage value from a Monte Carlo simulation?</li>
	<li>The values we are looking for are among the last few samples in the tails of the data</li>
	<li>So there will be random variation in these values according to the samples we randomly select</li>
	<li>Fitting a distribution to the data is not generally satisfactory because we are interested in the outer few outliers</li>
	<li>The behavior of these outliers is generally not Gaussian</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<p>Order Statistics</p>
<ul>	<li>We develop a method here of addressing requirements success, and determining desired percentage values of parameters, using Order Statistics</li>
	<li>Order the results from smallest to largest and study the statistics of the outlier cases</li>
	<li>This development is not new to statistics theory</li>
	<li>This is also called “acceptance sampling” in quality engineering</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Examining Results of a Monte Carlo Run as Successes and Failures</p>
<p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<p>N is the number of samples</p>
<p>p is the actual failure probability</p>
<p>k is the number of failures</p>
<p>If we take 2000 samples, with differing failure probability, how many failures will we see? </p>
<p>It varies, and the chance of seeing exactly 5 failures is nearly the same in these cases</p>
<ul>	<li>  Given 5 failures in 2000 samples, what is the actual probability of failure?  It varies.</li>
	<li>  So, given a required success probability and a confidence level, how do we determine how many failures are allowed?</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Risk for Maximum Likelihood and 10% Risk</p>
<p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<ul>	<li> 0.25% is not the 50% risk/confidence level, as may be thought</li>
	<li> About a 61% chance that if our requirement is 0.25% failures, the actual failure rate is higher</li>
	<li> At about 0.47% failures the risk drops below 10%</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Consumer Risk and Producer Risk</p>
<ul>	<li>Required success:  99.73%</li>
	<li>Graphed: probability of seeing 2 or fewer failures if the actual failure probability is as specified on the x-axis</li>
	<li>At 0.27%, there is a 10% chance we would get 2 or fewer failures if we run 2000 samples (and accept a bad product if 99.73% success is the requirement).  This is consumer risk.</li>
</ul>
<p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<ul>	<li>There is a 90% chance we would get more than 2 failures and reject a good product (with 99.73% actual success).  This is producer risk (rejecting a good product).</li>
	<li>So we design for a lower percentage failure rate in order to reduce the producer risk.</li>
</ul>
<p>“Operating Characteristic” curve</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Consumer Risk and Producer Risk</p>
<p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<ul>	<li>Design for a lower failure probability to reduce producer risk</li>
	<li>Accept a higher failure probability to reduce consumer risk</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Consumer Risk and Producer Risk</p>
<p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<ul>	<li>Increasing the number of samples tightens the operating characteristic</li>
	<li>Less conservatism needed to meet the 10% consumer risk number</li>
	<li>If we ignore producer risk, we can use a smaller number of Monte Carlo samples but need a more conservative design</li>
</ul>
<ul>	<li>2/2000 = 0.001, 44/20000 = 0.0022, 509/200000 = 0.002545  </li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>

<div class="slide-content"><p>Sample of Number of MC Samples Required</p>
<p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<ul>	<li>Need more samples if we want to allow for a higher failure fraction (smaller producer risk)</li>
	<li>Need more samples for a higher percentage of success</li>
	<li>Need more samples for lower consumer risk</li>
	<li>If the number of samples possible is limited by expense, we have to accept proving a lower level of success, or higher consumer risk, or both (with higher producer risk)</li>
	<li>Number of samples does not depend on the number of input parameters</li>
</ul>

<div class="slide-content"><p>Order Statistics and Design Parameters</p>
<p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<ul>	<li>With “Order Statistics”, we simply order our 2000 results from lowest to highest</li>
	<li>The failure cases are those that exceed the limiting value (orbit insertion error, propellant remaining, roll attitude error, stage separation clearance, …..)</li>
	<li>How do we determine the 99.865% with 10% consumer risk maximum dynamic pressure, acceleration, …?</li>
</ul>
<p>Interpolate between the outliers assuming similar triangles</p>
<ul>	<li>If 1705 samples, the 99.865%/10% value is equal to the value of point 1705</li>
	<li>Along segment B-A, the 99.865%/10% value of accel is found by interpolating between point 2000 and point 1999</li>
</ul>
<p> a1999 + (a2000 - a1999) *</p>
<p> (2880 - 2000) / (2880 - 1705)</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="slide"><div class="slide-master-content"></div>
<div class="slide-content"><p>Why this Method is so Useful</p>
<p>*</p>
<p>National Aeronautics and Space Administration</p>
<ul>	<li>These results are based on binomial successes and failures and are not tied to the underlying distribution</li>
	<li>These results don’t depend on how many parameters are varying in the input to the Monte Carlo simulation </li>
	<li>So they hold for all distributions of the input/output data and for any number of inputs</li>
</ul>
