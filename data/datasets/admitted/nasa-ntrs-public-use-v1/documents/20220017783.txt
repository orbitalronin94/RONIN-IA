This paper presents an overview of the state-of-the-art two-phase CFD models that have 

been developed for various propellant tank storage and transfer operations in 1g, partial 

gravity and microgravity. The models are developed in the framework of the industry 

standard ANSYS/Fluent CFD code, the capabilities of which have been significantly enhanced 

and customized through incorporation of unique submodels via User Defined Functions 

(UDF)s to satisfy the requirements of its intended variable gravity Cryogenic Fluid 

Management (CFM) applications. These models/submodels have been validated against 

experimental data that cross spatial scales, fluid types, and gravity levels in order to properly 

anchor the models’ physical and numerical fidelity. The CFM application/processes that have 

been modeled include tank self-pressurization, tank autogenous pressurization, tank pressure 

control using both subcooled jet mixing and droplet spray injection mechanisms, tank 

chilldown/filling, tank drainage, tank slosh for both volatile and non-volatile fluids. The 

strength and shortcomings of the two-phase models for each application is highlighted and 

discussed briefly.

II. Introduction 

 

 Propellant tanks are subject to various undesirable heat loads while loitering in LEO, in transit, or sitting on the 

surface of Moon or Mars. The resultant heat leak that passes through the protective passive insulation system into the 

tank will evaporate the propellant, raise the tank pressure, and ultimately lead to fuel loss when vapor is vented in 

order to relieve the excess pressure1. Transverse temperature gradients generated by the heat leaks will also give rise 

to natural convection that may be core-driven and weak at low Ra numbers and boundary layer driven and turbulent 

at high Ra numbers2. If this natural convection current is not controlled, it will create significant thermal stratification 

leading to considerable evaporation at the interface. The natural convection regime and the intensity of the buoyancy-

driven flow and heat transfer, as characterized by the tank Ra number, is gravity-dependent. Consequently, there can 

be large quantitative and qualitative differences between microgravity and 1g thermal stratifications and self-

pressurization rates. In space, the position of the liquid-vapor interface is also not well defined and will be influenced 

by the direction and magnitude of the time-dependent residual gravitational field. The effect of the multiple time scales 

associated with these convective, transport, and phase change mechanisms have to be all captured, isolated, and 

characterized by the CFD models with great fidelity in order to properly predict the tank self-pressurization and 

pressure reduction time constants2, 3. 

 Self-pressurization and pressure control of the storage tanks in microgravity are complicated by the complex 

dynamic interactions among forced mixing, the various gravity-dependent transport mechanisms in the vapor and 

liquid phases, the condensation-evaporation processes that occur at the moving and deforming vapor-liquid interface 

and the dominance of surface forces over body forces in weightlessness3-9. This complicated underlying physics 

together with the prohibitive costs associated with a much-needed but elusive large scale on-orbit Technology 

Demonstration, have created an increased reliance on computational models to provide the required crossing between 

scales, fluid types, and gravitational levels to guide the future propellant tank designs2.  

 A proposed method for controlling and mitigating the thermal stratification is through mixing of the bulk liquid 

by an intermittent forced jet flow10-12.  Due to the low viscosity of the cryogenic fluids (e.g., hydrogen, oxygen, and 

methane) and the large dimensions of the storage tank, both the natural and the forced convection will be turbulent for 

typical on-surface applications and even in microgravity, for very large tanks13. The interaction between the forced jet 

and the ullage and its effect on destratification and interfacial heat and mass transfer is neither trivial nor intuitive in 

microgravity. 

 Another means of controlling the tank pressure is via injection of subcooled liquid droplets through an axial spray-

bar14. Here, the injected subcooled liquid can promote mixing and destratification in the liquid and condensation at 

the bulk phases’ interface. The droplets that are injected into the ullage can also provide additional cooling of the 

ullage as they vaporize and extract the required heat of vaporization from the hot vapor. Although promising, this 

technology has never been tested in microgravity.  Various liquid injection mechanisms have also been proposed for 

tank chilldown and filling operations. Here again, the droplet transport and phase change issues are important and 

even more complex as the interaction of droplet with a hot wall during the chilldown process must be simulated using 

two-phase conjugated CFD models15,16.  



3 

 

 During autogeneous tank pressurization17 for liquid extraction the pressurization of the ullage is accompanied by 

significant condensation at the bulk vapor-liquid interface creating a relatively thin thermal layer with a sharp gradient 

in the liquid just below the interface that must be captured by CFD with great fidelity in order to ultimately estimate 

the amount of gas injection that is required18. Again, both gravity and slosh as it affects the interaction of the convective 

gaseous and liquid flows with the phase front will play important roles in the process.  

 While ullage movement due to sudden thrust acceleration in space can produce pressure spikes in a stratified tank, 

liquid slosh due to similar accelerations can lead to vehement oscillation of the interface that can potentially lead to 

the disintegration of the liquid surface and creation of droplets. The sloshing process can thus promote significant 

interfacial condensation and droplet evaporation leading to sudden reduction in pressure and ultimately ullage 

collapse19-20. CFD simulations that can predict liquid slosh dynamics in microgravity with and without phase change 

are thus indispensable in the design of future tanks and mission scenarios21-22.  

 In this light, in order to support the on-orbit storage and transfer of cryogenic propellant operations for future 

Space expeditions, NASA has been investing in improving the predictive capabilities of computational models and 

CFD codes that will serve as design tools for sizing the equipment and predicting the performance of different 

propellant storage and transfer strategies. This has created a great reliance on two-phase Computational Fluid 

Dynamics (CFD) models to help acquire the needed scientific understanding of the interacting phenomena that affect 

the two-phase heat and mass transfer involved with cryogenic propellants. However, before the CFD models can be 

fully trusted to perform this important role, they have to be properly validated and verified against 1g and microgravity 

data, for simulation of each propellant tank operation. 

 In this paper we present an overview of the two-phase CFD models that have been developed for various propellant 

tank storage and transfer operations in the framework of the ANSYS/Fluent CFD code that has been customized by 

in-house developed submodels implemented through User Defined Functions (UDF)s. These models have been 

validated against experimental data that cross spatial scales, fluid types, and gravity levels in order to properly anchor 

the models’ physical and numerical fidelity. To date, the model development and validations studies have been focused 

on: 

• Tank Self-Pressurization where several CFD models have been developed and validated against large scale 

1g cryogenic fluid experiments carried out at NASA (MHTB, Ksite, Saturn AS-203, SHIIVER), small scale 

microgravity experiments using simulant fluids (ZBOT) and medium scale cryogenic fluid experiments in 

microgravity (RRM3) 

• Tank Pressure Control with both jet mixing and spray-bar droplet injection operations where CFD models 

have been developed and validated against large scale, 1g, cryogenic jet mixing experiment (Ksite) and large 

scale, 1g, cryogenic spray-bar/droplet injection experiment (MHTB) and microgravity small scale simulant 

fluid experiments (ZBOT). 

• Tank Sloshing with nonvolatile and volatile fluids where CFD models have been developed and validated 

against large scale and small, 1g, simulant and cryogenic sloshing experiments (CNES, JAXA) 

• Tank Pressurization and Draining where CFD models have been developed and validated against large 

scale, 1g, cryogenic experiment pressurized with like gases (EDU and DLR/University of Bremen) 

 In this article we focus on the CFM processes listed above and describe the status of state-of-the-art two-phase 

CFD models that have been developed at Case Western Reserve University and NASA Glenn Research Center (GRC) 

and validated against several large-scale ground-based and subscale microgravity self-pressurization and pressure 

control experiments. The validation case studies are presented and discussed based on a color bar chart that relates 

both the model and the experimental dataset fidelities to the provisions of NASA STD 7009 Document. We hope to 

cover the CFD models and simulations that address the other important CFM operations in future articles. 

III. Computational Modelling Approach  

 During the ZBOT-1 investigations, two different two-phase CFD models, namely a Sharp Interface, SI-CFD model 

and a Volume-of-Fluid, VOF-CFD model, were developed to study storage tank self-pressurization and jet mixing 

pressure control. Both of these models were subjects to comprehensive and detailed model verification and validation 

campaigns that include validation against the results of ZBOT-1 ground-based experiments23-24, ZBOT-1 microgravity 

experiments25, and large-scale LH2 experiments that were performed in the NASA Ksite6,12, MHTB14 and SHIIVER26 

ground-based cryogenic testbed facilities. For a more detailed presentation of the two associated mathematical models, 

the reader is referred to Kassemi et al3-4. In these CFD models, fluid flow and energy transport are described in terms 

of the continuity, Navier-Stokes, and enthalpy-based energy equations for both phases. The liquid phase is treated as 

incompressible with constant or variable temperature-dependent properties. The liquid density is allowed to vary 

linearly with temperature in the momentum equation. The vapor is modeled as a compressible ideal gas. All the 



4 

 

thermo-physical and thermodynamic properties of the fluids required by the models are taken the NIST Chemistry 

WebBook27. Different treatments of the interface as adopted by the sharp interface SI-CFD and VOF-SI models are 

briefly described below. 

 

A. The Sharp Interface SI-CFD Model

boundary that represents the interface. The governing mass, momentum, and energy equations are solved separately

of shear stresses and energy at the sharp interface.  The energy balance at the interface plays a particularly important 

role in two-phase flow problems. In our formulation, the net difference between the conductive heat fluxes on the 

vapor and liquid sides of the interface are computed, and in turn determine the energy that is supplied/removed to 

sustain the interfacial evaporation/condensation processes. The two phases are coupled at the interface through the 

continuity of velocities and shear stresses, as well as an energy balance performed at the interface boundary that can

IV. Results & Discussion  

An overview of key CFD model validation and verification work that has been performed, to date, at the National 

Center for Space Exploration Research (NCSER) at NASA Glenn Research Center (GRC) and Case Western Reserve 

University (CWRU) is presented in this section. All CFD simulations were performed using an in-house customized 

version of Ansys/Fluent CFD code as described previously. First, we will consider propellant tank storage and begin 

with presentation of four large cryogenic (Liquid Hydrogen, LH2) tank self-pressurization cases and one small 

simulant fluid (Perfluoro-n-Pentane, PnP) validation study. The experiments were all performed at NASA 

experimental facilities.  

A. The 1G Tank Self-Pressurization Validation Studies 

The first set of benchmark experiments considered for model validation were performed in the K-site facility at 

NASA Glenn Research Center6-7. The tank, in this case, is a flight-weight aluminum ellipsoidal enclosure with an 

internal volume of 4.95 m3 as shown in Fig 1a. It was insulated with two Multi-layer Insulation (MLI) blankets. Self-

Pressurization tests were performed from a stratified state in a cryo-shroud with 2 and 3.5 W/m2 heat loads. The 

pressurization and pressure control results of these experiments have been used to validate and verify our two-phase 

Storage Tank CFD models as described in detail in previous papers3,4,13.   

The VOF conjugated turbulent and laminar CFD models that include coupling between wall conduction and the 

CFD computations are used for these axisymmetric simulations. The predicted self-pressurization rate for the case 

where 3.5 W/m2 heat load is applied to the outer tank wall boundary are presented in Fig 1c. The results generated by 

the conjugated VOF_CFD model for this high Ra and high Bo number case [RaL= 1.65(10)14, RaV = 1.74(10)13, Bo = 

1.78(10)6] are shown for both laminar and turbulent simulations and compared against the measured pressure rise data 

plotted as solid circles. It is clearly evident that the turbulent analysis under-predicts the experimental pressure rise by 



7 

 

a considerable margin. In contrast, the laminar conjugated simulation provides a quite good agreement with the 

measured self-pressurization rate.  It seems that the laminar analysis captures the overall energy distribution in the 

tank with better fidelity.  

The corresponding laminar and turbulent temperature fields in the K-site tank are shown in Fig. 2d and 2e, 

respectively. The turbulent model simulation predicts an increased level of mixing that produces more uniform 

temperatures in both the liquid and ullage regions compared to the laminar simulations. The effect of turbulence in 

the vapor region is more pronounced where due to turbulent mixing and transport, the extent of ullage stratification 

predicted by the turbulent model is almost 40% less than those predicted by the laminar model.  

The evolution of temperature with time at two locations in the vapor and liquid regions as predicted by the turbulent 

and laminar simulations are compared to their experimental counterparts in Figs. 1f and 1g. It is clear from the results 

in Fig. 1f that the turbulent model under-predicts the vapor temperature while the laminar model agrees quite closely 

with the measurement. Surprisingly, Fig. 1g shows the opposite holds true for the temperature in the liquid region. 

Here, the turbulent model agrees well with the point temperature measurement while the laminar model noticeably 

under-predicts the experimental results. The temperature trends in Fig. 1 seem to indicate that the laminar model 

captures the heat transport in the ullage fairly well while the turbulent model provides a fairer estimate of mixing and 

heat transfer in the liquid region. 

The lack of fidelity of the turbulent model in predicting the measured pressurization rate can be explained as 

follows.  In the turbulent model predictions due to the vigorous turbulent mixing in the ullage it can draw a larger 

portion of the imposed heat through the wall into the vapor domain.  This heat does not remain in the ullage and due 

to the strong turbulent transport in the vapor and the large effective turbulent conductivities at the interface, a large 

portion of this heat reaches the interface. As a result, in the turbulent simulations the extent of thermal stratification 

in the vapor side is relatively low.  A sizable portion of the energy reaching the interface from the vapor side is used 

to supply the heat of evaporation at the interface. The net result is that in the turbulent simulations relatively small 

energy drawn from the wall remains on the vapor side causing reduced thermal stratification and hence the under-

prediction of self-pressurization rate compared to the experiment.   

The reason for turbulent model’s poor predictions seem to lie at the interface. An examination of the vapor and 

liquid side effective thermal conductivities at the interface for the laminar and turbulent models suggest that turbulent 

thermal conductivities are significantly larger than their molecular counterparts. This allows for overprediction of heat 

leaving the vapor phase. In reality the interface can dampen turbulence significantly and reduce the effective thermal 

conductivities at the interface. Unfortunately, the current implementation of the RANS model does not include this 

interfacial damping of turbulent energies.  Thus, it seems, that the lack of the fidelity of the turbulent VOF model in 

predicting the tank pressurization rate and thermal stratification is due to the absence of an empirically based turbulent 

“law of interface” in the current implementation of the RANS turbulent model within the VOF scheme. In this case, 

the laminar VOF model seems to be providing a better assessment of energy transport across the interface and in the 

ullage region based on the use of molecular conductivities at the interface.   

 Two other 1g large tank cryogenic validation studies are presented next. The first test was performed in the Multi-

Purpose Hydrogen Test Bed (MHTB) facility at NASA Marshall Space Flight Center (MSFC)14, the case examined 

here, corresponds to a 50% fill level and subject to a non-uniform heat flux distribution of 15.35 W (0.89873 W/m2) 

in the un-wetted section and 35.65 W (2.0841 W/m2) in the wetted surface15. This distribution was determined based

The initial state of the tank just before the pressure control simulations were obtained from previous self-pressurization 

simulations that were also validated by experimental results. A comparison of the tank depressurization predictions 

by the SI-CFD and VOF -CFD models against experimental results for this pressure control test is presented in Fig 5b 

and again points to the importance of interfacial turbulence on the evolution of tank pressure during the thermal

 In the second ground-based pressure control validation case, we focus on the MHTB LH2 experiment where the

slight but noticeable edge15, 31.

D. The 1G Tank Slosh Validation Studies – Volatile and Non-Volatile Fluids 

Two validation case studies were performed for a tank in 1g where the fluid goes through s relatively strong slosh in 

response to an imposed forced lateral acceleration. In the first case, the fluid is Silicone Oil. Since this is a nonvolatile 

fluid, there is no phase change during the slosh cycle. In the second case, the fluid is liquid Nitrogen and goes through 

phase change at the interface during the slosh period. Both experiments were performed by University of Tokyo19, 20 and 

shared with NASA during a NASA-JAXA model validation collaboration.  

Two-phase flow and heat transfer simulations of the silicone oil sloshing experiment by Himeno et al.19 in normal 

gravity were conducted using the VOF- CFD model. The simulations presented here represent the highest level of lateral 

acceleration tested in the experiment with a value of 0.5 G. The simulations were performed with and without the tank 

wall21. The later included a conjugate heat transfer analysis. A comparison of simulation results in Fig 9a illustrate the 

inability of the model without the tank wall to predict pressure increase in the tank at the end of sloshing that occurs 

mainly due to heat transfer from a warmer wall to a cooler gas. This underscores the importance of a conjugate heat 

transfer analysis in sloshing studies. The effect of turbulence modeling was also investigated. Here, the predictions of a 

Reynolds Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) turbulence model and those of a Large Eddy Simulation (LES) model were 

compared to the experimental slosh results. Comparisons in Fig 9a indicate that the LES approach follows the 

experimental trends noticeably better than the RANS model, especially, since it captures with good fidelity the change 

in the rate between the first and second pressure drops at around 0.75 secs in addition to the final increase in pressure that 

is greatly influenced by wall heat transfer as captured by the conjugated analysis. These results underscore the fact that, 

at this value of lateral acceleration, the initial temperature conditions in the tank wall have a lesser effect on the tank 

pressure evolution during sloshing, compared to the effect of turbulence and wall conduction. Thus, appropriate 

turbulence models are needed together with conjugated heat transfer simulations in order to predict the sloshing behavior 

of propellant tanks at moderate to high accelerations.  

Our next validation study examines the two-phase flow and heat transfer with phase change during the university of 

Tokyo’s Liquid Nitrogen sloshing experiments in normal gravity20. The simulations were again performed with the VOF-

CFD model with both LES and RANS turbulence submodels22. The validation case study considered two different 

experimental cases with different thermal stratification thickness at the liquid side of the interface. The effects of 

turbulence modeling, initial wall temperature profiles and the accommodation coefficient on the tank pressure evolution 

were investigated. The results of these simulations presented in Figs 9c and 9d indicate good agreement with the 

experimental results especially with regard to the extent of the rapid pressure drop. It also showed that while turbulence 

modeling approach does not have a significant effect on predicting the tank pressure, the effect of initial conditions and 

the value of accommodation coefficient that controls interfacial mass transfer play a significant role.

E. The Microgravity Tank Self-Pressurization and Jet-Mixing Pressure Control Validation Studies 

 We start the microgravity validation cases with the large-scale LH2 Saturn S-IVB AS-203 Space Demonstration  

that flied in 1966 and was able to conduct a self-pressurization experiment under a time-dependent acceleration field 

that varied between (3.7e-4 to 8e-5 g)38,39. The domed cylindrical tank had a radius, r = 3.3 m, and a height, h = 11.3 

m and test was performed at a fill level of 32% with an initial tank pressure of 12.4 psia (85495 Pa) and with the liquid 

initially at a uniform temperature of 19.72 K and the ullage at an initial superheat of roughly 2.44 K. Due to the large 

dimensions of the tank that resulted in a Bond number ranging from 43.5 – 145 due to the varying thruster acceleration, 

the liquid was essentially in a settled configuration for the entire duration of the self-pressurization test. The self-

pressurization test examined here did not take place under normal tank operations. Due to insulation failure a large 

amount of heat (approximately 30,000 W) was imparted on the tank. This is equivalent to a heat flux of about 150 

W/m2. Comparing this value to the nominal operational heat load of 3.4 W for K-site tank, it becomes apparent that 

the self-pressurization of the Saturn tank took place under severely off-nominal conditions due to the insulation failure.  

 The predictions of the VOF-CFD model40 for the Saturn IB AS-203 self-pressurization is presented in Fig 10a and 

compared to both the experimental results and to the numerical predictions by Grayson et al.41 generated by a Flow3D 

CFD model. Fig 10a shows the pressure evolution in the tank as a function of time.  Unfortunately, due to certain 

malfunctioning, the microgravity pressurization data is available only for limited durations in the beginning and end 

of the self-pressurization tests. Nonetheless the excellent agreement between the fluent-based VOF-CFD model with 

the experimental data and its noticeable edge over the Flow3D predictions is evident. Figs10c shows a comparison 

between the predictions of flow and thermal stratification in the tank by the Fluent and Flow3D models. The CFD 

results agree favorably with each other while it seems the Flow3D results predicts a stronger flow near the axis of the 

tank that may be due to the numerical limitations created by the axis of symmetry.  

 In Fig 10b VOF-CFD model predictions with and without interfacial phase change mass transfer are compared to 

the experimental results. The two predictions are on top of each other and precisely follow the experimental trend. It 

seems that mass transfer at the liquid/ullage interface has a negligible effect on the tank pressure rise for this 

experiment due to the relatively large wall heat fluxes that is directly imparted to the vapor in S-IVB LH2 tank. 

Consequently, most of the pressure rise occurs as a result of direct heating of the ullage. This is not the case for 

operation of most propellant tanks in microgravity. Since due to effective insulation system and heat shields propellant 

tanks typically receive about two orders of magnitude less heat directly from the environment. Under nominal 

operational conditions, due to the low thermal inertia of the vapor compared to the liquid, pressure rise in the tank is 

mostly influenced by the interfacial phase change mass transfer and not by direct heating of the ullage.  Thus, while 

Saturn S-IVB experiment constitutes a large tank cryogenic tank experiment in Space, its pressurization data is not 

suitable for validating the CFD models as it does not correctly represent the role of interfacial phase change mass 

transfer that is the main contributor to tank self-pressurization under normal Space conditions.

The final microgravity validations of CFD model are performed against the self-pressurization and pressure control 

data provided by the Zero Boil-Off Tank experiment that was undertaken in the 2017-2018 timeframe aboard the 

ISS25, 46. As described before the microgravity tests were performed in a small-scale transparent acrylic tank using, 

Perfluoro-n-Pentane (PnP), as the simulant transparent low boiling point phase change fluid. The validation of the SI-

A microgravity self-pressurization validation case is presented in Fig 13 where the tank is heated by the vacuum 

jacket heating, the tank fill level is 75% and it is subject to an effective gravitation field of 5(10)-6 g. In this case, the 

vacuum jacket is uniformly heated to maintain its temperature always 1 degree above the average temperature of the 

tank wall (see Fig. 13e). Consequently, as is evident from the flow field and the temperature contours in Fig. 13b, the 

tank is also almost uniformly heated by the heated vacuum jacket through both radiation and conduction heat transfer 

across a gap that is evacuated and maintained at a vacuum pressure around 0.01 Pa. This situation mimics global heat 

leaks into an actual storage tank from its Space environment. At this near vacuum pressure, molecular conduction is 

still an important heat transfer mechanism and of similar magnitude to the radiation heat transfer across the gap. 

The numerical simulations were generated by the axisymmetric SI-CFD model using the T-sat approach for 

calculating the evaporation–condensation mass transfer that occurs at the phase front. The axisymmetric numerical 

simulations indicate that during vacuum jacket heating, evaporation takes place at the top of the ullage near the hot 

wall while condensation ensues in the bottom portion of the ullage, where the interface is next to the subcooled liquid. 

As a result, there is a net flow of vapor from the top of the ullage towards the bottom section of the interface. This 

mass transfer flow in the ullage drives clockwise vortices in the liquid next to the phase front that isolate the ullage 

from the weak natural convection vortices in the bottom of the tank which are driven by the residual gravitational 

acceleration. As the natural convection currents gain strength upon self-pressurization, they can compress the two 

upper vortices considerably against the ullage. This intricate flow structure is quite weak – as a result the mode of heat 

transfer through the liquid is dominated by conduction. The hottest part of the ullage is at the top, close to the wall, 

where evaporation takes place. The coldest section is at the bottom interface where heat is lost to the liquid and 

condensation ensues. However, the net mass transfer is still evaporative and as a result after an initial delay, the ullage 

pressure increases, and the self-pressurization rate approaches a constant slope after about six hours of heating as 

shown in Fig 13e. The axisymmetric model’s predictions of tank pressure and of average tank wall temperature 

evolutions are compared to their experimental counterparts in Fig. 13d and 13e respectively and indicate an excellent

agreement. Comparison between the predicted and measured local temperatures at various RTD location on the tank 

wall and in the fluid are shown in Figs. 13f-i and again show excellent agreement. These results provided the first 

successful validation of a CFD model predictions against undisturbed microgravity self-pressurization data in a sealed 

(zero-boil-off) tank. 

Finally, we will focus attention on validation of the CFD models against the ZBOT microgravity subcooled jet 

mixing tests. In the validation case considered here, comparisons of the predicted and experimental evolution of fluid 

flow, ullage deformation and movement, and tank pressure drop created by a 10 cm/sec (To-1) subcooled jet flow in 

a 91% filled tank during forced jet mixing pressure control in microgravity is presented. The simulations were 

performed by our 3D VOF-CFD model using both LES and RANS-SST turbulent submodels and direct comparisons 

were made with their experimental white light images and particle streak images counterparts captured during unique 

Particle-Image-Velocimetry (PIV) measurements in microgravity. 

The comparison between the predictions of our two-phase VOF-CFD model using the RANS and LES turbulence 

submodels and their experimental counterparts are presented in Figs 14 and 15. From Fig 14, it becomes evident that 

by capturing the complex large eddy structure of the turbulent mixing flow with numerical and physical accuracy, the 

LES VOF-CFD model can predict the influence of the jet on the shape, deformation, and movement of the ullage 

under microgravity conditions with impressive fidelity. However, the RANSVOF-CFD model, which integrates the 

detailed effects of the turbulent eddies in favor of faster engineering computations, predicts the settling of the ullage 

at an incorrect position in the test tank at t = 38.7 s, and misses entirely the correct initial dynamic ullage behavior.  

The evolution of the tank pressure during the first 6 minutes of the 10 cm/sec (To-1) subcooled jet mixing test is 

shown in 15a. The experimental results show that in the first 20 s of subcooled jet mixing, the tank pressure goes 

through an initial rapid drop followed by a more gradual decline. The RANS CFD model does not capture this initial 

drop, while the LES model predicts it quite well. This is to be expected, since, as shown previously, the RANS model 

is not able to simulate the ullage deformation and movement with fidelity and predicts a premature settling of the 

ullage into an incorrect stationary position. However, the long-term pressure drop in the tank (30 mins) occurs when 

the ullage is hovers around its final position in the middle of the tank as in Fig 14d. Therefore, it is governed more by 

the net removal of heat from the test cell through the mixing and cooling process rather than by the initial jet impact 

and ullage deformation and movement. Thus, as shown in Fig 15b, the RANS model provides a good representation 

of the long-term cooling behavior and pressure decline rate of the ullage. This is good news for practical engineering 

applications, since, due to the overwhelming computational burden, it is not possible to model the whole long term 

cooling-depressurization interval with the LES VOF-CFD model. 

V. Conclusion 

A comprehensive overview of recent model validation case studies of propellant tank pressurization and pressure 

control was presented. The two main CFD models, namely, SI-CFD and VOF-CFD, that have been validated and 

discussed were developed through in-house development and customization in the framework of the industry-standard 

Ansys/Fluent multi-purpose CFD code. A diverse set of 1g and microgravity experiments were selected for validation 

in order to anchor the models’ fidelity across fluid types, gravity environments, and spatial scales. The results are 

promising and show the established credibility and reliability of the models for engineering predictions and increasing 

the confidence in the CFD models to be used as effective and reliable computational tools for the design of future 

propellant tanks for in-orbit or on-surface applications. From a scientific point of view, the CFD models are still 

lacking in two important areas:  

1. Delineating the dissipating influence of the interface on turbulence and its impact on interfacial mass, 

momentum, and heat transfer during pressurization and pressure control.  

2. Correctly predicting the interfacial phase change mass transfer for nonequilibrium situations or dynamic 

conditions with major interfacial deformations and fragmentation where kinetics may dictate interfacial 

mass transfer with hard to specify accommodation coefficients. Here, the use of accommodation 

coefficients as a tuning parameter has so far provided only an engineering solution for the VOF models.
