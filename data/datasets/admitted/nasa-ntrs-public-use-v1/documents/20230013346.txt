As the advancement of In-Situ Resource Utilization concepts and systems continue to 



develop, applicable technology development and maturation continues in parallel. While there 



are many different ways to use the resources found on other bodies, one of the most prevalent 



suggested applications is the manufacturing of propellants. One of the key technologies for the 



eventual use of these propellant production based ISRU systems is the liquefaction and storage 



of the produced propellants. The most mentioned propellant combinations include oxygen-



hydrogen and oxygen-methane. The liquefaction of oxygen in these systems will be different 



than that on Earth, which mainly revolves around the distillation of air. These systems have 



been developed conceptually and many of the components have been previously tested or are 



in development. However, the need to demonstrate the system level operations still exists. The 



demonstration of a prototypical oxygen liquefaction system using tube-on-tank broad area 



cooling was completed to better understanding system level operations during liquefaction 



activities. Demonstration testing included system performance determination, constant 



liquefaction demonstrations, and transient liquefaction demonstrations. The demonstrations 



showed the operational capabilities of the tube-on-tank system with an integrated cryocooler. 



Additional testing explored subsurface vs ullage introduction of the gaseous oxygen flow 



stream as well as demonstrating a novel fiber optic sensor that measured the temperature 



gradients along the fluid center line within the tank. 



I. Introduction 



The production of cryogenic fluids for use as rocket propellant on surfaces beyond the Earth has long been a sought-



after sustainable capability for both NASA and the space industry. The liquefaction and storage of these fluids on the 



surface of the Moon or Mars is a key technology that needs development for the production cycle to work. While the 



liquefaction of cryogenic fluids has well developed operations on the surface of the Earth, initial small scale production 



systems in extraterrestrial locations may look and operate differently, requiring new techniques and capabilities1,2. To 



begin addressing this need, a series of tests were developed. Each test improved the system level maturation of the 



liquefaction and storage capability by either improving the fidelity of the hardware or testing the hardware in more

II. Experimental System 



As shown in Figure 1, the test used an industrial cryocooler system consisting of pulse tube cryocoolers and a cryofan 



that drove a forced convection loop of neon through tubes welded to the outer surface of an aluminum tank. The tank 



liquefaction flow was fed by an external oxygen trailer upstream from a flow controller. The storage tank was 



contained within a thermal vacuum chamber. The neon flow across the tank surface was oriented top to bottom, which 



significantly reduced thermal stratification within the tank to less than one Kelvin and allowed for the most efficient 



use of the refrigeration power. The tank was approximately half scale of a Lunar or Mars lander tank by surface area 



and thus the oxygen design flow rate was approximately half scale of the target liquefaction rate4. The design oxygen 



liquefaction rate was 1.1 kg/hr. When set to its best performance the test system was able to achieve 1.6 kg/hr at a 



constant tank pressure steady state over a 48 hour period.

III.Results and Sensitivities 



Three different control operations were demonstrated. Setting an oxygen flow rate, environmental temperature, and 



cryocooler configuration while allowing the pressure of the system to respond to these inputs was the main operational 



approach. A second operational approach was controlling the tank pressure by varying the incoming gaseous oxygen 



flow rate and holding the environmental temperature and cryocooler configuration constant. The third control 



technique varied the neon loop heater power (representative of variations to cryocooler heat removal) to control the 



tank pressure. All three control operations were successful and could easily be implemented on a flight system. It 



was determined that it is easier to validate nodal models developed using SINDA/FLUINT to the first form of control, 



where the pressure is allowed to vary. 



Testing was focused on three main areas: determination of nominal operation variables of the liquefaction system, 



understanding steady state sensitivities on the nominal operation of the system, and transient operations of the system. 



The main variables that were allowed to change from nominal operations were the tank heaters (simulating variations 



in parasitic heat load), neon heaters (simulating variations in cryocooler input power), gaseous oxygen flow rate (as 











3 



 



might be driven by the balance of a propellant production plant), oxygen saturation pressure, environmental 



temperature, fill level, and location of gaseous oxygen insertion. 



Tests were run both inserting gas into the ullage and liquid (through a dip tube) while varying the neon loop heater 



to simulate the cryocooler power changing with the liquid level less than 5% full. Operationally the ullage and dip 



tube insertion liquefaction tests were run the same. The only variance was the gaseous oxygen’s point of entry. The 



time response of the liquefaction rate control when the tank was in constant pressure mode and the gas was inserted 



into the ullage is shown in Figure 2. The tank pressure is seen to vary less than 1 kPa and the flow control system 



takes on the order of two hours to stabilize the flow rate at the new heater power setting. Figure 3 plots those 



liquefaction rates as a function of neon loop heat power setting for both ullage and dip tube oxygen insertion. At all 



heater power levels, the ullage insertion performed better than the dip tube insertion. Theoretically, the inverse of the 



slope of the lines should be approximately the change in enthalpy of the oxygen between the insertion temperature 



and liquid saturation enthalpy (all of these tests were run at 207 kPa). The inverse of the ullage insertion is 376 J/g 



while the inverse of the dip tube insertion is 334 J/g. The change in enthalpy for all of the tests was approximately 



380 J/g using a temperature sensor in the vent line as the incoming temperature, less than 2% off of the slope of the 



ullage insertion data, but a puzzling 12% higher than the dip tube slope. This would suggest that even though the dip 



tube testing had a lower change in enthalpy, the liquefaction rate was lower, a puzzling contradiction assuming the 



cryocooler system was in a similar configuration for both test series.

For varying the oxygen saturation pressure, three different saturation pressures were chosen: 138 kPa (20 psia), 



207 kPa (30 psia), and 276 kPa (40 psia). Figure 4 shows the liquefaction rate as a function of oxygen saturation 



pressure with the cryocooler settings essentially unchanged. At 276 kPa, the liquefaction rate was 17% higher than at 



138 kPa. Table 1 shows the tabulated results and compares the relative difference in liquefaction rate to the relative 



difference in enthalpy change for gas being inserted at 300 K. While the enthalpy rate would be expected to only 



change by roughly 4%, the change in liquefaction rate of 17% indicates that even with the small change in temperature 



(from 93 K at 138 kPa to 101 K at 276 kPa) was enough to affect the cryocooler efficiency significantly. Calculating 



the Carnot efficiencies for a 93 K and 101 K refrigeration system with 300 K heat rejection, the ideal efficiency jumps 



from 44.9% at 93 K to 50.8% at 101 K, a change of approximately 12%. The combined 4% change in enthalpy and 



12% change in expected cryocooler efficiency increase approaches the measured 17% change in liquefaction rate. The 



second observation from Figure 4 is that the pressure of the neon cryocooler loop in this configuration plays an 



important role. During Test 10 (the 138 kPa liquefaction test), it was noticed that the pressure in the neon loop had 



decreased by 30-40 kPa (nominal starting pressure of 483 kPa (70 psia)), upon refilling the loop, the liquefaction rate 



was increased by about 10%. This was calculated to be mainly driven by the increased density of the neon causing a 



higher flow rate in the neon loop and thus a higher cryocooler heat removal capability.

Once nominal operations of the system were demonstrated, system level sensitivities were tested. One of the tests 



evaluated tank pressurization rate as both a function of oxygen flow rate and tank pressure. As can be seen in Figure 



5, the main variable in the pressurization rate is the incoming gaseous oxygen flow rate (initial tests run at fill levels 











5 



 



less than 5% full). However, the scatter in the data, especially at low flow rates, can be attributable to tank pressure 



as shown in Figure 6. The linear behavior of this response allowed the test team to predict and then perform repeatable 



transient tests where a flow rate of 0.44 g/s for 7 hours was selected for daytime operations that drove the tank pressure 



up and then a second flow rate of 0.24 g/s for 17 hours that brought the tank back down to its original pressure. This 



simulated a situation where the plant might be driven to a more cyclical production rate due to power balance 



challenges (such as day-night cycles) and its results are plotted in Figure 7. These variations also averaged out to the 



target flow rate of 1.1 kg/hr of liquefaction over the full 24-hour period. The cycle was repeated three times to show 



the stability of the response of the system. The results are shown in Table 2, with the pressurization rates being within 



6.2% of the average for each cycle and the depressurization rates within 5.2% of the average for each cycle. This flow 



transient was then repeated at > 90 % full tank fill level with similarly repeatable results. Figure 8 shows the results 



at approximately 90% full for three cycles of ullage gas insertion followed by a single cycle of dip tube gas insertion. 



For the ullage gas insertion cycles, the pressurization and depressurization rates are noted to be much less linear and 



as opposed to the 5% full transient cases, stratification was observed in the vapor space during the times of 



pressurization. Figure 9 breaks out the pressurization and depressurization rates by time periods to be able to compare 



them more closely. These breakouts show that most of the variation in response was seen in the first time period (Cycle 



3 was > 10% different than the other two) where the initial pressurization and depressurization spikes occurred in the 



test pressurization rates. The rest of the rates were within 10% of each other. The fourth cycle, using the dip tube, had 



a very different response. With the fill level so high, the dip tube served to essentially mix the incoming gas within 



the liquid and slowly increase the pressure as the system stayed on the saturation curve as opposed to the ullage 



becoming superheated as had occurred in the first three cycles. This indicates it may be preferential to use dip tube 



insertion in a transient situation where the fill level is sufficiently high. 



Similar steady state parametric and transient tests were performed with varying environmental temperature of the 



thermal vacuum chamber and varying neon heater powers to simulate the changing of cryocooler heat removal 



representative of variances in cryocooler input power.

Results of the experimental fiber optics sensors monitoring temperature change throughout each of the liquefaction 



tests are promising, with close agreement with co-locating silicon diodes, as shown in Figure 11 for the duration of a 



period where the oxygen system was being increased in saturation pressure. The stratification in the system is evident 



during the times where the tank was pressurizing. Figure 12 shows the ullage response on the silicon diode and 



similarly placed fiber grating at the 95% fill level during transient mass flow testing at high fill levels. The difference 



in stratification can be seen between the first three spikes and the fourth hump (around 80 hours). Figure 13 shows the 



temperature as a function of fill level for several of the time slices within that test. The stratification within the system 



is much more evident using the fiber optic system than in the much more widely spaced silicon diodes. 



Within Figure 13, in the plot of the 3rd hour elapsed time, only the top few silicon diodes show stratification at the 



ullage, but the fiber sensors show more of consistent temperature change trend from ullage to liquid level. By the 5 th 



hours, when tank starts to depressurize as the flow slows down, both temperature distributions return to an unstratified 



profile. The stratification can be seen again at 20th and 51st hour of elapsed time, from the second and third high flow 



cycle, respectively.

Results of the oxygen testing confirmed many of the results of previous nitrogen testing: (i) fill level was not a 



large driver in system operations (with the exception of pressure response swings above 90% full), (ii) at low fill 



levels, ullage insertion of the gas created a higher liquefaction rate than dip tube insertion, (iii) transient pressure 



responses were much smaller when bubbling oxygen through the liquid than when inserting into the ullage of the tank, 



and (iv) the tank could be filled to greater than 95% full without having to reduce the oxygen flow rate while holding 



tank pressure constant. New lessons learned included the sensitivity of the system to neon loop pressure and 



temperatures and pressure sensitivities of liquefaction rates. 



IV. Conclusion 



The CryoFILL testing demonstrated liquefaction of up to 1.6 kg/hr of oxygen coming into a tank. Performance trends 



as a function of tank pressure, fill level, and injection method was established. Steady state performance was 



successfully used to predict transient system operations. Multiple different transient cases were run with the test 



hardware to demonstrate preferential operational strategies that might be used in transient operations. The FOSS 



system was successfully demonstrated in a liquid oxygen environment to provide a much higher detailed view of the 



temperature stratification of the oxygen. 



This CryoFILL series of testing met all of its performance goals (see Table 3). The next step is to incorporate a 



more flight-like cryocooler to better understand the cryocooler driven responses on the system. Eventually the 



additional pieces of a Lunar or Mars surface oxygen production system including electrolyzers to separate water and 



associated processing hardware could be incorporated into this system.
