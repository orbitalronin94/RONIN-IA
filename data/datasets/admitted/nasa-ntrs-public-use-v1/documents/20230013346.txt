<p>Cryogenic Fluid In-Situ Liquefaction for 
</p>
<p>Landers: Prototype Demonstration 
</p>
<p>Wesley L Johnson1, Ryan J Grotenrath2 
 NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH 44135, USA  
</p>
<p> 
Ramaswamy Balasubramaniam3 
</p>
<p> Case Western Reserve University, NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, 44135, USA 
</p>
<p>Hon Mon Chan4 
NASA Armstrong Flight Research Center, Edwards, CA, 44135, USA 
</p>
<p>James W Smith5, Patrick A Giddens6 
 NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, AL 35812, USA  
</p>
<p>As the advancement of In-Situ Resource Utilization concepts and systems continue to 
</p>
<p>develop, applicable technology development and maturation continues in parallel. While there 
</p>
<p>are many different ways to use the resources found on other bodies, one of the most prevalent 
</p>
<p>suggested applications is the manufacturing of propellants. One of the key technologies for the 
</p>
<p>eventual use of these propellant production based ISRU systems is the liquefaction and storage 
</p>
<p>of the produced propellants. The most mentioned propellant combinations include oxygen-
</p>
<p>hydrogen and oxygen-methane. The liquefaction of oxygen in these systems will be different 
</p>
<p>than that on Earth, which mainly revolves around the distillation of air. These systems have 
</p>
<p>been developed conceptually and many of the components have been previously tested or are 
</p>
<p>in development. However, the need to demonstrate the system level operations still exists. The 
</p>
<p>demonstration of a prototypical oxygen liquefaction system using tube-on-tank broad area 
</p>
<p>cooling was completed to better understanding system level operations during liquefaction 
</p>
<p>activities.  Demonstration testing included system performance determination, constant 
</p>
<p>liquefaction demonstrations, and transient liquefaction demonstrations.  The demonstrations 
</p>
<p>showed the operational capabilities of the tube-on-tank system with an integrated cryocooler.  
</p>
<p>Additional testing explored subsurface vs ullage introduction of the gaseous oxygen flow 
</p>
<p>stream as well as demonstrating a novel fiber optic sensor that measured the temperature 
</p>
<p>gradients along the fluid center line within the tank. 
</p>
<p>I.  Introduction 
</p>
<p>The production of cryogenic fluids for use as rocket propellant on surfaces beyond the Earth has long been a sought-
</p>
<p>after sustainable capability for both NASA and the space industry. The liquefaction and storage of these fluids on the 
</p>
<p>surface of the Moon or Mars is a key technology that needs development for the production cycle to work. While the 
</p>
<p>liquefaction of cryogenic fluids has well developed operations on the surface of the Earth, initial small scale production 
</p>
<p>systems in extraterrestrial locations may look and operate differently, requiring new techniques and capabilities1,2. To 
</p>
<p>begin addressing this need, a series of tests were developed. Each test improved the system level maturation of the 
</p>
<p>liquefaction and storage capability by either improving the fidelity of the hardware or testing the hardware in more 
</p>

<p>realistic environments. Initial Brassboard testing of the liquefaction operational system was completed by Valenzuela3. 
</p>
<p>Most recently, the second in the series of liquefaction tests that were performed improved the fidelity of the test 
</p>
<p>hardware.  
</p>
<p>II. Experimental System 
</p>
<p>As shown in Figure 1, the test used an industrial cryocooler system consisting of pulse tube cryocoolers and a cryofan 
</p>
<p>that drove a forced convection loop of neon through tubes welded to the outer surface of an aluminum tank.  The tank 
</p>
<p>liquefaction flow was fed by an external oxygen trailer upstream from a flow controller. The storage tank was 
</p>
<p>contained within a thermal vacuum chamber. The neon flow across the tank surface was oriented top to bottom, which 
</p>
<p>significantly reduced thermal stratification within the tank to less than one Kelvin and allowed for the most efficient 
</p>
<p>use of the refrigeration power. The tank was approximately half scale of a Lunar or Mars lander tank by surface area 
</p>
<p>and thus the oxygen design flow rate was approximately half scale of the target liquefaction rate4.  The design oxygen 
</p>
<p>liquefaction rate was 1.1 kg/hr. When set to its best performance the test system was able to achieve 1.6 kg/hr at a 
</p>
<p>constant tank pressure steady state over a 48 hour period. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>III.Results and Sensitivities 
</p>
<p>Three different control operations were demonstrated. Setting an oxygen flow rate, environmental temperature, and 
</p>
<p>cryocooler configuration while allowing the pressure of the system to respond to these inputs was the main operational 
</p>
<p>approach.  A second operational approach was controlling the tank pressure by varying the incoming gaseous oxygen 
</p>
<p>flow rate and holding the environmental temperature and cryocooler configuration constant. The third control 
</p>
<p>technique varied the neon loop heater power (representative of variations to cryocooler heat removal) to control the 
</p>
<p>tank pressure.  All three control operations were successful and could easily be implemented on a flight system.  It 
</p>
<p>was determined that it is easier to validate nodal models developed using SINDA/FLUINT to the first form of control, 
</p>
<p>where the pressure is allowed to vary. 
</p>
<p>Testing was focused on three main areas: determination of nominal operation variables of the liquefaction system, 
</p>
<p>understanding steady state sensitivities on the nominal operation of the system, and transient operations of the system.  
</p>
<p>The main variables that were allowed to change from nominal operations were the tank heaters (simulating variations 
</p>
<p>in parasitic heat load), neon heaters (simulating variations in cryocooler input power), gaseous oxygen flow rate (as </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>3 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>might be driven by the balance of a propellant production plant), oxygen saturation pressure, environmental 
</p>
<p>temperature, fill level, and location of gaseous oxygen insertion.  
</p>
<p>Tests were run both inserting gas into the ullage and liquid (through a dip tube) while varying the neon loop heater 
</p>
<p>to simulate the cryocooler power changing with the liquid level less than 5% full. Operationally the ullage and dip 
</p>
<p>tube insertion liquefaction tests were run the same. The only variance was the gaseous oxygen’s point of entry. The 
</p>
<p>time response of the liquefaction rate control when the tank was in constant pressure mode and the gas was inserted 
</p>
<p>into the ullage is shown in Figure 2.  The tank pressure is seen to vary less than 1 kPa and the flow control system 
</p>
<p>takes on the order of two hours to stabilize the flow rate at the new heater power setting.  Figure 3 plots those 
</p>
<p>liquefaction rates as a function of neon loop heat power setting for both ullage and dip tube oxygen insertion. At all 
</p>
<p>heater power levels, the ullage insertion performed better than the dip tube insertion. Theoretically, the inverse of the 
</p>
<p>slope of the lines should be approximately the change in enthalpy of the oxygen between the insertion temperature 
</p>
<p>and liquid saturation enthalpy (all of these tests were run at 207 kPa). The inverse of the ullage insertion is 376 J/g 
</p>
<p>while the inverse of the dip tube insertion is 334 J/g.  The change in enthalpy for all of the tests was approximately 
</p>
<p>380 J/g using a temperature sensor in the vent line as the incoming temperature, less than 2% off of the slope of the 
</p>
<p>ullage insertion data, but a puzzling 12% higher than the dip tube slope. This would suggest that even though the dip 
</p>
<p>tube testing had a lower change in enthalpy, the liquefaction rate was lower, a puzzling contradiction assuming the 
</p>
<p>cryocooler system was in a similar configuration for both test series. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

</p>
<p>variation testing.

<p>For varying the oxygen saturation pressure, three different saturation pressures were chosen: 138 kPa (20 psia), 
</p>
<p>207 kPa (30 psia), and 276 kPa (40 psia). Figure 4 shows the liquefaction rate as a function of oxygen saturation 
</p>
<p>pressure with the cryocooler settings essentially unchanged. At 276 kPa, the liquefaction rate was 17% higher than at 
</p>
<p>138 kPa.  Table 1 shows the tabulated results and compares the relative difference in liquefaction rate to the relative 
</p>
<p>difference in enthalpy change for gas being inserted at 300 K.  While the enthalpy rate would be expected to only 
</p>
<p>change by roughly 4%, the change in liquefaction rate of 17% indicates that even with the small change in temperature 
</p>
<p>(from 93 K at 138 kPa to 101 K at 276 kPa) was enough to affect the cryocooler efficiency significantly. Calculating 
</p>
<p>the Carnot efficiencies for a 93 K and 101 K refrigeration system with 300 K heat rejection, the ideal efficiency jumps 
</p>
<p>from 44.9% at 93 K to 50.8% at 101 K, a change of approximately 12%. The combined 4% change in enthalpy and 
</p>
<p>12% change in expected cryocooler efficiency increase approaches the measured 17% change in liquefaction rate. The 
</p>
<p>second observation from Figure 4 is that the pressure of the neon cryocooler loop in this configuration plays an 
</p>
<p>important role.  During Test 10 (the 138 kPa liquefaction test), it was noticed that the pressure in the neon loop had 
</p>
<p>decreased by 30-40 kPa (nominal starting pressure of 483 kPa (70 psia)), upon refilling the loop, the liquefaction rate 
</p>
<p>was increased by about 10%.  This was calculated to be mainly driven by the increased density of the neon causing a 
</p>
<p>higher flow rate in the neon loop and thus a higher cryocooler heat removal capability. 
</p>

</p>

<p>Once nominal operations of the system were demonstrated, system level sensitivities were tested. One of the tests 
</p>
<p>evaluated tank pressurization rate as both a function of oxygen flow rate and tank pressure.  As can be seen in Figure 
</p>
<p>5, the main variable in the pressurization rate is the incoming gaseous oxygen flow rate (initial tests run at fill levels </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>5 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>less than 5% full).  However, the scatter in the data, especially at low flow rates, can be attributable to tank pressure 
</p>
<p>as shown in Figure 6.  The linear behavior of this response allowed the test team to predict and then perform repeatable 
</p>
<p>transient tests where a flow rate of 0.44 g/s for 7 hours was selected for daytime operations that drove the tank pressure 
</p>
<p>up and then a second flow rate of 0.24 g/s for 17 hours that brought the tank back down to its original pressure. This 
</p>
<p>simulated a situation where the plant might be driven to a more cyclical production rate due to power balance 
</p>
<p>challenges (such as day-night cycles) and its results are plotted in Figure 7. These variations also averaged out to the 
</p>
<p>target flow rate of 1.1 kg/hr of liquefaction over the full 24-hour period. The cycle was repeated three times to show 
</p>
<p>the stability of the response of the system. The results are shown in Table 2, with the pressurization rates being within 
</p>
<p>6.2% of the average for each cycle and the depressurization rates within 5.2% of the average for each cycle.  This flow 
</p>
<p>transient was then repeated at &gt; 90 % full tank fill level with similarly repeatable results. Figure 8 shows the results 
</p>
<p>at approximately 90% full for three cycles of ullage gas insertion followed by a single cycle of dip tube gas insertion. 
</p>
<p>For the ullage gas insertion cycles, the pressurization and depressurization rates are noted to be much less linear and 
</p>
<p>as opposed to the 5% full transient cases, stratification was observed in the vapor space during the times of 
</p>
<p>pressurization. Figure 9 breaks out the pressurization and depressurization rates by time periods to be able to compare 
</p>
<p>them more closely. These breakouts show that most of the variation in response was seen in the first time period (Cycle 
</p>
<p>3 was &gt; 10% different than the other two) where the initial pressurization and depressurization spikes occurred in the 
</p>
<p>test pressurization rates. The rest of the rates were within 10% of each other. The fourth cycle, using the dip tube, had 
</p>
<p>a very different response.  With the fill level so high, the dip tube served to essentially mix the incoming gas within 
</p>
<p>the liquid and slowly increase the pressure as the system stayed on the saturation curve as opposed to the ullage 
</p>
<p>becoming superheated as had occurred in the first three cycles.  This indicates it may be preferential to use dip tube 
</p>
<p>insertion in a transient situation where the fill level is sufficiently high.  
</p>

</p>
<p>thermal vacuum chamber and varying neon heater powers to simulate the changing of cryocooler heat removal 
</p>
<p>representative of variances in cryocooler input power.

</p>
<p>configured for a maximum liquefaction rate by raising the tank pressure to 276 kPa (40 psia) and turning off the system 
</p>
<p>heaters.  In that configuration, as seen in Figure 10, the system achieved 0.434 g/s (1.56 kg/hr) liquefaction rate over 
</p>
<p>48 hours.

<p>Results of the experimental fiber optics sensors monitoring temperature change throughout each of the liquefaction 
</p>
<p>tests are promising, with close agreement with co-locating silicon diodes, as shown in Figure 11 for the duration of a 
</p>
<p>period where the oxygen system was being increased in saturation pressure. The stratification in the system is evident 
</p>
<p>during the times where the tank was pressurizing. Figure 12 shows the ullage response on the silicon diode and 
</p>
<p>similarly placed fiber grating at the 95% fill level during transient mass flow testing at high fill levels.  The difference 
</p>
<p>in stratification can be seen between the first three spikes and the fourth hump (around 80 hours). Figure 13 shows the 
</p>
<p>temperature as a function of fill level for several of the time slices within that test.  The stratification within the system 
</p>
<p>is much more evident using the fiber optic system than in the much more widely spaced silicon diodes. 
</p>
<p>Within Figure 13, in the plot of the 3rd hour elapsed time, only the top few silicon diodes show stratification at the 
</p>
<p>ullage, but the fiber sensors show more of consistent temperature change trend from ullage to liquid level.  By the 5 th 
</p>
<p>hours, when tank starts to depressurize as the flow slows down, both temperature distributions return to an unstratified 
</p>
<p>profile.  The stratification can be seen again at 20th and 51st hour of elapsed time, from the second and third high flow 
</p>
<p>cycle, respectively.

<p>Results of the oxygen testing confirmed many of the results of previous nitrogen testing: (i) fill level was not a 
</p>
<p>large driver in system operations (with the exception of pressure response swings above 90% full), (ii) at low fill 
</p>
<p>levels, ullage insertion of the gas created a higher liquefaction rate than dip tube insertion, (iii) transient pressure 
</p>
<p>responses were much smaller when bubbling oxygen through the liquid than when inserting into the ullage of the tank, 
</p>
<p>and (iv) the tank could be filled to greater than 95% full without having to reduce the oxygen flow rate while holding 
</p>
<p>tank pressure constant.  New lessons learned included the sensitivity of the system to neon loop pressure and 
</p>
<p>temperatures and pressure sensitivities of liquefaction rates. 
</p>
<p>IV.  Conclusion 
</p>
<p>The CryoFILL testing demonstrated liquefaction of up to 1.6 kg/hr of oxygen coming into a tank.  Performance trends 
</p>
<p>as a function of tank pressure, fill level, and injection method was established. Steady state performance was 
</p>
<p>successfully used to predict transient system operations. Multiple different transient cases were run with the test 
</p>
<p>hardware to demonstrate preferential operational strategies that might be used in transient operations. The FOSS 
</p>
<p>system was successfully demonstrated in a liquid oxygen environment to provide a much higher detailed view of the 
</p>
<p>temperature stratification of the oxygen. 
</p>
<p>This CryoFILL series of testing met all of its performance goals (see Table 3). The next step is to incorporate a 
</p>
<p>more flight-like cryocooler to better understand the cryocooler driven responses on the system.  Eventually the 
</p>
<p>additional pieces of a Lunar or Mars surface oxygen production system including electrolyzers to separate water and 
</p>
<p>associated processing hardware could be incorporated into this system.   
</p>
