Introduction


The SLS propulsion system induces an energetic launch environment the vehicle must withstand





Water is injected into the mobile launcher hole (MLH) to mitigate the environment on the vehicle


The ignition overpressure/sound suppression (IOP/SS) system consists of six water nozzles per SRB and 16 water nozzles for the Core Stage





Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations of the multiphase launch environment conducted to support SLS


Comparison to Artemis I flight data provided a validation opportunity

SLS Launch Environment


The solid rocket booster (SRB) transient generates the most energetic portion of the SLS launch environment


Includes igniter shock (IS), ignition overpressure (IOP), and duct overpressure (DOP)





Igniter shock results from activation of the SRB igniter


The shock and ‘cold’ flow precedes the hot combustion gases





IOP and DOP result from the SRB plume transient


The plume accelerates through the MLH and trench as the motor pressure rises


Compression of ambient air and vapor in the plume’s path forms the pressure sources


Large magnitude, low frequency waves generated when the sources release


IOP is the upward traveling portion while DOP is the downward traveling portion

Multiphase CFD Predictions Support the SLS Program


The plume-water interaction physics during the SLS launch are highly non-linear and difficult to model


Multiple, hot, supersonic rocket plumes


Multiphase flow (gas and liquid) + phase change


Dependent on both large and small length/time scales





Significant uncertainty existed about the plume-water interactions prior to the Artemis I flight 


Lack of integrated SLS tests prior to flight


Significant configuration differences between SLS, Shuttle, and subscale testing





SLS Program relied on CFD to inform design and launch risks


Supported hydrogen burn-off igniters (HBOIs) operation, water system design, debris transport, environment development, etc.


Multiphase CFD evolved over time, conducted 5 simulations in total with different models and flow conditions


Those simulations that most closely represents the Artemis I launch are presented here

Model Comparison


Two different fidelity multiphase models have been fielded


Loci/Stream-Volume of Fluid (VoF) pressure-based solver (high fidelity)


Gas-liquid interface tracked explicitly via geometric reconstruction


Surface tension applied to the interface


Liquid structures properly breakdown to smaller droplets in response to external forces


Accuracy (and expense) dependent on mesh resolution


Loci/CHEM-Real Fluids (RF) density-based solver (low fidelity)


Gas-liquid interface not tracked (no surface tension, liquid diffuses non-physically)


Water acts as a dense fluid with appropriate thermodynamic/phase change properties


Captures water mass and general location, but neither full water density nor droplets/splashing


Results reasonable where:


Interface details less important


Numerical diffusion timescales longer than fluid convective timescales


Three different simulations will be examined presently


One VoF, two RF, two different water flowrates

IOP1 Formation: Dry


IOP expected to form as the accelerating SRB plume compresses ambient air in the MLH


Examining CFD without water (i.e., dry) provides insight on IOP


Two IOP waves identified in the dry CFD





Dry IOP1 formation coincides with:


The hot plume overtaking the cold IS flow


Plume intensification (increased total temperature)


The pressure source traveling from the confined MLH to the larger trench


18





The dry IOP1 waves travel both upwards and downwards

IOP1 Formation: Wet, Min RF


Water delays the advection of the plume


Hot combustion gases catch up to IS flow within the MLH


Plume intensifies within the MLH


Plume takes longer to exit the MLH





The wet IOP1 source releases as travels from the confined MLH to the larger trench


Wave only releases downwards, upward wave appears to be absorbed/blocked by water


More examination needed to confirm mechanism and model accuracy

The SRB plume continues to advance until turning on the MFD

Once this pressure source releases, a portion of it travels upstream

IOP2 Formation: Wet, Min RF


The wet simulation IOP2 source forms in a similar manner


SRB plume advances and turns on the MFD


Compresses flow in the trench


Releases both up and downstream





The difference for the wet simulation is that the water significantly delays the SRB plume advection


The wet IOP2 wave encounters the heat shield much later and lower in magnitude than the dry IOP2 wave


21





Note that unlike the wet IOP1 wave, the significantly damped wet IOP2 wave does travel up the MLH duct


It is currently unclear why this is the case


Possibilities include:


Water altering the IOP1 source release


Differences in the wave release locations


Further investigation is needed

Conclusion


Qualitative validation achieved by comparing CFD flowfields to Artemis I flight video


Both solvers successfully predicted the initial SRB plume-water interaction


CFD outward velocity jets match flight-observed outward water spray





Quantitative validation achieved by comparing CFD to flight pressures at the base heat shield


Both solvers successfully predicted the SRB igniter shock, secondary igniter shock, and the magnitude of the IOP mitigation


CFD suggests IOP forms as the MLH pressurization diffracts from the confined MLH duct into the larger flame trench volume


More work needed to fully understand the launch environment


More details in the accompanying paper





This analysis considered successful validation of both the Loci/STREAM-VoF and Loci/CHEM-RF CFD solvers for multiphase launch environments


CFD successfully predicted IS propagation and IOP magnitude reduction


Validation provides confidence in future use of these tools on multiphase launch environments
