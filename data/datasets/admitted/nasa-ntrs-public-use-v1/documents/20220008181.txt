Abstract 
 
The ESP-PSYCHIC model is the present standard by European and US space 
agencies for mission design limits on solar energetic proton (SEP) fluences, which gives 
predictions of cumulative fluences, the highest fluences, and peak fluxes over a user-
specified mission duration as a function of confidence level. Additional SEP data and 
new methodologies have led to the advancement of solar radiation environment models 
in several directions, including the explicit treatment of event durations and waiting 
times in between events, the occurrence of SEP events during quieter periods (e.g. 
solar minimum), and extending model coverage up to much higher energies. As most 
current state-of-the-art spacecraft are designed with thick shielding, the high energy 
protons are particularly relevant to radiation analysis for spacecraft reliability and crew 
protection during the planned exploration missions. However, the extension to higher 
energies of most previous models relied on extrapolation with functional fittings of 
historical events, which contains implicit uncertainties as the characteristics of each 
event are unique. In this work we reported a multi-source calibrated GOES dataset of 
past 3 solar cycles and used it to update the worst fluence model with energy coverage 
of 10-1000 MeV through the ESP-PSYCHIC approach. Both the low energy and high 
energy sections of the predicted spectrum are compared with previous models and 
historical large events. The dataset can be further applied with more advanced 
methodologies to develop more models for use by spacecraft designers and mission 
operators.

1. Introduction 
 

Bursts of Solar Energetic Particles (SEP) can be suddenly released from the Sun along 
with electric and magnetic fields through coronal mass ejections (CME) and solar flares. 
Under certain conditions, these fast-moving particles can further accelerate themselves 
by shock waves and consolidate other sources in interplanetary space to form large 
scale Solar Particle Events (SPE) that impact extensive portion of heliosphere 
(Reames, 2013). If the Earth is in the path of these large volume of SEP and the 
embedded electromagnetic fields, its magnetosphere can be deformed and many 
essential satellites circuiting around it are prone to disruptions such as displacement 
damage, single-event upsets, and total dose effects (Feynman & Gabriel, 2000). They 
can even impact the ground electricity grid network and aviation routes passing through 
the polar regions. These particles are particularly of concern to space exploration 
missions beyond the protection of the Earth’s magnetic field such as the planned trips to 
the Moon and Mars, and significant efforts are undergone in spacecraft design, mission 
plan, and scientific research to mitigate their risks to the electronic systems and crew 
(Xapsos, 2019).  
 
Although physical shielding can prevent most of these energetic particles from 
penetrating into the spacecraft, the space system design needs to balance the trade-
offs between risk and cost in a systematical way. Accurate solar radiation environment 
models are therefore essential for developing reliable and cost-effective new space 
technologies. It was shown that the energy release due to SPE is consistent with the 
dynamics of a self-organized criticality system such as earthquakes, avalanches, and 
rainfall (Xapsos, 2006), and the models to describe solar radiation environment are 
generally assumed to be stochastic in nature. They could provide predictions of 
cumulative fluences of a mission, highest fluences or peak fluxes resulting from a single 
event to be seen over a user-specified mission duration as a function of confidence 
level, etc. Past models of SPE occurrences include the King model (King, 1974), the Jet 
Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) model (Feynman et al., 1990; 1993; 2002), the Emission of 
Solar Protons (ESP) model (Xapsos et al., 1998; 1999), and Moscow State University 
(MSU) model (Nymmik, 1999; 2007; 2011). ESP was expanded to also include models 
of the cumulative fluence environment extrapolated from the distribution of yearly solar 
proton fluences and was later renamed as the PSYCHIC (Prediction of Solar particle 
Yields for Characterizing Integrated Circuits) model (Xapsos et al., 2000; 2004; 2007). 
The ESP-PSYCHIC model is the present standard for long-term SEP fluences as 
specified in the relevant documents from European and US space agencies (ECSS, 
2020; SWORM, 2018).   
 
With additional SEP data and new methodologies, the modeling of solar radiation 
environment advances recently in several directions. The first group of works 
emphasize the non-point-like property of SPEs, by considering event durations and 
waiting times between events (Jun et al., 2007; Jiggens et al., 2012). The second 
category of models follow the MSU modeling approach, i.e., assuming the event 
frequency is solar cycle dependent based on sunspot (Wolf) number rather than 
invariant over seven solar active years per cycle (Kim et al., 2009; Robinson et al., 
2019). This allows periods of high solar activity (solar maximum) with quieter periods 



 
2 

(solar minimum) to be treated nonhomogeneously and all time can be considered in 
mission planning and analysis. The third direction leads to models of predicted spectra 
extended to much higher energies than early models, such as the Solar Accumulated 
and Peak Proton and Heavy Ion Radiation Environment (SAPPHIRE) Model (Jiggens et 
al., 2018), the Mission Specific Solar Radiation Environment Model (MSSREM) 
(Robinson et al., 2019), and the University of Turku (UTU) model (Raukunen et al., 
2018), all using the Band function formalism (Band et al., 1993) to extrapolate spectra to 
reach 1 GeV. Extending the solar radiation environment models to higher energies are 
important for spacecraft reliability analysis and astronaut protection, as most current 
state-of-the-art spacecraft are designed with thick shielding (e.g., the Orion Multiple 
Purpose Crew Vehicle (MPCV) (Mertens et al., 2018)) and only the high energy protons 
are relevant to radiation exposure analysis inside vehicles (Hu et al., 2020).  
 
The accuracy of these statistic models is highly relied upon the dataset of proton 
measurement that was available at the time of development. For example, the 
PSYCHIC model is considered as a significant improvement of ESP model (Xapsos et 
al., 2004). Though both models used data of IMP-3, -4, -5, and -8 satellites and GOES-
5, -6, and -7 covering the time period from 1966 to 2001, the ESP model treated data 
from different instruments independently, and the model was limited with an upper 
energy bound of  >100 MeV and had to use spectra of several large SPEs to 
extrapolate to >300 MeV (Xapsos et al., 1999). But after combining the IMP-8 GME data 
and GOES-6 and -7 data into an integrated data set, the highest energy of PSYCHIC 
model extended to 327 MeV (Xapsos et al., 2004). For this reason, the developers of 
recent models such as SAPPHIRE and MSSREM exerted significant efforts to prepare 
high quality proton datasets (Jiggens et al., 2012, 2018; Robinson et al., 2019). In this 
work, we reported a multi-source calibrated GOES proton data covering the period of 
1986-2019 with highest energy of 1 GeV, and presented an updated worst case fluence 
model through the ESP-PSYCHIC approach. The paper is structured in 4 further 
sections: 

• GOES proton data calibration (Sect. 2); 
• methods to prepare the data (Sect. 3); 
• modeling methodology, statistical distributions, results, and comparison with 

previous models and historical large events (Sect. 4); 
• concluding remarks (Sect. 5). 

 
2. Multi-source calibration of GOES proton measurement 

For several decades, the Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellites (GOES) 
series provide both real-time and historical data for radiation exposure estimation and 
solar proton radiation environment modeling. Recently, several groups conducted 
calibration studies that significantly reduced the uncertainties on the response of GOES 
proton detectors, thus improving the reliability of the spectral observations of solar 
energetic particle events. In the work of Sandberg et al. (2014), the Energetic Particle 
Sensors (EPS) measurements of GOES 5, 7, 8, and 11 were compared with NASA 
IMP-8/Goddard Medium Energy (GME) experiment data set, and the proton energies 
derived for the P2–P7 channels were significantly lower than the nominal energies 
through ground proton beam calibration (Onsager et al., 1996). In a later work by Bruno 
(2017), using the high-energy particle measurements of the Payload for Antimatter 



 
3 

Matter Exploration and Light-nuclei Astrophysics (PAMELA), a set of effective energies 
were determined for the proton channels of the P6 and P7 and the High Energy Proton 
and Alpha Detector (HEPAD) P8–P11 sensors on board GOES 13 and 15. In addition, 
Raukunen et al. (2020) reported a comprehensive bowtie analysis of the HEPAD proton 
channel energy responses as measured with energetic proton beams.  
 
We also reported a calibration study of GOES proton data by using models of Ground 
Level Enhancement (GLE) events based on measurement of multiple satellites and 
ground neutron monitors (Hu & Semones, 2022). Two sets of GLE models were applied 
as references; One is the Band function form (Tylka & Dietrich, 2009), assuming a 
constant background proton flux before and after an event, and the other is the Modified 
Band Function (MBF) form, assuming a variant background flux through each GLE and 
a revised neutron-monitor yield function (Koldobskiy et al., 2021). Comparing with 
event-integrated fluences of the GLEs recorded by GOES 6, 8, 10, 11, 13, and 15, we 
obtained two sets of effective energies for each proton channel of EPS and HEPAD 
onboard these satellites. Except for GOES 6, the energies obtained from uncorrected 
data and corrected data through the Zwickl algorithm (Rodriguez et al., 2017) have 
minor differences (Hu & Semones, 2022), which has been demonstrated in the 
calibration work using the PAMELA measurements (Bruno, 2017). Like this work, 
directly using the uncorrected flux, we showed the integral channel P11 can be treated 
as a differential channel at about 1 GeV. Because most proton channels, except for P4 
of GOES 6, of both EPS and HEPAD functioned properly in all these missions during 
the period of 1986-2019, a contiguous and uniform dataset of solar proton fluxes 
encompassing energies from several MeV to about 1 GeV can be developed for the 
past 3 solar cycles.  
 
The quality of such a long-range dataset not only depends on the energy coverage, but 
also the consistency of the measurements as compared with other observations of high 
fidelity. The datasets developed for SAPPHIRE and MSSREM models (Jiggens et al., 
2012, 2018; Robinson et al., 2019) were based on cross-calibration with IMP-8 GME 
measurement, which is known to suffer saturation effects during some of the largest 
events, and the sporadic invalid values had to be carefully detected and filled with other 
measurements such as those of GOES 7 (Xapsos et al., 2004). In addition, our recent 
study indicated the IMP-8 GME instrument may have experienced performance 
degradation along its service time from 1973 to 2001 (Hu &Semones, 2022). To verify 
the effective energies obtained by calibrating with GLE models, we compared the 
spectra of 4 SPEs not used in our list of GLEs but recorded by GOES-10, -11, -13 and 
15, with PAMELA measurement, bowtie analysis, and GOES-16 data analysis (Hu 
&Semones, 2022). Of the two sets of energies, we found the spectra generated with 
MBF energies (Table 1) are more consistent with those from these multiple sources as 
well as the calibration work of Bruno (2017). The PAMELA data are distinctive in situ 
measurements from those of GOES series, the bowtie analysis and GOES-16 energies 
are based on different ground calibrations, and the GLE models are derived from the 
analysis of the data of multiple satellites and ground neutron monitors, therefore, the 
effective energies we obtained for the proton channels of GOES 6-15 can be regarded 
as a set of multi-source calibrated energies (Table 1).

3. Methods for preparing the dataset 

We used an in-house software WASPE (Web App for Solar Particle Events) to help 
generating a homogeneous proton flux dataset for SEP environment modeling. All 
available 5-min averaged proton data from GOES 6-15 at National Centers for 
Environmental Information website (https://satdat.ngdc.noaa.gov/sem/goes/data/avg/) 
were downloaded and stored locally, which can be retrieved for any time from 1986-01-
01 to 2020-02-29. The plotting library dygraphs (https://dygraphs.com/) was used to 
visualize flux variation in time and rendered a spectrum of the selected period. This tool 
allows zooming in the plots either horizontally or vertically, which is useful to examine 
the details of the records and results. Another good feature of it is to display information 
of each data point by just hovering over it with mouse cursor, which is very helpful to 
determine the onset and end of an event, and to detect gaps of bad data and data 
spikes. For the data gaps as reported in Raukunen et al. (2020), which are not 
overlapped with any significant SPE, we simply used the average flux of the two ends to 
fill the missing points; for spikes, we followed the algorithm described in Raukunen et al. 
(2020) to identify them and clean the data.

As we observed in our previous work (Hu & Semones, 2022), during the two time 
periods observing GLEs 47-50 and 54, the background fluxes of HEPAD channels were 
elevated significantly higher than those before and after the periods, and the fluences of 
these events at the high energy channels were much larger than those of EPS 
channels, resulting in non-monotonic spectra in energy that are incomparable to 
functional fitting models (Figure 1). To remedy this caveat, we calculated the ratios of 
background fluxes before and after the onset of elevation. For the period of 1990-05-01 
to 1990-05-31, the ratios for P8-11 are 65.8, 42.0, 284.0, and 1286.5, respectively; For 
the period of 1992-07-01 to 1992-12-31, the ratios are 71.0, 41.4, 312.8, and 1356.0, 
respectively. We found by simply dividing the elevated point fluxes during these two 
periods with these ratios, the corrected event-integrated fluences of the GLEs are 
aligned with MBF spectra derived by Koldobskiy et al. (2021). The right panel of Figure 
1 shows the comparison of the 1990-05-21 event before and after the correction. All raw 
data were visually checked with WASPE to ensure no other such abnormal sections 
exists.  
 
Except for GOES 6, other satellites in our list had some overlapped service time with at 
least one other satellite. Because GOES 8 provided the most reliable data for deriving 
SEP event fluence (Rosenqvist et al. 2005), we chose it over GOES 10 and 11 
whenever its data was provided. The detectors on GOES 10 faced eastwardly during 
the mission (Rodriguez et al. 2014), therefore, for events with east-west anisotropic 
particles, the recorded flux should be different from those that faced westwardly. In 
addition, the data of P6 and P7 had not been reported since 2004-06-30, so we only 
used the data of GOES 10 in the year of 2003 to fill the gap between GOES 8 and 11, 
even though GOES 11 was in storage mode from 2000 to 2005, whose data quality 
suffers bias due to satellite spinning (Rodriguez et al. 2014). For the two EPEADs 
(Energetic Proton, Electron and Alpha Detectors) on both GOES 13 and 15, we always 
chose the one facing westward. The data quality of GOES 13 and 15 is similar 
(Rodriguez et al. 2014); GOES 15 is preferred over GOES 13 simply because it was the 
last mission of this series, and its service time is much longer than GOES 13 (Table 2).

radiation exposure analysis for space vehicles (Figure 3 in Hu et al., 2020). The

energies in the raw data. To extrapolate GOES 6 measurements to channel 11 (with

4. Solar radiation environment model update 

 
4.1. Event definition 

Traditionally, the onset and end of an SPE are defined as the first and last time points of 
a period with continually elevated proton fluxes higher than a threshold (Feynman et al., 
1990; Jiggens et al., 2012). However, because of the modulation of solar cycles, the 
background fluxes change significantly from the solar minimum to the solar maximum, 
and the difference can be as large as 6 times (Xapsos, 2019). If a fixed flux were 
chosen as the threshold to determine the onset and end of an event, the duration of a 
same event would be different if it occurred at different times in solar cycles, and a 
certain part of the elevated measurements relative to the background might be 
disregarded. To avoid this problem, we used the background fluxes as references for 
the threshold of an event, which are not fixed but varying along solar cycles. The 
algorithm to automatically calculate the background and to determine the onset and end 
of an event was reported in our previous work (Hu et al., 2020). We modified it to 
calculate 3 background fluxes, i.e., 1 day, 7days, and 28 days before a time point to be 
checked if it can be considered as the onset of an event. The lowest one-day average 
flux of these three levels was considered as the pre-event background, and a value of 
1.8 times the background was used as the threshold, which was a bit lower than the 
previous algorithm (Hu et al., 2020). If fluxes of 24 consequent time points (i.e., 2 hours) 
are above it, the first point was considered as the start of an event.  
 
Following the work of Jiggens et al. (2012), we chose channel 1 fluxes (F1, at 10 MeV) 
to check if SPEs occurred, making sure all events were considered that could be 
significant in terms of their peak flux or their integrated flux (fluence) values in any of the 
11 energy channels. A minimum dwell time of 24 h was introduced such that if the flux 
rose above the threshold again within 24 h of the end of an event, the enhancements 
were treated as the same event. In addition, a minimum event duration of 24 h and a 
minimum event fluence of 1.29E+4 cm-2 sr-1 MeV-1 were used to remove erroneous and 
insignificant events. The automatic SPE detecting algorithm identified 230 SPEs in the 
dataset, which are comparable to the lists of the National Oceanographic and 



 
7 

Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) SEP events1 and the Solar Energetic Particle 
Environment Modelling (SEPEM) reference events2.  
 

4.2. Background removal 
 
All pre-event background fluxes were recorded during the processing steps and 
compared with the Sunspot Numbers (SSN) as the solar modulation indexes, which are 
plotted in the left panel of Figure 2. The background fluxes of several events did not 
follow the trend of anti-correlation of the solar modulation. We checked each with the 
WASPE tool and found all the enhanced backgrounds were caused by one or more 
events occurred immediately before the event being analyzed. The impacts to the 
duration and fluence size of these events were found to be insignificant, because, for 
the flux of low energy protons in a typical event, the elevation relative to the pre-event 
background is usually several order of magnitude. Their impacts to the analysis of 
higher energy protons are also minimal. The right panel of Figure 2 shows the pre-event 
background fluxes for channel 6 (i.e., 100 MeV), which are perfectly anti-correlated with 
the solar modulation for all events.

those for high energy protons, for F6-11 of each event, we processed the data only 
during the elevation period of F6, so that the background noises outside the periods of 
real elevation in these channels have no impacts to event fluences. After the first round 
of process, we found, among the 230 events, there are 12 events that have zero 
accumulated fluence at channel 7 but some of these 12 events have non-zero fluences

4.5. Upper limits modeling 
 
A unique feature of ESP-PSYCHIC approach is the capability to estimate the upper 
limits for SPE fluences (Xapsos 2006). Because the values of ϕmax are determined from 
limited data, the model applies a parametric “bootstrap” technique to assess the 
uncertainties of the upper limits. The idea is to randomly select event fluences 
according to the distribution given by equation (2), until the number of events in the 
distribution for each energy level is simulated. The simulated data is then used to fit the 
same equation, and a set of parameters can be extracted. The procedure is repeated, 
and each time the parameters have different values. After a number of simulations, the 
standard deviation of the parameter of interest can be determined.

5. Summary and discussion 

 
We have reported a new solar proton data set recorded by the EPS and HEPAD 
instruments onboard the GOES series during the last 3 solar cycles and used it to 



 
15 

update the worst case fluence model through the standard ESP-PSYCHIC approach. 
The HEPAD measurements are known to be contaminated with higher level noises than 
the EPS measurements. In our recent work, we used the spectra of past GLEs modeled 
with improved space-borne data and ground-based neutron monitor measurements and 
achieved a set of effective energies for the 4 HEPAD channels consistent with other 
multiple sources, including the bow tie analysis, PAMELA measurements, and GOES-R 
records of the new suite of energetic particles detectors (Hu & Semones, 2022). This 
makes it feasible to build up a long duration, contiguous, and homogenous solar proton 
data with energy extended to as high as 1000 MeV. To our knowledge, this is the first 
data set that have a such high energy coverage in the space radiation environment 
modeling community. The contribution of high energy protons is important to radiation 
analysis for current space technology, as most current state-of-the-art spacecraft are 
designed with thick shielding and only the high energy protons can penetrate the 
shielding and induce secondary particles to enhance radiation inside vehicles.  

 
To build a proton data record with only solar contribution, we followed an algorithm to 
remove the background fluxes of all energy channels which are due to instrument noise 
and GCRs. The noise levels of the data in high energy channels are hard to determine, 
hindering its usage for a long time. We identified a procedure to estimate these by 
requiring the thresholds of event definition for all channels follow a universal power law, 
and found that, after applying the noise levels, the number of events detected at each 
channel decreases gradually as energy increases, no matter solar minimum periods are 
included or not. With this procedure, the background fluxes are found well anti-
correlated with the solar modulation indexes in the past 3 solar cycles. Using the pre-
event background fluxes as reference rather than fixed thresholds, an automatic 
algorithm detected 230 SPEs during the period of 1986-2019, a list similar to other 
investigations. As the background fluxes vary significantly from solar minima to solar 
maxima, the calculated event durations as well fluences should be more accurate than 
obtained with a fixed threshold for all events.  
 
Following the standard ESP-PSYCHIC approach, we obtained an updated worst case 
fluence model as a function of mission duration and confidence level. The modeling 
results are close to the original ESP-PSYCHIC model but with extended energy 
coverage up to 1000 MeV. The discrepancies at energy > 30 MeV can be related to the 
earlier large events that are not included in our data set. The projected spectrum lies 
between the JPL model and the SAPPHIRE model, similar to previous comparison of 
these models with the ESP-PSYCHIC model (Jiggens et al., 2018). Comparing with the 
two models with extension to 1000 MeV, the SAPPHIRE fluences are lower by a factor 
of 3-4 for energies > 60 MeV, which may be related to the lower effective energies of 
GOES P6 and P7 detectors for the calibrated dataset as compared to our work (Hu & 
Semones, 2022). The UTU modeling results are very close to this work, which is 
interesting as both approaches utilized the spectra of historical GLEs, though the 
methodologies are vast different. The uncertainty estimation of the upper limits via the 
parametric “bootstrap” technique indicates the spectrum of the upper limit + 1σ can be 
considered as a perfect combination of the 1972-08 and 1956-02 episodes, though 
these two large events are not included in the modeling data set. Further works can be 
done similarly to update the accumulative fluence model which is essential to estimate 



 
16 

the exploration mission cancer risks due to SPEs, and peak flux model which provides a 
practical and objective guideline for designing spacecraft to endure single event effects. 
The data set can be also applied with more advanced methodologies to develop more 
models for use by spacecraft designers and mission operators.
