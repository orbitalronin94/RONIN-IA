<p>Numerical Simulation of No-Vent Chill and Fill of a Large 
</p>
<p>Liquid Hydrogen Tank 
 
</p>
<p>Alok Majumdar1, Andre LeClair2 
</p>
<p>NASA Marshall Space Flight Center, Huntsville, AL, 35812 
</p>
<p>Jason Hartwig3  
</p>
<p>NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, 44135 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>To enable the design of future in-space cryogenic propellant transfer vehicles, high accuracy models of the 
</p>
<p>cryogenic propellant transfer process are desired. For in-space refueling applications, accurate simulations are 
</p>
<p>desired of the propellant tank chilldown and no-vent fill (NVF) process in order to estimate the amount of 
</p>
<p>propellant required, to determine the maximum sustainable flow rate to fill the tank, and to establish a timeline 
</p>
<p>for the filling process. NVF is a complex process that involves numerous two-phase heat and mass transfer 
</p>
<p>phenomena, such as boiling at the wall and evaporation and condensation at the liquid/vapor interface. In 
</p>
<p>recent years, progress has been made in developing a numerical modeling technique using the Generalized 
</p>
<p>Fluid System Simulation Program (GFSSP) that has been validated against several small-scale liquid nitrogen 
</p>
<p>NVF tests. This paper presents GFSSP numerical model validation against three liquid hydrogen NVF tests on 
</p>
<p>a large-scale propellant tank. Comparing the GFSSP model to the data for pressure, fill level, and wall 
</p>
<p>temperature, the mean absolute percentage error is below 20% across the range of simulated conditions, 
</p>
<p>indicating very good predictive agreement for this large-scale hydrogen NVF test series. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>he in-space transfer of cryogenic propellants will likely be required for enabling multiple future space missions, 
</p>
<p>such as nuclear thermal propulsion (NTP) or nuclear electric/chemical systems [1]. The refueling process can be 
</p>
<p>subdivided into five steps: (1) pressurization of the supply tank, (2) propellant management devices used to extract 
</p>
<p>only-liquid, (3) chill down of the connecting transfer line, (4) chilldown of the customer receiver tank, and (5) fill of 
</p>
<p>the customer receiver tank. The focus of this paper is on filling the customer receiver tank. 
</p>
<p>  Cryogenic systems offer unique benefits in efficiency and specific impulse, at launch mass savings, as well 
</p>
<p>as safety and environmental benefits as compared to toxic non-cryogenic propellants. However, cryogens exist as 
</p>
<p>liquids at extremely low temperatures, generally leading to high heat transfer rates, phase change, and difficulty 
</p>
<p>controlling and predicting the propellant transfer process. Due to the cost of launching and storing propellant outside 
</p>
<p>Earth’s gravity well, it is highly desired to perform the propellant transfer process conserving propellant mass. 
</p>
<p>Therefore, the goal is to transfer liquid from the supply to the receiver tank in a mass-conserving fashion, while 
</p>
<p>remaining well below the maximum operating pressure, and achieving a high final fill fraction in the receiver tank at 
</p>
<p>the end of the fill. 
</p>
<p>  Fluid transfer in 1-g is straightforward. In 1-g, gravity forces the heavier liquid to the bottom of the tank 
</p>
<p>while the lighter vapor rises to the top. Fluid can be transferred with the vent valve open; vapor generated due to heat 
</p>

<p>transfer between cold cryogen and warm tank wall can simply be vented as the liquid displaces the vapor. In 
</p>
<p>microgravity however, surface tension forces tend to dominate over gravitational forces such that the liquid/vapor 
</p>
<p>configuration is only known over very long settling times. In general, the location of the liquid/vapor interface is 
</p>
<p>unknown and therefore the vent valve cannot simply be left open during propellant transfer. Therefore, the no-vent 
</p>
<p>fill (NVF) method was developed as a feasible means to fill propellant tanks in reduced gravity. The tank is first 
</p>
<p>chilled down to some “target temperature” [2], defined as the maximum allowable average tank wall temperature that 
</p>
<p>permits a continuous NVF with the tank vent valve closed to the desired final fill level, typically &gt;90%.  
</p>
<p>  Multiple methods have been proposed to prechill the tank to the target temperature before a NVF can occur. 
</p>
<p>Chato and Sanabria [3] pioneered the charge/hold/vent (CHV) method. During the charge period, a small quantity of 
</p>
<p>liquid cryogen is injected into the evacuated receiver tank.  Initially, the liquid flashes due to the low tank pressure, 
</p>
<p>and then the remaining liquid droplets evaporate as they contact warm vapor or the tank wall.  During the hold period, 
</p>
<p>the circulating flow pattern provides convective heat transfer from cold vapor to the tank wall.  The primary mode of 
</p>
<p>heat transfer during the hold is convection.  At the completion of the hold period, the pressure has risen considerably, 
</p>
<p>most, if not all of the liquid has boiled or evaporated, and the tank is ready to be vented.  Since venting occurs as an 
</p>
<p>isentropic blowdown, some additional cooling may be recovered by stage-wise venting [4]. The CHV process is 
</p>
<p>repeated cyclically until the tank wall temperature is at or below the target temperature. 
</p>
<p>  A simpler method is that of Vented-Chill / No-Vent-Fill (VCNVF) [5].  In this method, the tank vent valve 
</p>
<p>is open for an initial period while the tank walls are chilled with a spray of cryogenic liquid that boils to vapor and 
</p>
<p>exits through the vent.  When the tank walls have been sufficiently chilled, the vent valve is closed, and the tank is 
</p>
<p>filled with liquid.  Care must be taken with the timing of the vent closure.  If the vent is closed too early, residual heat 
</p>
<p>in the walls may drive sufficient boil-off to raise the tank pressure high enough to stall the flow. If the vent is closed 
</p>
<p>too late, some liquid propellant may be lost out the vent. Compared to the CHV method, VCNVF is less mass efficient; 
</p>
<p>the gas being vented during the chilldown may not extract the maximum possible thermal energy from the tank wall.  
</p>
<p>There is always the risk that some of the liquid propellant will be vented.  However, VCNVF is much simpler from 
</p>
<p>an operational standpoint. The transfer lines need to be chilled down only once and there is less cycling of inlet and 
</p>
<p>vent valves.  Since there is no hold period, overall loading time is likely to be shorter. 
</p>
<p>  A third and possibly even simpler method than the VCNVF or CHV/NVF is using a cryocooler to prechill 
</p>
<p>the receiver tank below the target temperature. However, high efficiency spaceflight cryocoolers are a developing 
</p>
<p>technology, require extra system mass, and most importantly require electrical power that the spacecraft may not be 
</p>
<p>able to afford. In the absence of cryocoolers, using the cooling potential of the working fluid is straightforward. 
</p>
<p>  NVFs have been performed on a variety of tanks in the literature, see [5] for example for an up-to-date 
</p>
<p>summary of previous testing performed. As shown, NVF with cryogens is a complex process that involves multiple 
</p>
<p>two-phase heat and mass transfer phenomena, such as boiling at the wall and evaporation and condensation at the 
</p>
<p>liquid/vapor interface. Being able to predict and control NVF is a challenging and necessary step in enabling future 
</p>
<p>in-space propellant transfers. In particular, being able to predict the receiver tank pressure, fill level, wall temperature, 
</p>
<p>propellant consumed, and filling time will be crucial. 
</p>
<p>  The success of future in-space cryogenic propellant transfer systems and missions will be augmented by 
</p>
<p>accurately being able to model the various stages of cryogenic transfer. Poor models lead to higher launch mass. 
</p>
<p>Nearly all CFD models as well as lumped capacitance models rely on robust correlations or subroutines at the 
</p>
<p>fundamental level. Recently, attention has been drawn to the fact that existing room-temperature based models and 
</p>
<p>correlations do a poor job in predicting certain cryogenic phenomena, yet most correlations used in the most popular 
</p>
<p>thermal/fluid design codes are based on room-temperature fluids. To this end, work is being performed across the 
</p>
<p>board to update and upgrade the existing subroutines in these thermal/fluid design codes, where appropriate. 
</p>
<p>  Initial NVF modeling efforts included simplified lumped capacitance models pioneered by researchers such 
</p>
<p>as Chato [6]. In the 1990s and 2000s, higher fidelity lumped capacitance models were used to model NVF, such as 
</p>
<p>the Generalized Fluid System Simulation Program (GFSSP) [7] and SINDA/FLUINT. GFSSP was previously used to 
</p>
<p>model the CHV and NVF of the system from [3] and showed reasonable agreement with test data. GFSSP was also 
</p>
<p>used to model the VCNVF method of filling the small flightweight CRYOTE tank with liquid nitrogen tests from [5]. 
</p>
<p>The GFSSP model was upgraded to include subroutines for boiling at the tank wall and condensation and evaporation 
</p>
<p>heat and mass transfer. The model results compared quite favorably with the test data. The tank filling model of the 
</p>
<p>CRYOTE tank described in [5] was extended to simulate no-vent transfer tank filling with a Thermodynamic Vent 
</p>
<p>System (TVS) assisted spray injector from [8]. The model performed quite well against the data, and results showed 
</p>
<p>that GFSSP captured each phase of the no-vent transfer (initial pressure rise, stall, recovery, condensation at the 
</p>
<p>liquid/vapor interface, and final ullage compression); results are available in [9]. A two-dimensional GFSSP model of 
</p>
<p>the liquid nitrogen tests from [5] was also developed within the framework of the nodal network code to capture the 
</p>
<p>thermal stratification and recirculating flow due to natural convection, and results agreed well against the data [10]. </p>

</p>

</p>
<p>  This paper presents GFSSP numerical model validation against three liquid hydrogen (LH2) NVF tests 
</p>

</p>
<p>the test series that will be modeled. Next, the numerical model is described. Then results are discussed to compare the 
</p>
<p>model against the data to assess model performance. Finally, key takeaways are summarized in the conclusions 
</p>
<p>section.

</p>
<p>m3) spheroidal Aluminum (Al) flight-weight tank using LH2. Two injectors were tested, a top spray and a bottom jet; 
</p>
<p>the focus of the current paper is on modeling the top spray tests. The top spray nozzle was composed of a manifold of

<p>13 full cone 120o nozzles as shown in Figure 1. The nozzle was mounted at the 92% fill level approximately.

<p>III. Description of Numerical Model 
</p>
<p>Numerical modeling of filling a tank with liquid cryogen is highly complex. The physical processes are 
</p>
<p>complex because the flow is unsteady, two-phase, and involves boiling and condensation with heat transfer between 
</p>
<p>solid and fluid. The objectives of a numerical model of filling a cryogenic tank are to predict the pressure, fill level, 
</p>
<p>temperature, and time and amount of propellant consumption to fill the tank. Following is a description of the GFSSP 
</p>
<p>model used for NVF modeling. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>A. Introduction to GFSSP 
</p>
<p>GFSSP is a general-purpose computer program for analyzing fluid flow and heat transfer in a complex 
</p>
<p>network of fluid and solid systems.  It employs a pressure based, finite volume algorithm [14] that solves the mass 
</p>
<p>and energy conservation equations at the nodes and the momentum conservation equation at the branches connecting 
</p>
<p>the nodes. The energy conservation equations for the solid are solved at solid nodes that are connected with the fluid 
</p>
<p>nodes to allow conjugate heat transfer.  The mass and momentum conservation equations along with the equation of 
</p>
<p>state are solved simultaneously to calculate pressure, flow rate, and resident mass using the Newton-Raphson method. 
</p>
<p>The energy conservation equations are solved by a successive substitution method to calculate enthalpies at the nodes 
</p>
<p>within the iteration loop of a Newton-Raphson scheme.  
</p>
<p>Thermodynamic properties are calculated for propellants using the computer program GASP [15], which is 
</p>
<p>integrated with GFSSP.  The fluid resistance library includes pipes, orifices, and common fittings and valves.  GFSSP 
</p>
<p>has three major parts.  The first part is the graphical user interface MIG (Modeling Interface for GFSSP).  MIG allows 
</p>
<p>users to create a flow circuit by a “point and click” paradigm.  It creates the GFSSP input file after the completion of 
</p>
<p>the model building process. It can also create a customized GFSSP executable by compiling and linking User 
</p>
<p>Subroutines with the solver module of the code.  The user can run GFSSP from MIG and post-process the results in 
</p>
<p>the same environment.  The second major part of the program is the Solver and Property Module.  This is the heart of 
</p>
<p>the program, which reads the input data file and generates the required conservation equations for fluid and solid nodes 
</p>
<p>and branches with the help of thermodynamic property programs.  It also interfaces with User Subroutines to receive 
</p>
<p>any specific inputs from users.  Finally, it creates output files for MIG to read and display results.  The User Subroutine 
</p>
<p>is the third major part of the program.  This consists of several blank subroutines that are called by the Solver Module.  </p>
<p></p>

</p>
<p>Further details of the finite volume procedure and the computer program are described in [7, 9, 10, 14]. 
</p>

<p>B. Pool Boiling Model 
</p>
<p>The chilldown of a propellant tank is primarily governed by boiling of liquid propellant at the tank wall. 
</p>
<p>Figure 3 shows different pool boiling regimes. During chilldown, the heat flux versus temperature history of the solid 
</p>
<p>surface moves from right to left (E - D - C – B – A). Initially, the difference between the solid wall surface temperature 
</p>
<p>and saturation temperature (wall superheat) is typically several hundred degrees. Therefore, the boiling starts in the 
</p>
<p>Film Boiling regime and continues until it reaches the Leidenfrost (Minimum Film Boiling, MFB) point. After 
</p>
<p>reaching the Leidenfrost point, it switches to Transition Boiling and continues to Critical Heat Flux. Nucleate Boiling 
</p>
<p>starts after reaching Critical Heat Flux and, finally, heat transfer is controlled by natural convection beyond the Onset 
</p>
<p>of Nucleate Boiling (ONB) point. 
</p>
<p>GFSSP uses a set of correlations that have been individually fit and validated against over 10,000 cryogenic 
</p>
<p>pool boiling data points. The correlations are patched together to ensure continuity at each of the transition points. 
</p>
<p>The correlations are called as a user defined function (UDF) in GFSSP to provide a smooth, continuous function of 
</p>
<p>wall heat flux versus wall superheat. For specific information on the correlations, the reader is referred to [16]. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>C. Condensation Model 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>1. Condensation on a droplet from liquid spray 
</p>
<p>Some of the vapor in the tank ullage condenses during the process of filling the tank by spraying liquid 
</p>
<p>propellant in the tank. Condensation of vapor occurs when the vapor comes in contact with the subcooled liquid spray. 
</p>
<p>Condensation reduces the tank pressure and therefore must be accounted for in the numerical model to predict the 
</p>
<p>pressure and the resident liquid mass in the tank. A simple condensation model was developed [16] for such analysis. 
</p>
<p>It is assumed that the liquid spray in the tank remains in the form of droplets of constant effective diameter. The 
</p>
<p>number of droplets in the tank is estimated from the resident mass. The method of estimating the condensation rate 
</p>
<p>was previously described in [9]. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>2. Condensation at the liquid pool/vapor interface 
</p>
<p>At the liquid-vapor interface, there is a possibility of either evaporation or condensation depending upon the 
</p>
<p>condition of vapor and liquid. The phase change occurs as a result of the imbalance between sensible heat transfer 
</p>
<p>rates on the vapor and liquid sides of the interface.  If the heat transfer from the ullage to the interface (QUI) is larger 
</p>
<p>than the heat transfer from the interface to liquid (QIL), evaporation of liquid occurs as shown in Figure 4. On the other 
</p>
<p>hand, if the heat transfer from the interface to liquid (QIL) is larger than the heat transfer from the ullage to the interface </p>

<p> In the CRYOTE experiments from [5], the pressures and temperatures at the injector inlet were measured, 
</p>
<p>and the measured pressure was used as the boundary pressure. It was also observed that the predicted tank pressure 
</p>
<p>was similar to the boundary pressure, but lower due to the pressure drop in the spray nozzle. This information was 
</p>
<p>critical for accurate prediction of the receiver tank pressure. In the experiment being modeled here [13], the pressure 
</p>
<p>at the nozzle inlet was not provided. The pressure in the supply tank was provided, but this was far upstream from the 
</p>
<p>injector spray nozzle. There was not enough information about the transfer line connecting the supply tank and the 
</p>
<p>receiver tank such that a model could be extended to the supply tank enabling an accurate prediction of inlet pressure 
</p>
<p>at the receiver tank. Therefore, an iterative method to calculate the boundary pressure was developed. Here are the 
</p>
<p>steps of the iterative scheme employed in GFSSP’s UDF:  
</p>
<p>1. Assume a pressure at inlet boundary (Node 13 in Figure 5) for the current time step. 
</p>
<p>2. Obtain the converged solution of the flow network with the assumed pressure at the inlet boundary. 
</p>
<p>3. Calculate the total flowrate through the nozzle entering the tank (i.e., sum of flowrate in branches 131 through 
</p>
<p>139 in Figure 5). 
</p>
<p>4. Check the total flowrate with the measured average flowrate for the particular test as shown in Table 4. 
</p>
<p>5. If the flowrates do not match within the acceptable accuracy level, adjust the pressure at the inlet boundary 
</p>
<p>using a Newton-Raphson scheme of correcting the pressure. 
</p>
<p>6. Repeat steps 2 to 5 until the difference between the calculated flowrate (at step 3) and measured flowrate of 
</p>
<p>Table 4 match within 0.1%. 
</p>
<p>7. Proceed to the next time step and repeat steps 1 to 6 until the last time step is reached. 
</p>

<p>V. Conclusion 
</p>
<p>  Developing accurate models of various cryogenic propellant transfer phenomena will reduce risk and reduce 
</p>
<p>margins in the design of future in-space cryogenic propellant vehicles. Particularly, the process of filling a cryogenic 
</p>
<p>propellant tank is challenging to model due to the various competing two-phase heat and mass transfer phenomena 
</p>
<p>that occur during filling. This paper presented model validation results of a GFSSP numerical model of the non-vented 
</p>
<p>filling of a large liquid hydrogen propellant tank. Three tests were simulated over a range of initial receiver tank wall 
</p>
<p>temperatures and mass inflow rate. The GFSSP model with newly developed user-defined subroutines for boiling at 
</p>
<p>the wall and various condensation and evaporation processes predicts the data quite well. The GFSSP model captures 
</p>
<p>the various stages of NVF, flash evaporation, boiling at the wall, condensation and evaporation, and ullage 
</p>
<p>compression. When compared to pressure, fill level and tank wall temperature, the model predicts the data well within 
</p>
<p>20%, omitting errors due to the offset in the temperature sensor for Test 20. The GFSSP model with new UDFs has 
</p>
<p>thus been shown to agree well with cryogenic NVF data across various tank scales, cryogens, flow rates, and initial 
</p>
<p>receiver tank wall temperatures. </p>
