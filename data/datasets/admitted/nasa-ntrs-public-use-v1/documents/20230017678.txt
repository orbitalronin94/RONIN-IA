To enable the design of future in-space cryogenic propellant transfer vehicles, high accuracy models of the 



cryogenic propellant transfer process are desired. For in-space refueling applications, accurate simulations are 



desired of the propellant tank chilldown and no-vent fill (NVF) process in order to estimate the amount of 



propellant required, to determine the maximum sustainable flow rate to fill the tank, and to establish a timeline 



for the filling process. NVF is a complex process that involves numerous two-phase heat and mass transfer 



phenomena, such as boiling at the wall and evaporation and condensation at the liquid/vapor interface. In 



recent years, progress has been made in developing a numerical modeling technique using the Generalized 



Fluid System Simulation Program (GFSSP) that has been validated against several small-scale liquid nitrogen 



NVF tests. This paper presents GFSSP numerical model validation against three liquid hydrogen NVF tests on 



a large-scale propellant tank. Comparing the GFSSP model to the data for pressure, fill level, and wall 



temperature, the mean absolute percentage error is below 20% across the range of simulated conditions, 



indicating very good predictive agreement for this large-scale hydrogen NVF test series. 



 



 



 



 



 



I. Introduction 



 



he in-space transfer of cryogenic propellants will likely be required for enabling multiple future space missions, 



such as nuclear thermal propulsion (NTP) or nuclear electric/chemical systems [1]. The refueling process can be 



subdivided into five steps: (1) pressurization of the supply tank, (2) propellant management devices used to extract 



only-liquid, (3) chill down of the connecting transfer line, (4) chilldown of the customer receiver tank, and (5) fill of 



the customer receiver tank. The focus of this paper is on filling the customer receiver tank. 



 Cryogenic systems offer unique benefits in efficiency and specific impulse, at launch mass savings, as well 



as safety and environmental benefits as compared to toxic non-cryogenic propellants. However, cryogens exist as 



liquids at extremely low temperatures, generally leading to high heat transfer rates, phase change, and difficulty 



controlling and predicting the propellant transfer process. Due to the cost of launching and storing propellant outside 



Earth’s gravity well, it is highly desired to perform the propellant transfer process conserving propellant mass. 



Therefore, the goal is to transfer liquid from the supply to the receiver tank in a mass-conserving fashion, while 



remaining well below the maximum operating pressure, and achieving a high final fill fraction in the receiver tank at 



the end of the fill. 



 Fluid transfer in 1-g is straightforward. In 1-g, gravity forces the heavier liquid to the bottom of the tank 



while the lighter vapor rises to the top. Fluid can be transferred with the vent valve open; vapor generated due to heat

transfer between cold cryogen and warm tank wall can simply be vented as the liquid displaces the vapor. In 



microgravity however, surface tension forces tend to dominate over gravitational forces such that the liquid/vapor 



configuration is only known over very long settling times. In general, the location of the liquid/vapor interface is 



unknown and therefore the vent valve cannot simply be left open during propellant transfer. Therefore, the no-vent 



fill (NVF) method was developed as a feasible means to fill propellant tanks in reduced gravity. The tank is first 



chilled down to some “target temperature” [2], defined as the maximum allowable average tank wall temperature that 



permits a continuous NVF with the tank vent valve closed to the desired final fill level, typically >90%. 



 Multiple methods have been proposed to prechill the tank to the target temperature before a NVF can occur. 



Chato and Sanabria [3] pioneered the charge/hold/vent (CHV) method. During the charge period, a small quantity of 



liquid cryogen is injected into the evacuated receiver tank. Initially, the liquid flashes due to the low tank pressure, 



and then the remaining liquid droplets evaporate as they contact warm vapor or the tank wall. During the hold period, 



the circulating flow pattern provides convective heat transfer from cold vapor to the tank wall. The primary mode of 



heat transfer during the hold is convection. At the completion of the hold period, the pressure has risen considerably, 



most, if not all of the liquid has boiled or evaporated, and the tank is ready to be vented. Since venting occurs as an 



isentropic blowdown, some additional cooling may be recovered by stage-wise venting [4]. The CHV process is 



repeated cyclically until the tank wall temperature is at or below the target temperature. 



 A simpler method is that of Vented-Chill / No-Vent-Fill (VCNVF) [5]. In this method, the tank vent valve 



is open for an initial period while the tank walls are chilled with a spray of cryogenic liquid that boils to vapor and 



exits through the vent. When the tank walls have been sufficiently chilled, the vent valve is closed, and the tank is 



filled with liquid. Care must be taken with the timing of the vent closure. If the vent is closed too early, residual heat 



in the walls may drive sufficient boil-off to raise the tank pressure high enough to stall the flow. If the vent is closed 



too late, some liquid propellant may be lost out the vent. Compared to the CHV method, VCNVF is less mass efficient; 



the gas being vented during the chilldown may not extract the maximum possible thermal energy from the tank wall. 



There is always the risk that some of the liquid propellant will be vented. However, VCNVF is much simpler from 



an operational standpoint. The transfer lines need to be chilled down only once and there is less cycling of inlet and 



vent valves. Since there is no hold period, overall loading time is likely to be shorter. 



 A third and possibly even simpler method than the VCNVF or CHV/NVF is using a cryocooler to prechill 



the receiver tank below the target temperature. However, high efficiency spaceflight cryocoolers are a developing 



technology, require extra system mass, and most importantly require electrical power that the spacecraft may not be 



able to afford. In the absence of cryocoolers, using the cooling potential of the working fluid is straightforward. 



 NVFs have been performed on a variety of tanks in the literature, see [5] for example for an up-to-date 



summary of previous testing performed. As shown, NVF with cryogens is a complex process that involves multiple 



two-phase heat and mass transfer phenomena, such as boiling at the wall and evaporation and condensation at the 



liquid/vapor interface. Being able to predict and control NVF is a challenging and necessary step in enabling future 



in-space propellant transfers. In particular, being able to predict the receiver tank pressure, fill level, wall temperature, 



propellant consumed, and filling time will be crucial. 



 The success of future in-space cryogenic propellant transfer systems and missions will be augmented by 



accurately being able to model the various stages of cryogenic transfer. Poor models lead to higher launch mass. 



Nearly all CFD models as well as lumped capacitance models rely on robust correlations or subroutines at the 



fundamental level. Recently, attention has been drawn to the fact that existing room-temperature based models and 



correlations do a poor job in predicting certain cryogenic phenomena, yet most correlations used in the most popular 



thermal/fluid design codes are based on room-temperature fluids. To this end, work is being performed across the 



board to update and upgrade the existing subroutines in these thermal/fluid design codes, where appropriate. 



 Initial NVF modeling efforts included simplified lumped capacitance models pioneered by researchers such 



as Chato [6]. In the 1990s and 2000s, higher fidelity lumped capacitance models were used to model NVF, such as 



the Generalized Fluid System Simulation Program (GFSSP) [7] and SINDA/FLUINT. GFSSP was previously used to 



model the CHV and NVF of the system from [3] and showed reasonable agreement with test data. GFSSP was also 



used to model the VCNVF method of filling the small flightweight CRYOTE tank with liquid nitrogen tests from [5]. 



The GFSSP model was upgraded to include subroutines for boiling at the tank wall and condensation and evaporation 



heat and mass transfer. The model results compared quite favorably with the test data. The tank filling model of the 



CRYOTE tank described in [5] was extended to simulate no-vent transfer tank filling with a Thermodynamic Vent 



System (TVS) assisted spray injector from [8]. The model performed quite well against the data, and results showed 



that GFSSP captured each phase of the no-vent transfer (initial pressure rise, stall, recovery, condensation at the 



liquid/vapor interface, and final ullage compression); results are available in [9]. A two-dimensional GFSSP model of 



the liquid nitrogen tests from [5] was also developed within the framework of the nodal network code to capture the 



thermal stratification and recirculating flow due to natural convection, and results agreed well against the data [10].

II. Test Description 



NVF tests from [13] are used to compare against the GFSSP model in the current work. Details are presented 



in [13], only a brief description of the system is given here. Chato conducted 9 NVF experiments on a 175 ft3 (4.96 



m3) spheroidal Aluminum (Al) flight-weight tank using LH2. Two injectors were tested, a top spray and a bottom jet; 



the focus of the current paper is on modeling the top spray tests. The top spray nozzle was composed of a manifold of 



13 full cone 120o nozzles as shown in Figure 1. The nozzle was mounted at the 92% fill level approximately.

 The receiver tank is ellipsoidal with an 87 in (2.2 m) major diameter and a 1.2-to-1 major-to-minor axis ratio. 



The two top and bottom halves were joined together by a short 1.5 in (3.8 cm) cylindrical section. The tank was 



constructed out of 2219 Al and had a thickness of 0.087 in (2.21 mm). The tank was covered with a blanket of 34 



layers of multi-layer insulation made with double aluminized mylar and silk net spaces. Overall, the tank weighed 



~329 lbm (149 kg) and was rated for a maximum operating pressure of 50 psi (345 kPa). 



 Tests were performed at NASA Glenn Plumbrook facility, also known at the time as K-Site. Figure 2 shows 



a simplified schematic of the test facility and receiver tank. For testing, the tank was mounted inside a vacuum chamber 



where the background pressure was maintained below 1.33x10-3 Pa. A cryoshroud was installed inside the chamber 



which provided a uniform heat transfer environment. 



 Temperature sensors were placed inside the tank along a vertical rake, and along the tank wall, lid, and at the 



inlet to the receiver tank. Pressure was measured at the inlet and inside the receiver tank. A capacitance probe was 



used to determine the liquid level as the tank filled. Flow rate of the incoming fluid was also measured via a flow 



meter. 



 The experimental methodology was as follows: The test began with prechilling the receiver tank so that the 



tank lid, flange, and tank were at or below the desired starting temperature. Any residual liquid in the tank was then 



emptied and the tank was vented to partial vacuum (~2 psia). Once this tank pressure was achieved, the receiver tank 



vent valve was closed to initiate a controlled NVF. A bypass valve was used to flow subcooled LH2 up to the inlet of

III. Description of Numerical Model 



Numerical modeling of filling a tank with liquid cryogen is highly complex. The physical processes are 



complex because the flow is unsteady, two-phase, and involves boiling and condensation with heat transfer between 



solid and fluid. The objectives of a numerical model of filling a cryogenic tank are to predict the pressure, fill level, 



temperature, and time and amount of propellant consumption to fill the tank. Following is a description of the GFSSP 



model used for NVF modeling. 



 



A. Introduction to GFSSP 



GFSSP is a general-purpose computer program for analyzing fluid flow and heat transfer in a complex 



network of fluid and solid systems. It employs a pressure based, finite volume algorithm [14] that solves the mass 



and energy conservation equations at the nodes and the momentum conservation equation at the branches connecting 



the nodes. The energy conservation equations for the solid are solved at solid nodes that are connected with the fluid 



nodes to allow conjugate heat transfer. The mass and momentum conservation equations along with the equation of 



state are solved simultaneously to calculate pressure, flow rate, and resident mass using the Newton-Raphson method. 



The energy conservation equations are solved by a successive substitution method to calculate enthalpies at the nodes 



within the iteration loop of a Newton-Raphson scheme. 



Thermodynamic properties are calculated for propellants using the computer program GASP [15], which is 



integrated with GFSSP. The fluid resistance library includes pipes, orifices, and common fittings and valves. GFSSP 



has three major parts. The first part is the graphical user interface MIG (Modeling Interface for GFSSP). MIG allows 



users to create a flow circuit by a “point and click” paradigm. It creates the GFSSP input file after the completion of 



the model building process. It can also create a customized GFSSP executable by compiling and linking User 



Subroutines with the solver module of the code. The user can run GFSSP from MIG and post-process the results in 



the same environment. The second major part of the program is the Solver and Property Module. This is the heart of 



the program, which reads the input data file and generates the required conservation equations for fluid and solid nodes 



and branches with the help of thermodynamic property programs. It also interfaces with User Subroutines to receive 



any specific inputs from users. Finally, it creates output files for MIG to read and display results. The User Subroutine 



is the third major part of the program. This consists of several blank subroutines that are called by the Solver Module.

Further details of the finite volume procedure and the computer program are described in [7, 9, 10, 14]. 



 



B. Pool Boiling Model 



The chilldown of a propellant tank is primarily governed by boiling of liquid propellant at the tank wall. 



Figure 3 shows different pool boiling regimes. During chilldown, the heat flux versus temperature history of the solid 



surface moves from right to left (E - D - C – B – A). Initially, the difference between the solid wall surface temperature 



and saturation temperature (wall superheat) is typically several hundred degrees. Therefore, the boiling starts in the 



Film Boiling regime and continues until it reaches the Leidenfrost (Minimum Film Boiling, MFB) point. After 



reaching the Leidenfrost point, it switches to Transition Boiling and continues to Critical Heat Flux. Nucleate Boiling 



starts after reaching Critical Heat Flux and, finally, heat transfer is controlled by natural convection beyond the Onset 



of Nucleate Boiling (ONB) point. 



GFSSP uses a set of correlations that have been individually fit and validated against over 10,000 cryogenic 



pool boiling data points. The correlations are patched together to ensure continuity at each of the transition points. 



The correlations are called as a user defined function (UDF) in GFSSP to provide a smooth, continuous function of 



wall heat flux versus wall superheat. For specific information on the correlations, the reader is referred to [16].

C. Condensation Model 



 



1. Condensation on a droplet from liquid spray 



Some of the vapor in the tank ullage condenses during the process of filling the tank by spraying liquid 



propellant in the tank. Condensation of vapor occurs when the vapor comes in contact with the subcooled liquid spray. 



Condensation reduces the tank pressure and therefore must be accounted for in the numerical model to predict the 



pressure and the resident liquid mass in the tank. A simple condensation model was developed [16] for such analysis. 



It is assumed that the liquid spray in the tank remains in the form of droplets of constant effective diameter. The 



number of droplets in the tank is estimated from the resident mass. The method of estimating the condensation rate 



was previously described in [9]. 



 



2. Condensation at the liquid pool/vapor interface 



At the liquid-vapor interface, there is a possibility of either evaporation or condensation depending upon the 



condition of vapor and liquid. The phase change occurs as a result of the imbalance between sensible heat transfer 



rates on the vapor and liquid sides of the interface. If the heat transfer from the ullage to the interface (QUI) is larger 



than the heat transfer from the interface to liquid (QIL), evaporation of liquid occurs as shown in Figure 4. On the other 



hand, if the heat transfer from the interface to liquid (QIL) is larger than the heat transfer from the ullage to the interface

 In the CRYOTE experiments from [5], the pressures and temperatures at the injector inlet were measured, 



and the measured pressure was used as the boundary pressure. It was also observed that the predicted tank pressure 



was similar to the boundary pressure, but lower due to the pressure drop in the spray nozzle. This information was 



critical for accurate prediction of the receiver tank pressure. In the experiment being modeled here [13], the pressure 



at the nozzle inlet was not provided. The pressure in the supply tank was provided, but this was far upstream from the 



injector spray nozzle. There was not enough information about the transfer line connecting the supply tank and the 



receiver tank such that a model could be extended to the supply tank enabling an accurate prediction of inlet pressure 



at the receiver tank. Therefore, an iterative method to calculate the boundary pressure was developed. Here are the 



steps of the iterative scheme employed in GFSSP’s UDF: 



1. Assume a pressure at inlet boundary (Node 13 in Figure 5) for the current time step. 



2. Obtain the converged solution of the flow network with the assumed pressure at the inlet boundary. 



3. Calculate the total flowrate through the nozzle entering the tank (i.e., sum of flowrate in branches 131 through 



139 in Figure 5). 



4. Check the total flowrate with the measured average flowrate for the particular test as shown in Table 4. 



5. If the flowrates do not match within the acceptable accuracy level, adjust the pressure at the inlet boundary 



using a Newton-Raphson scheme of correcting the pressure. 



6. Repeat steps 2 to 5 until the difference between the calculated flowrate (at step 3) and measured flowrate of 



Table 4 match within 0.1%. 



7. Proceed to the next time step and repeat steps 1 to 6 until the last time step is reached.

V. Conclusion 



 Developing accurate models of various cryogenic propellant transfer phenomena will reduce risk and reduce 



margins in the design of future in-space cryogenic propellant vehicles. Particularly, the process of filling a cryogenic 



propellant tank is challenging to model due to the various competing two-phase heat and mass transfer phenomena 



that occur during filling. This paper presented model validation results of a GFSSP numerical model of the non-vented 



filling of a large liquid hydrogen propellant tank. Three tests were simulated over a range of initial receiver tank wall 



temperatures and mass inflow rate. The GFSSP model with newly developed user-defined subroutines for boiling at 



the wall and various condensation and evaporation processes predicts the data quite well. The GFSSP model captures 



the various stages of NVF, flash evaporation, boiling at the wall, condensation and evaporation, and ullage 



compression. When compared to pressure, fill level and tank wall temperature, the model predicts the data well within 



20%, omitting errors due to the offset in the temperature sensor for Test 20. The GFSSP model with new UDFs has 



thus been shown to agree well with cryogenic NVF data across various tank scales, cryogens, flow rates, and initial 



receiver tank wall temperatures.
