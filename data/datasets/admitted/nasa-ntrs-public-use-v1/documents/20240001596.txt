<p>1. INTRODUCTION 
</p>
<p>For damage tolerance testing of polymer matrix composites, impact damage is typically inflicted 
</p>
<p>using a rounded tip impactor to represent a generic impact event. The ASTM standard for 
</p>
<p>Compression After Impact (CAI) testing [1] calls for a blunt, hemispherical striker tip, although 
</p>
<p>it does state that the use of a sharp striker tip may be appropriate in certain circumstances. In 
</p>
<p>actual practice, many different forms of impactor shapes can impact a composite structure; from 
</p>
<p>very large impactors such as bumpers on ground handling equipment [2] to very small 
</p>
<p>“puncture-type” impacts such as a Kevlar rocket motor case than was hit against a wall with a 
</p>
<p>protruding stove bolt in 1991. This study will focus on the damage tolerance differences between 
</p>
<p>two commonly used sized hemispherical impactors (12.7 mm and 24.5 mm) and a sharp tipped 
</p>
<p>impactor. 
</p>
<p>Past studies that the authors are familiar with where sharp tip impactors were used 
</p>
<p>involved damage tolerance of rocket motor cases [3-5]. For rocket motor cases, the laminate was 
</p>
<p>shown to be so thick that there was little difference in the impact response of sharp versus blunt 
</p>
<p>impactors. The thick laminate enabled the first few layers to crush at the tip of the sharp 
</p>
<p>impactors and the debris of these first few plies acted as a “blunting mechanism” ahead of the 
</p>
<p>sharp tip.  
</p>
<p>However, there is space flight hardware made of thinner laminates than those of rocket 
</p>
<p>motor cases and some of these involve sandwich structure with relatively thin face sheets. 
</p>
<p>For thinner laminates some results in the open literature relevant to this current study 
</p>
<p>were found comparing the impact response of different shape and size impactors [6-13]. Three of 
</p>
<p>these references [6,10,13] provided post impact compression strength results and two utilized 
</p>
<p>sandwich structure [6,13]. The results showed that, as far as the impact response, of commonality 
</p>
<p>in all these studies was that, for a given impact energy, the damage size as determined by non-
</p>
<p>destructive evaluation (NDE) techniques was smaller for the sharper (or smaller diameter) 
</p>
<p>impactors and dent depth was larger for the sharper or smaller diameter impactors. For CAI 
</p>
<p>strength response, mixed results were found. One study that utilized sandwich structure [6] 
</p>
<p>showed that, in general, the larger impactor gave lower CAI values for a given impact energy but 
</p>
<p>another study [10] showed that a larger diameter impactor gave higher CAI strength values for a 
</p>
<p>given impact energy. A study using foam core sandwich structure [13] found mixed results in 
</p>
<p>that the CAI strength was lower for a sharp tipped impactor at low impact energy values, but 
</p>
<p>higher at higher impact energy levels. 
</p>
<p>This study examined the effect of impactor shape (sharp tip) and diameter (12.7 and 25.4 
</p>
<p>mm) on the CAI strength response of aluminum honeycomb core sandwich structure that may be 
</p>
<p>used for launch vehicle structure. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>2. MATERIALS 
 
</p>
<p>The face sheets of the sandwich specimens tested in this study consisted of IM7 carbon fiber 
</p>
<p>with 8552-1 epoxy resin and were co-cured to the aluminum honeycomb core which had a 
</p>
<p>density of 72 kg/m3, cell size of 3.175 mm and 2.54 cm thickness.  All the face sheets were 
</p>
<p>manufactured by automated fiber placement (AFP) at NASA’s MSFC. The layup for the face 
</p>
<p>sheets was 8-ply [-45/90/+45/0]S quasi-isotropic with the 0⁰ fiber direction coinciding with the 
</p>
<p>“L” direction of the honeycomb. The sandwich structure had a layer of FM 300-2M epoxy film 
</p>
<p>adhesive placed over the core prior to the automated tape laying process used to manufacture the 
</p>
<p>face sheets. </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>4 
</p>
<p> The sandwich structure was cured in an autoclave with a pressure of 276 kPa and a 
</p>
<p>temperature of 175 ⁰C. The flat sandwich panel made for this study was 122 cm × 61 cm in size. 
</p>
<p>The sandwich structure showed good consolidation with some minor porosity in the bottom plies 
</p>
<p>as seen in the cross-sectional photomicrographs shown in figure 1. Using photomicroscopy and 
</p>
<p>measuring tools contained within the software attached to the microscope, the nominal face sheet 
</p>
<p>thickness of the sandwich structure was measured at 1.27 mm. 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p> 
Figure 1. Cross sectional photomicrographs of a face sheet showing good consolidation and minor 
porosity in lower plies. Specimen cuts are in width direction (left) and loading direction (right). 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>The sandwich structure was cut into 15.2 cm tall (direction of loading) by 10.2 cm wide 
</p>
<p>specimens using a diamond saw. The top and bottom edges of these specimens had to be filled 
</p>
<p>with a paste epoxy to prevent end brooming. This was accomplished by crushing the core at the 
</p>
<p>ends by about 6 mm as shown in figure 2a and then filling the channel with EA 9394 paste epoxy 
</p>
<p>as shown in figure 2b. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
</p>
<p>Figure 2. Photograph of the process to “pot” the ends to prevent end brooming during compression 
testing. a) crush the core down about 6 mm on ends and b) fill the resulting channel with epoxy paste 
adhesive. 
</p>
<p> 
 
</p>
<p>The “potted” ends were then machined to ±0.0025 mm tolerance of parallelism using a 
</p>
<p>vertical end mill with a solid carbide cutting tool (Onsrud 67-526 designed for carbon fiber 
</p>
<p>machining). The side edges of the specimens were machined to be perpendicular to the top and 
</p>
<p>bottom edges. 
</p>
<p>Undamaged strength testing of the sandwich structure was not pursued in this study since 
</p>
<p>the undamaged specimens exhibited end-brooming despite the potted ends. This was deemed 
</p>
<p>suitable since this study concerns damage tolerance testing and the differences between impactor 
</p>
<p>shapes and sizes and the undamaged strength values are not relevant to the scope of this study. 
</p>

<p>3.0 IMPACT DAMAGE TESTING 
 
</p>
<p>An instrumented drop weight impact apparatus was used in this study. The mass and height of 
</p>
<p>the impactor could be varied to get a variety of impact energies. A pneumatic rebound brake 
</p>
<p>prevented multiple impacts on the specimen. Each specimen was clamped between two steel 
</p>
<p>plates during impact. The top steel plate had an opening to allow the impactor to pass through 
</p>
<p>and hit the specimen. The bottom plate was solid with no cutout. 
</p>
<p> Three impactors were used in this study, two hemispherical impactors, one of diameter 
</p>
<p>25.4 mm and one of 12.7 mm and a conical sharp tipped impactor with a diameter of 19.0 mm. 
</p>
<p>Photographs of the three types of impactors are shown in figure 3. 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

Figure 3. Photograph of three types of impactors used in this study.

</p>
<p>Preliminary CAI strength testing showed that at impact energies less than 2.7 J failure was by 
</p>
<p>end brooming and thus 2.7 J was chosen as a lower impact energy bound. At 8.1 J the 12.7 mm 
</p>
<p>tup began to penetrate the face sheet, and this was chosen as an upper bound impact energy. 
</p>
<p> It was decided to test the specimens with all three types of impactors with these two 
</p>
<p>impact energies bracketing the high and low impact energies and using one intermediate impact 
</p>
<p>energy (5.4 J). Thus, the honeycomb core sandwich specimens were to be impacted at 2.7, 5.4 
</p>
<p>and 8.1 J with each of the three types of impactors for a total of nine impact conditions. Multiple 
</p>
<p>specimens were tested at each of the nine impact conditions. Although the impacts were 
</p>
<p>instrumented, the instrumented impact results (load-deflection curves) will not be presented here 
</p>
<p>for two reasons. First, the load-deflection data was suspect for the sharp tip and 25.4 mm 
</p>
<p>impactors since they were drastically different than static indentation results (excess “ringing” in 
</p>
<p>the impact data was present despite applying numerous filters to the data). Second, in actual 
</p>
<p>practice an object that may impact a composite part is not instrumented and thus the 
</p>
<p>instrumented data is only of academic interest and does little to help disposition an actual impact 
</p>
<p>event. Thus, only post impact parameters (ones that could be measured in the field) were 
</p>
<p>measured and assessed in this study. 
</p>

<p>3.1 Visual Damage 
</p>
<p>Examples of the visual damage caused by each of the nine impact conditions are shown in the 
</p>
<p>photographs in figure 4. The main takeaway from the visual impact results is that the damage 
</p>
<p>from the sharp impactor is easier to see and will result in a lower impact energy for any visual 
</p>

</p>
<p>visual damage between the 12.7 mm and 25.4 mm impactors is not as notable except at the 
</p>

4.0 COMPRESSION AFTER IMPACT TESTING 
</p>
<p> 
 
The impacted sandwich specimens were assessed for residual compression strength using the test 
</p>
<p>fixture shown in figure 9. Three strain gages were placed on the specimen to ensure even loading 
</p>
<p>of each of the face sheets. Two gages on the impact side were to ensure even loading across the 
</p>
<p>specimen width and one gage in the center on the opposite (non-impacted) side to monitor for 
</p>
<p>even loading across the specimen thickness. The specimens were taken to approximately 1000 
</p>
<p>microstrain compression and if one gage was lower than the others by more than 10%, shims 
</p>
<p>were placed under the edge that was reading low until the gages were even. During compression 
</p>
<p>testing the gages were monitored and if any deviation greater than 10% occurred, the test was 
</p>
<p>stopped, and shims would be rearranged until the gages read within 10% of each other all the 
</p>
<p>way until failure of the specimen.

<p> 
5.0 CONCLUSIONS 
</p>
<p> 
 
The effects of 25.4 mm and 12.7 mm diameter hemispherical impactors and a sharp impactor on 
</p>
<p>the impact response (visual damage, dent depth and damage size as detected by thermography) 
</p>
<p>and CAI strength of honeycomb core sandwich structure with carbon/epoxy face sheets has been 
</p>
<p>investigated in this study. The results show that the impact response and resulting CAI strength 
</p>
<p>values, especially of the sharp impactor, are different for the three types of impactors. 
</p>
<p> The visual damage is much easier to see with the sharp impactor which would be 
</p>
<p>beneficial in actual practice since this increases the chances of an impact event being discovered. 
</p>
<p>Sharp Impactor 
</p>
<p>12.7 mm Impactor 
</p>
<p>25.4 mm Impactor </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>16 
</p>
<p>For the two sizes of hemispherical impactors, the visual damage is similar until higher impact 
</p>
<p>energies when the smaller hemispherical impactor begins to penetrate the face sheet. 
</p>
<p> The dent depth measurements were larger for the sharp impactor across all energy levels 
</p>
<p>tested. The differences in dent depth of the two hemispherical impactors did not vary much until 
</p>
<p>the highest impact energy level used (8.1 J) was reached where the larger impactor gave a 
</p>
<p>smaller dent depth. This was due to the beginning of penetration of the face sheet for the 12.7 
</p>
<p>mm impactor at the 8.1 J energy level. 
</p>
<p>Thermography results showed the sharp impactor yielding a smaller damage zone for a 
</p>
<p>given impact energy for all three impact energy levels tested. The difference in damage size 
</p>
<p>between the two hemispherical impactors was not notably different. 
</p>
<p> The CAI results showed that the sharp impactor gave essentially the same CAI strength 
</p>
<p>values regardless of impact energy or damage size. This suggests that at the three impact energy 
</p>
<p>levels used in this study that the sharp tipped impactor essentially punctured a hole in the 
</p>
<p>specimen and the specimen behaved as such giving a constant CAI strength value as if a hole of 
</p>
<p>a given size was drilled through the face sheet. The CAI strength results for the hemispherical 
</p>
<p>impactors was more “well behaved” in terms of showing a decrease in CAI strength with an 
</p>
<p>increase in impact energy or damage size. Differences in CAI strength between the 12.7 mm and 
</p>
<p>25.4 mm impactors was most notably evident at the higher impact severity levels where the 25.4 
</p>
<p>mm impactor gave higher CAI strength values. 
</p>
<p> These results raise practical questions about using impact energy alone, or even 
</p>
<p>measurable post impact parameters, as metrics to define an impact event, as is commonly done in 
</p>
<p>“damage threat assessments” since the impact response and subsequent CAI strength can be 
</p>
<p>quite different depending on the shape and size of the impactor. It is suggested to use a lower 
</p>
<p>bound strain allowable rather than attempting to develop a CAI strength allowable based on 
</p>
<p>testing using the typical methodology of using one hemispherical size impactor since in practice, 
</p>
<p>any shape impactor may hit the piece of hardware. 
</p>
