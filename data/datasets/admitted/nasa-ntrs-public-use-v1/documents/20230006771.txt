Hybrid Modeling of Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Electric
Powertrain for Fault Detection and Diagnostics



Matteo Corbetta∗



KBR Inc., NASA Ames Research Center, Moffett Field, CA, 94035

This paper shows the application of hybrid physics-informed machine learning to a rep-
resentative electric powertrain for unmanned aerial vehicles. The model is composed of
physics-derived principles and empirical equations, as well as fully connected networks that are
strategically placed within the model to substitute equations that are subject to large uncertainty.
Polynomial fitting driven by heuristics or empirical observations is replaced by more flexible
networks that can minimize the error between model predictions and observations without
being restricted to a predefined functional form. This approach allows training of networks
deep inside the model and unknown parameters in a single learning stage. It has already
been applied to Li-ion batteries in the past, and in this work the application is extended to
include other electric powertrain components, specifically an electronic speed controller with
pulse-width modulation, and brush-less DC motor with connected propeller. Training and
testing of the model is carried out using experimental data from Li-ion battery discharge in a
laboratory environment and synthetic data from simulated speed controller, three-phase motor
and fixed-pitch propeller.

I. Introduction



Forecasts on the future of aeronautics suggest a drastic increase in electric aircraft entering low-altitude airspace
in the near future. Both small Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) and urban air mobility vehicles are expected to



operate in complex urban environments at high frequency, resulting in a high number of take-off and landings per
unit time. To adapt to these new design and operational requirements, aircraft are expected to be equipped with new
electric powertrains subject to unknown failure modes and degradation patterns. Precise fault detection and failure
prediction systems will be necessary, not only to maintain or exceed current aviation safety standards and optimize
maintenance policies, but also to enable high-level autonomous decision making and improve system resilience to
unexpected disruptions.



Fault detection and diagnosis requires knowledge about the discrepancy between how a system is currently behaving
and how it is expected to perform when healthy. When current sensor measurements deviate too far from the expected,
a fault may be occurring, and once identified, diagnostic and mitigation efforts can begin. The ability to distinguish
anomalous conditions requires a model of the healthy system, whose accuracy is high enough to detect faults when
compared with sensor measurements. While a variety of modeling techniques can be used to accurately describe the
system (e.g. physics-based, data-driven, or hybrid), all models result in some error. One key challenge of fault detection
is determining when discrepancies between model predictions and system measurements are meaningful faults, and
when the differences can be attributed to modeling errors. Success in fault detection and diagnostics requires a model
that is well-calibrated to the physical system it describes.



Given this new set of challenges driven by new vehicle design, new operational requirements, and new environments,
the goal of this work is to explore a novel surrogate model architecture based on Hybrid Physics-Informed Neural
Networks (H-PINNs), and apply it to aircraft electric propulsion systems and fault detection. In the recent years, this
blending of physical and engineering knowledge within machine learning algorithms has been explored. In the area of
neural networks, one of the seminal works can be attributed to Daw and co-workers in lake temperature modeling [1].
They introduced a physical constraint on one of the variables of interest (water density) in the loss function used to train
a neural network model. The constraint was very simple yet effective: water density must increase with depth. If such
relationship is not satisfied, then the loss should rise proportionally. Subsequently, a paper by Raissi et al. [2] applied
the same philosophy to the modeling of partial differential equations. In this work, the differential equations, as well as
the initial and boundary conditions, were embedded in the loss function, encouraging the training to find solutions
that satisfied the physical constraints. This methodology has been extended to a number of applications where partial
differential equations or stochastic differential equations fully describe the system. More recently, Lu et al. worked on a
deep neural network architecture to learn nonlinear operators, for cases where the differential equations are not known



2











[3]. Other attempts to connect physics to neural networks include Lagrangian and Hamiltonian networks, neural ordinary
differential equations and others. Gaussian processes provide another physics-informed machine learning method,
where it is possible to draw samples from a distribution conditioned on linear differential equations, as summarized in
[4]. Lastly, data-driven discovery of governing equations of physical systems has also been discussed in a number of
recent papers including [5, 6], and [7].



In this work, we present a novel H-PINN modeling method and apply it to describing aircraft propulsion systems. Our
approach differs from the methodologies discussed above, and takes advantage of the ability of deep learning libraries
to perform automatic differentiation of models. We do not try to embed physical knowledge in a pure data-driven
model, but rather build an empirical model composed of physics-based principles and phenomenological equations, and
place small neural networks and trainable parameters within the model. These data-driven kernels (fully-connected
networks (FCN) with one or more hidden layers) are strategically placed within the physics-based model to replace
empirical equations previously obtained through curve-fitting or other empirical procedures. Additionally, we define
some single-value parameters, either directly or indirectly related to the physics of the system, as trainable through
gradient descent, achievable through the automatic differentiation feature of the deep learning package. While similar
approaches have been applied to other modeling and prediction problems, to the best of our knowledge, they have never
been applied to a propulsion system like the one presented here.



This specific approach was initially proposed by Viana and co-authors [8–11], and has successfully been applied to
Li-ion battery discharge predictions in [12]. In the work presented here, we extend the modeling effort from [12] to a
powertrain system composed of a Li-ion battery, electronic speed controller (ESC), brushless-DC (BLDC) motor and
dual-blade propeller typical of small UAVs. The physics-based portion of our model includes an electro-chemistry
Li-ion battery model, a simplified ESC (wihtout PWM, using a simple proportional modulation), a single-phase DC
motor model, and a propeller. In the battery model, we train two parameters, 𝑅0 and 𝑞𝑚𝑎𝑥 , related to the battery residual
capacity and the battery internal resistance, respectively, together with two FCNs to compensate for the output voltage
estimate error from the simplified Nernst’s equation. In the simplified single-phase BLDC motor model, we train the
armature resistance 𝑅𝑠 and the friction coefficient 𝐵, as well as a FCN after the rotor speed estimate from the BLDC
motor to correct for any rotor speed prediction error. By combining physics-based knowledge with neural networks, we
develop a more accurate powertrain model that is well-calibrated to a healthy system and therefore easily lends to fault
detection and diagnosis.



II. Powertrain Architecture and Models
The powertrain is modeled according to the diagram in Fig. 1. A Li-ion battery is connected to a DC/DC converter,



which provides voltage to the ESC. The ESC is composed of a pulse-width-modulation (PWM) unit and MOSFET
switches. It generates a three-phase voltage which drives the BLDC motor to produce the torque necessary to drive the
propeller, which ultimately produces the desired thrust. Inputs and outputs of the sub-systems are highlighted in cyan in
Fig. 1. The observed values include measurements of voltage, current, and temperature at the battery level, 𝑉𝑏, 𝑖𝑏, 𝑇𝑏,
respectively, as well as at the motor level 𝑉𝑚, 𝑖𝑚, 𝑇𝑚, and are indicated by black arrows in Fig. 1. These values are
representative of typical sensor readings for small UAVs without dedicated or advanced sensors and measurement
systems. The input to the PWM module defines a square wave indicating intervals of “on” and “off”, where the period is
given by the operating frequency. For example, an input of 𝑢=0.7 into the PWM means the square wave is split into two
parts, 70% “on” and 30% “off.” The PWM carrier frequency, for the propulsion system we used as reference, is on the
order of 16-32 kHz.



The trainable elements of the model are implemented in PyTorch and shown in red in Fig. 1. In the battery module,
parameters 𝑞𝑚𝑎𝑥 , 𝑅0 are indirectly connected to physical quantities, and drive the rate of change of voltage discharge.
Additionally, two FCNs are used to compensate for the error of the equilibrium potential calculated by the simplified
Nernst’s equation, and are defined as non-ideal voltage correction terms, 𝑉𝑛𝑖 (one for the positive and one for the
negative side of the electrode). In the motor module, two other physics-derived quantities are learned during training.
These trainable values are the motor armature resistance 𝑅𝑠 and the friction coefficient 𝐵, affecting the current and
rotor speed rates-of-change, respectively. To highlight the flexibility of the approach, we additionally added one FCN
after the BLDC motor model estimate, aimed at correcting the error between rotor speed predictions and observations
extracted from the data set. More details on the trainable elements are discussed later in the dedicated sub-sections.

and the interested reader can refer to [12] for further details.

often hard to retrieve from manufacturers’ data sheets and subject to large uncertainty.



C. Electronic Speed Controller
Since we are adopting a single-phase motor model, we eliminated the complexity related to the pulse-width



modulation and the ESC switches forcing the three-phase dynamics. Both voltage and current are scalar quantities.
Therefore, we can drive the motor speed by multiplying the throttle value by the voltage coming from the DC/DC
converter, 𝑉𝑚 = 𝑉0 𝑢. At this stage, we assume the DC/DC converter is capable of providing constant voltage with no
fluctuations, but the framework allows the modeling of the converter to be embedded in the powertrain.

III. Data Sets and Model Training Details
The data set utilized to train the Li-ion battery model is composed of both low-amperage, constant discharge cycles



carried out at 1A, useful to estimate the residual capacity of the battery, and step-wise random loading discharges where
the current load is sampled from a uniform distribution 𝑖 ∼ U([1, 4]), 𝑖 ∈ R, every 300 s, which is more representative
of a real operational profile. Figure 2a shows an example of input current and output voltage from a constant discharge
(upper panel) and random-walk discharge (bottom panel) from Battery # 2 in the "Randomized Battery Usage" data set,
NASA Prognostics Data Repository. The batteries were subject to several loading cycles to observe the effect of battery
aging, however, aging is not discussed in this work; all results reported here refer to discharge curves from the early
stages of the battery life. Although the data set is sampled at 1 Hz, we down-sample the constant discharge curves to 0.1
Hz to speed up training, since the voltage dynamic is slow-changing when the battery is subject to constant, low current
input values.

To train the BLDC model parameters and the FCN to compensate for rotor speed prediction errors, we generated
synthetic data from a higher-fidelity powertrain model, composed of a Li-ion Battery, ESC with PWM, three-phase
BLDC motor, and fixed-pitch propeller. The model parameters for BLDC and ESC were taken from the datasheet of
UAV propulsion system we used as reference, KDE. The time series used for training include throttle values, voltage
values from the DC/DC converter (assumed constant for simplicity at this stage), and rotor speed, which is used as a
dependent variable (output) of the powertrain. Figure 2b shows three examples of time series according to the three
different types of input profiles we selected for training: constant (top panel), step-wise constant (middle panel), and
sinusoidal (bottom panel) throttle. All profiles start from a stationary condition with null input and null rotor speed and
are run for 0.5 s with a time step size of d𝑡 = 10−4 s. Throttle value(s), frequency of oscillation, and time instant(s)
of throttle change are randomly sampled for each run. Throttle is sampled from a uniform distribution U([0.1, 1]),
and that applies for all three types of input profiles. Frequency for the sinusoidal load is sampled between 4 and 20
Hz following a uniform distribution, U([4, 20]). The mean value of the sinusoidal profile is sampled from the same
distribution of the throttle, U([0.1, 1]), and the amplitude of the sinusoid is kept fixed to 1/3, with cutoffs at 0 and 1
whenever the total signal exceeds those limits. For the step-wise constant profile, the two time instants at which the
throttle changes are sampled within the time vector range, again following a uniform distribution. The Appendix V
reports the details of the ESC with PWM and three-phase BLDC motor model used to generate the training data set.



In the data training implementation, we fit the battery parameters and FCNs separately from the motor parameters and
rotor speed correction FCN. This improves the computational efficiency and numerical stability of training. Additionally,
since the battery parameters are independent of the motor model and are trained with separate loss functions, this
separate optimization is equivalent to training the entire system together.



A. Battery Model Training
The battery model is trained with four voltage-discharge curves for each battery, two constant-discharge and two



random-walk discharge curves per specimen. Given inter-specimen variability, the battery model has a pair of (𝑞𝑚𝑎𝑥 ,
𝑅0) values for each physical battery. However, the FCNs used to compensate the error of the equilibrium potential
can be shared across all batteries, as found out in [12]. By so doing, the number of samples used to train the FCN
is the product of the number of curves for each battery specimen times the number of specimens. Fitting required
pre-training of the FCNs to ensure the non-ideal voltage correction would assume meaningful values throughout
training [12]. We used a linear curve to pre-train the network for 𝑉𝑛𝑖,𝑛 to obtain a range of values similar to the one
necessary in the battery model, and we used a piece-wise linear curve as a simplified representation of the error that
𝑉𝑛𝑖, 𝑝 has to compensate for. Specifically, the pre-training linear curve for 𝑉𝑛𝑖,𝑛 has been defined by the two points
(0.0,−8𝑒 − 4), (0.6, 2𝑒 − 4) while the piece-wise linear curve for 𝑉𝑛𝑖, 𝑝 has been defined as 𝑉𝑛𝑖, 𝑝 = −0.8 𝑥 + 4.3 for
𝑥𝑝 ∈ [0.4, 0.97] and 𝑉𝑛𝑖, 𝑝 = −19 𝑥 + 22 for 𝑥𝑝 ∈ [0.97, 1].



The FCN for 𝑉𝑛𝑖, 𝑝 was pre-trained for 3000 epochs, while the FCN for 𝑉𝑛𝑖,𝑛 was trained for 1000 epochs. The
learning rate was set to 2e-1 for both networks, and they were initialized with the standard PyTorch initialization function
for linear layers. After being pre-trained, the FCNs were embedded in the hybrid Battery model, and both physics-derived
parameters 𝑞𝑚𝑎𝑥 , 𝑅0 as well as weights and biases of the FCNs were trained simultaneously. We used different learning
rates for the physics parameters and the network weights, to minimize the chance of exploding and vanishing gradients.
We also added limits to 𝑞𝑚𝑎𝑥 , 𝑅0 to ensure they remaining within the range of physically-meaningful values. The
parameters are summarized in Table 2.

B. Rotor Speed Predictions
In this sub-section we report the results of the BLDC motor model parameter learning, as well as the training of



the FCN for rotor speed error correction. A first attempt is shown in Fig. 4. First, we trained the BLDC motor model
parameters only (Fig. 4a), and then we combined the training of the physics-derived parameters and the FCN for rotor
speed error correction (Fig. 4b). Both results where obtained using a batch size of 4 curves randomly selected from the
training set at each epoch. Training only 𝑅𝑠 and 𝐵 allows the model to capture the main trend of the rotor speed curve.
The ramp-up is perfectly captured, and the amplitude of the oscillations due to the sinusoidal throttle input profile are
captured better than with the default parameters (Fig. 4a). However, there is still some discrepancy between data and
model, and so we additionally train the FCN to capture this residual. We find that the introduction of this FCN does not
produce any additional benefit, as visible in Fig. 4(b). Additionally, a series of training runs performed over different
batch sizes, learning rates, and initialization of weights and biases did not show performance improvements. These
results were consistent over several test curves, despite only one test curve shown here for brevity. At this stage, we did
not identify any systematic issue within the models or with the training procedure.



In an attempt to improve fits, we then tried training the parameters 𝑅𝑠 and 𝐵 first, and then used a single training
curve to train the FCN weights and biases. This resulted in an improved fit, as seen on two different sinusoidal-input
curves in Figure 5. The zoomed subplots highlight the improvement of model predictions when the FCN is embedded.
Thus, the neural network is indeed capable of compensating for (some of) the rotor speed discrepancy when trained on a
single curve, but it converges to a sub-optimal solution when the training contains many curves as done in the previous
test from Fig. 4 (a).



Based on our analyses, we speculate that this issue is caused by inconsistent error trends between the predictions from
the physics-based model (without FCN) and observations across multiple rotor speed curves. In this case, including two
trained physical parameters and one FCN at the end of the model, right before comparing predictions to observations,
did not give enough flexibility to the single-phase model. It it likely that the FCN averages out the prediction error
among multiple curves, thus producing an “average” correction that does not significantly improve any single prediction
when compared to a model with only 𝑅𝑠 , 𝐵 as trainable parameters.



It is possible that the discrepancy between data and model predictions may be caused by the stiffness of the
single-phase BLDC motor model with only two trainable parameters. Adding more parameters to be trained could make
the powertrain model more flexible and therefore compensate for the discrepancy. However, another, possible explanation
is that the loss landscape generated by a mixture of physics-based and neural network parameters might be particularly
challenging, making it hard to find a good local minima. In that case, careful tuning of training hyper-parameters (like
optimizer parameters) or other training methods could be beneficial.

V. Conclusion
This paper presented the application of a hybrid physics-informed machine learning approach to a powertrain system



representative of those that can be found in small UAVs. The approach leveraged the PyTorch deep learning library and,
thanks to its automatic differentiation capability, allowed learning both physics-derived parameters as well as weights
and biases of neural networks strategically placed within the empirical model. The powertrain model can be seen as a
system-of-system model, albeit the sub-system models are simple, and this work allowed us to analyze advantages and
challenges related to the implementation of the proposed approach.



One such difficulty is network pre-training. We found that if neural networks are deep inside the model, pre-training
is necessary to successfully train the whole hybrid model. Without pre-training, the random output of the neural
network, caused by the random initialization of its weights, produces values that are not meaningful for the rest of the
physics-driven model. In the example shown here, we had a basic understanding of values and trend of the non-ideal
voltage correction term, which helped us set up an effective pre-training stage. But this step can be challenging in cases
where there is no knowledge of what the neural networks output is supposed to be.



Another challenge is driven by the simultaneous training of physics-derived parameters and network weights and
biases, as we observed in the rotor speed prediction case. This work has shown mixed results. In the battery model, the
simultaneous training of 𝑞𝑚𝑎𝑥 , 𝑅0 and the FCNs for voltage error correction converged to results that can be considered
acceptable for voltage discharge prediction. However, the simultaneous training of 𝑅𝑠 , 𝐵 and the FCN for rotor speed
error correction in the motor model did not produce the desired outcome, with the effect of the FCN being almost
negligible if not trained on a single curve after 𝑅𝑠 and 𝐵 were already trained. As we speculated earlier, this result
might be caused by the stiffness of the model we chose, thus deserving further study.



We also observed slow convergence of the model due to the dynamic nature of the system. By using a deep learning
library like PyTorch, both battery and motor dynamic equations need to be solved by integrating forward in time,
replicating the forward propagation pass of a recurrent neural network cell. Therefore, all input curves (current and
throttle profiles) have to be fed sequentially to the model. This makes the training fairly slow when compared against
pure data-driven models. This is likely to be caused by the forward pass of the physics model and the computation
of the gradient through physics-derived equations, which is typically more complicated than standard neural network
architectures. Its complexity increases with the complexity of the model, thus suggesting that training large hybrid
models might require GPUs or other advanced computing hardware. It should also be noticed, however, that other
implementations of the Li-ion battery model in Tensorflow has shown much faster computation time, at the cost of less
flexibility of implementation.



Future steps of this work include a deeper analysis of the training of the FCN for rotor speed error correction, to
understand the reason why the FCN cannot generalize the function learned from the residuals. After some preliminary
tests, we observed that the architecture for the FCN used for the rotor speed error correction is capable of predicting
complex residual functions. However, the successful training was performed off-line by treating the residuals as a



12











mapping function between input variables (𝑡, 𝜔𝑅𝑠 ,𝐵, 𝑉, 𝑡ℎ) and observed rotor speed. In addition, the single-phase
BLDC motor model might require more parameters, other than 𝑅𝑠 and 𝐵, to be trained to better fit the observations
from the three-phase model. This might allow the FCN to focus on the oscillatory residuals and avoid compensating for
biases. Training the model on real powertrain data obtained in a laboratory environment would then help us evaluate the
prediction performance of the model for future diagnostic applications.



Despite these challenges and future extensions, the H-PINN work presented here provides a novel, hybrid approach
to modeling aircraft electric propulsion systems. The embedding of the models in the PyTorch deep learning framework
allowed a modular approach where physics-derived or empirical parameters and data-driven kernels can be trained
simultaneously without the need for separate fitting methods. The dynamic nature of PyTorch graphs allow the forward
integration in time with no restriction of length of time series as well as the use of variable d𝑡 during the simulation, which
is another advantage for physical and engineering system models when compared to other deep learning frameworks
like TensorFlow. We are expecting, in the near future, to capture the nominal dynamics of a UAV powertrain system
with this modeling approach and to further investigate fault detection and diagnostic capabilities.
