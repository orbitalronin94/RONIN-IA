Abstract



Aircraft icing remains a significant threat to aviation safety. Software
that predicts the impingement and ice accretion on full aircraft
geometries and aircraft components are in demand and NASA Glenn
is committed to produce software that meets this need. One of the key
parameters affecting an accurate prediction of iced geometry is the
effect of ice roughness on the heat transfer coefficient. While many
efforts have been made to implement the roughness in the flow solver,
this report takes a correlation for roughness height distribution that is
based on experimental measurements and demonstrates how to relate
those measurements to an augmentation to the heat transfer coefficient
provided by the flow solution. The outcome of this effort was the
callibration of defaults for user supplied parameters to this correlation
through comparison with 95 large glaze conditions from experiment
by adjusting user-supplied parameters in the roughness augmentation
equation.



Introduction



Surface roughness has long been an important consideration in icing.
Early works focused on the measurement of roughness and its effect
on heat transfer [1–5]. The initial attempts to incorporate roughness
effects into icing simulation used an integral boundary layer method
described by Schlichting [6] and incorporated roughness to determine
transition [7] as well as the augmentation to heat transfer [8]. These
criteria were used in LEWICE [9] and LEWICE3D [10] to predict
transition and turbulent heat transfer with roughness effects. This
technique was extended for 3D boundary layers by Blanchard et.
al. [11]. Other researchers built roughness effects through turbulence
models such as that developed by Aupoix [12] and Menter [13].
Examples include the efforts of Beaugendre [14], Knopp [15],
Bernardini [16] Tagawa [17], and Min [18]. Neither the boundary
layer approach nor the turbulence model approach is always
applicable in GlennICE [19]. GlennICE is a computational tool that is
being developed by NASA to predict ice shapes on multi-dimensional
geometries of higher complexities based on a computational flow
solution that a user provides. GlennICE does not calculate the flow
solution, rather it reads in a solution from other sources. These flow
solvers may or may not have a turbulence model that includes
roughness effects on heat transfer. Heat transfer coefficient is a critical
parameter for predicting the ice shape and will increase as ice begins
to accrete and the surface becomes rough. A methodology is needed
to incorporate this effect other than through the use of turbulence
models. The current implementation within GlennICE incorporates a
roughness model that was designed by McClain [20] which
incorporates measurements of ice roughness distributions over a wide
variety of conditions and geometries to produce the correlation



provided. The roughness measurements have been reported in several
papers [21–27]. The final correlation is based on parameters that are
obtained from the flow field such as pressure coefficient (Cp),
specified values for the icing conditions such as liquid water content
(LWC), and the rate of mass impinging the surface. This
implementation also includes the introduction of two user-defined
parameters (UDPs): the ideal rime limit and turbulent heat transfer
augmentation. The ideal rime limit is a dimensional value in meters
and determines the point at which the growth of ice stops being
considered roughness and subsequently becomes an ice shape. The
turbulent heat transfer coefficient (htc) augmentation is a factor that
scales the heat transfer coefficient augmentation to the roughness
values provided by the correlation. This value had previously been set
within GlennICE to a default value of 3 based on the best prediction
of case 242 from the Ice Prediction Workshop [28].



Since the roughness augmentation model implemented within
GlennICE requires these two UDPs to be defined, it is desired that
suggested values be obtained that show the best agreement with
experimental ice shapes over a variety of pressure gradient profiles,
temperature differences, and freestream conditions. In order to
accomplish this, six different airfoils have been chosen that have
documented ice shapes and freestream conditions. Previous
experimental testing within the NASA Icing Research Tunnel (IRT)
provides a matrix of 95 different conditions to be analyzed [29]. The
airfoils being investigated are the NACA-0012, NACA-0015,
NACA-23012, NLF-0414, GLC-305, and LTHS geometries [30].



Methodology



This section is intended to describe the workflow of an initial
GlennICE run to aid the user in becoming familiar with the GlennICE
software. The complete workflow for the GlennICE software is
illustrated in Figure 1. The white boxes in the process are external to
GlennICE and accomplished with third party software. The green,
blue, red, and orange boxes denote work accomplished by the
GlennICE software.



This flow chart illustrates a multi-shot simulation strategy where the
user is responsible for remeshing the new iced geometry and
rerunning the Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) solver. This
multi-shot capability is optional, and currently only single shot
computations have been performed.



GlennICE uses a discretized flow field generated from a Computation
Fluid Dynamics (CFD) solver. To maintain flexibility with a wide
variety of commercial and in-house CFD solvers, the coupling
between GlennICE and the CFD software is currently all one-way

coupling. As such GlennICE can be classified as a CFD
post-processor. Currently this level of coupling has been sufficient for
external icing problems. Because of the one-way coupling, GlennICE
can theoretically accept solutions from a wide variety of CFD solvers.
It does this by supporting a handful of widely used CFD file formats
that a majority of CFD solvers utilize. However, it should be noted
that there are many storage variants. GlennICE requires pressure,
density, u, v, w-velocity components, and the three components of the
surface wall shear. It also requires the estimated wall distance to the
surface which was used by the turbulence model for the adaptive
refinement scheme published previously [31]. Finally, GlennICE
requires either the heat transfer coefficient or two solutions with the
wall heat flux and different wall temperatures from which it can
calculate the heat transfer coefficient. Currently GlennICE will read
solutions in Fieldview [32] unstructured format or Tecplot [33] ’szplt’
binary format.



GlennICE integrates particle trajectories using a method developed by
Dormand and Prince [34]. It is a 4th order Runge-Kutta scheme that is
designed to minimize error of the 5th order term. This means that it
computes both the 5th order result and the 4th order result and returns
the difference as the error. Particles are released from a user-supplied
bounding box and subsequently refined based on the distance of the
trajectory to the surface or based on whether it hits. Details of this
method were presented previously [31]. Due to the large number of
cases studied, the MVD drop size was used for all runs. A droplet
distribution was not expected to change the results for the straight
wing cases studied.



Heat transfer coefficients can be specified using either a single CFD
solution or two solutions. The single solution methodology assumes
the CFD solver provided a value of heat transfer coefficient.
Alternatively, the user can provide GlennICE with two CFD solutions
differing only by the wall thermal boundary condition. GlennICE uses
this information to calculate heat transfer coefficient and adiabatic
wall temperature. It is assumed that the heat transfer coefficient, htc,
and adiabatic wall temperature, Taw, are independent of wall thermal
condition. Therefore, at every location, given (T1, q1) and (T2, q2)
two simultaneous equations are solved for htc and Taw. This
technique relies on the definition of htc shown in Eq 1.



q = htc ∗ (Twall − Taw) (1)



The user can specify these two files if they consist of identical
meshes, however, the two CFD solutions differ in their specification
of the temperature of the surface. These options do not necessarily
account for the roughness effects, however. Many solvers contain a
turbulence model that accounts for sand-grain roughness such as those
proposed in references [14–18]. In lieu of this, the heat transfer
coefficient computed by this process was augmented using an
empirical roughness model that was developed by McClain [35] to
predict roughness variation on a surface. The equations programmed
into GlennICE are shown in Eqns. 2-4. These equations use two
user-defined values that can be adjusted. The first user-defined value
is the ideal rime limit (IRL), tr,max and the second empirical constant
is the augmentation constant, A. The McClain paper only provides
Eq. 2 and does not define tr,max or the heat transfer augmentation.
The best values for these parameters are the subject of ongoing
research. The values selected for this paper were chosen to provide
the best fit to the experimental data. Since this equation depends on
freezing fraction, which depends on the heat transfer coefficient

second iteration would then use the updated heat transfer coefficient
and the process would continue until values no longer change.

heat transfer coefficient. While this parameter introduces a third UDP

The mass and energy balance at the surface uses the same terms as

A fully three-dimensional ice growth was produced using a
prismatoid method with node-based smoothing. The process is based
on computing the node centered normal vectors and solving a cubic
equation at each location to find the appropriate distance from the
surface that maintains the computed ice volume. This surface
redefinition process was described in a previous paper [28] and will
not be replicated here.

Experimental Overview



Experimental data for the comparisons were taken from tests in the
Icing Research Tunnel over the course of several years [30, 36–45].
All of the geometries were straight wing (extruded airfoils) that went
from floor to ceiling in the IRT. The wing sections were mounted on a
turntable so that angle of attack could be varied. Aerodynamic and
meteorological conditions chosen in the test were based on the
objectives of the specific test. Ice shapes were documented by cutting
out a small section with a heated piece of metal, then inserting a
cardboard template into the slot. The shapes were traced with a pencil
and later digitized. The conditions chosen for this study were large
glaze ice shapes as these shapes are typically the most difficult to
predict. Conditions with large ice shapes was based on the
Accumulation Parameter as defined by Eq. 5:

Geometries



A 2-D representation of the previously mentioned airfoils is used and
a subsequent mesh for each airfoil is generated. The profiles of the six
different airfoils being investigated are shown in the Figure 2. Point
distributions for NACA airfoils were produced from the equations that
define those airfoils. Airfoil coordinates for the other three airfoils
(NLF-414, GLC305, LTHS) were read into the software Rhino [47]
where the profiles were smoothed before grids were generated.



Computational Domain



The domain is setup such that the boundary conditions induce a
subsonic pressure driven flow utilizing the experimental conditions
from the case matrix. A viscous wall boundary condition is imposed
on the airfoils, two symmetry planes are setup on the minimum and
maximum Y-locations, and freestream boundaries are located on the
minimum and maximum Z-locations. The inflow and outflow
boundaries along with the freestream boundaries are located +/- 10
chord lengths away from the origin which coincides with the leading
edge of the airfoils. A representation of the computational domain can
be seen in the Figure 3 where the domain boundaries are identified
with the exclusion of a single symmetry plane. Each mesh that was
generated is fully unstructured where the boundary layer is resolved
such that initial wall spacing is obtained from the case which required
the minimum wall spacing such that a value of y+ = 1 is obtained.
The mesh is then geometrically grown for a total of 40 times at a
defined growth rate of r = 1.2 utilizing Pointwise and the T-Rex
extrusion solver [48]. Each airfoil is then extruded along the spanwise
direction for a total of 4 times such that a ’three-dimensional’
geometry can be analyzed. This results in a subsequent mesh of
approximately 300K cells for each airfoil. An example grid is shown
in Figure 4. Since GlennICE acts as a post-processing tool, a volume

Computational Solver



The NASA production CFD code FUN3D was used in order to
generate the flow solutions and surface quantities necessary for ice
accretion as described previously [49]. For the roughness cases, the
HANIM CFL controller was utilized to limit the amount of
monitoring needing and to decrease the computational cost of a single
solution [50]. The cases were run compressibly while using the SA
turbulence model with a roe flux differencing scheme and a consistent
linearization scheme for the residual construction [51, 52]. No flux
limiters were used for any of the roughness cases. This process was
automated such that the matrix of conditions was read in, a single
FUN3D case was generated that adjusted the unique conditions from
the matrix for each case, and then submitted the job without the need
for any user input. The NACA0012 and GLC305 geometries had
cases with more than one chord length, all of the grids were generated
using a unit chord (non-dimensional) and the various chord sizes were
specified in the FUN3D input. Multiple grid resolutions and inviscid
wall boundaries were also generated for each geometry to ensure a
grid-independant solution. Since GlennICE requires two different
wall temperature ratios, a wall temperature ratio of 1 and 1.04 were
used such that the heat transfer coefficient can be solved for within
GlennICE. The wall temperature ratio is simply the ratio of wall
temperature to the freestream. This resulted in approximately 200
different cases being generated and run automatically with minimal
input by the user while simultaneously reducing the risk of an error
being introduced unknowingly within the case generation. The
convergence criteria for each case was kept constant and required the
RMS of the meanflow and turbulence residuals to be converged below
1E − 14 which typically corresponded to around a 10-order reduction
in the residuals. The Mach contours and surface pressures for a
sample case (AC1038436) from the selected test matrix are shown in
Figure 5 and Figure 6.
