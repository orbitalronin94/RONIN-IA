<p>Thermal Desktop Modeling of the 2016 CRYOTE-2 Tank Chilldown and Fill Testing 
</p>
<p>Erin Tesnya and Jason Hartwiga 
</p>
<p>aNASA Glenn Research Center, Fluids and Cryogenics Branch, Cleveland, OH, 44135 
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>Abstract 
</p>
<p>The storing and transfer of cryogenic propellants is an enabling technology for NASA 
</p>
<p>and industry as civilization moves to expand future missions into low Earth orbit and beyond. 
</p>
<p>Several ground propellant transfer tests have been conducted in past decades examining various 
</p>
<p>tank chilldown and fill methods. Creating accurate models using these historical datasets is a 
</p>
<p>vital step toward developing appropriate modeling tools to form pre-test predictions for 
</p>
<p>subsequent ground and flight propellant transfers. This paper presents model validation of 
</p>
<p>Thermal Desktop using data from the 2016 CRYOTE-2 liquid nitrogen transfer experiments 
</p>
<p>whose purpose was to demonstrate the chill and fill process in a spherical receiver tank. Tank 
</p>
<p>pressure, fill level, and wall temperature were all modeled and compared with 8 test cases using 
</p>
<p>two modeling methods, Mass Controlled, and Pressure Controlled. This test series is the hardest 
</p>
<p>Thermal Desktop tank chill and fill validation case to-date, yet the model was able to accurately 
</p>
<p>predict each of these metrics for the 2016 tests within 28%, 10%, 20% respectively, for pressure, 
</p>
<p>fill level, and wall temperature using the Mass Controlled model, and 29%, 79%, and 12%, 
</p>
<p>respectively for the Pressure Controlled model. 
</p>
<p>Keywords: no-vent fill, Thermal Desktop, chilldown, condensation, liquid nitrogen 
</p>
<p> 
</p>

<p>1.0 Introduction 
</p>
<p>The storing and transfer of cryogenic propellants is an enabling technology for NASA 
</p>
<p>and industry as civilization moves to expand future missions into Low Earth Orbit (LEO) and 
</p>
<p>beyond. The transfer of cryogenic propellants is of particular interest because it has never been 
</p>
<p>successfully demonstrated in microgravity to-date. An in-space propellant transfer is divided into 
</p>
<p>various stages, including (1) pressurization of the supply tank, (2) propellant management 
</p>
<p>devices to extract single-phase liquid, (3) transfer line chilldown, (4) receiver tank chilldown, 
</p>
<p>and (5) receiver tank fill. This paper focuses on modeling stages 4 and 5. 
</p>
<p>While somewhat inefficient, the cryogenic transfer process in 1-g is trivial; the receiver 
</p>
<p>tank is vented throughout the transfer, and the boil-off vapor produced during chilldown is 
</p>
<p>vented through the top of the tank while the liquid accumulates to the desired final fill level. In 
</p>
<p>microgravity, where the fluid is in an unsettled configuration however, propellant transfer is 
</p>
<p>complicated by the unknown location of the liquid/vapor interface, complex heat transfer 
</p>
<p>processes, and propensity to vent liquid. Due to the high cost of launching and storing propellant 
</p>
<p>in space, venting liquid is to be avoided at all times. 
</p>
<p>Cryogenic propellants such as liquid oxygen (LO2) are prone to boiling at low heat flux 
</p>
<p>(relative to water), causing pressure rise rates that can disrupt the transfer process. Furthermore, 
</p>
<p>complex two-phase flows can arise, which would complicate system modeling efforts, making 
</p>
<p>the transfer process more unpredictable. At the time of writing this paper, no reduced gravity 
</p>
<p>cryogenic chill and fill transfer experiments have been conducted. In lieu of experimentation in 
</p>
<p>the space environment, ground tests have been conducted that still capture many of the 
</p>
<p>operational issues and heat and mass transfer processes that arise during cryogenic propellant 
</p>
<p>transfer. As a result, several chilldown and fill methods have been conceived and tested over the </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>3 
 
</p>
<p>past several decades to address the difficulties of transferring cryogens in reduced gravity 
</p>
<p>environments. 
</p>
<p>The simplest method is to utilize a cryocooler heat exchanger to prechill the receiving 
</p>
<p>tank, followed by a continuous no-vent fill (NVF). Spaceflight cryocoolers with high efficiency 
</p>
<p>are still in development and require additional system mass, power, and complexity. Without 
</p>
<p>cryocoolers, the cooling capacity of the propellant must be used to chill down the transfer 
</p>
<p>hardware. However, this leads to loss of propellant; the liquid undergoes phase change and is 
</p>
<p>vented overboard. Thus, is it necessary to remove as much thermal energy as possible to 
</p>
<p>conserve propellant. A second candidate chill and fill method was first proposed by [1] and later 
</p>
<p>explored analytically by [2, 3] deemed the charge/hold/vent (CHV) method. Here, a “charge” of 
</p>
<p>liquid is introduced into the tank with the receiver tank vent valve closed. The liquid is then held 
</p>
<p>for some time to allow complete phase change. The fluid and wall reach thermal equilibrium at a 
</p>
<p>new lower temperature. Then the vapor is vented, and the process is repeated until the wall 
</p>
<p>temperature reaches the “target temperature” [4] that is cold enough to allow a NVF. The CHV 
</p>
<p>method alleviates the possibility of venting liquid but introduces complexity due to valve 
</p>
<p>cycling, extended transfer duration, and the need to potentially gauge mass in the tank. A third 
</p>
<p>proposed method is called the vented chill (VC)/NVF originally demonstrated in [5]. Here, the 
</p>
<p>receiver tank vent valve remains open until the receiver tank reaches the target temperature, 
</p>
<p>followed by a continuous NVF. The goal of VC/NVF is to expedite the transfer process through 
</p>
<p>less valve cycling, however, care must be taken to ensure the injection method maximizes the 
</p>
<p>evaporation rate between receiver tank ullage and incoming liquid as well as the boiling heat 
</p>
<p>transfer between liquid and wall to prevent any liquid from being vented. The VC/NVF method 
</p>
<p>is the focus of this paper. A fourth method that was recently devised at NASA Marshall Space </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>4 
 
</p>
<p>Flight Center (MSFC) was the thermodynamic vent system (TVS) assisted transfer. Here, the 
</p>
<p>propellant transfer is conducted with the main vent valve closed; the injector has an embedded 
</p>
<p>TVS heat exchanger where a small amount of liquid from the main injection line is routed 
</p>
<p>through a Joule-Thomson device, expanded, cooled, and integrated with the injector to assist in 
</p>
<p>subcooling the main injection line. Data analysis from [6] showed, however, that the benefit of 
</p>
<p>the TVS was in chilling down the injector itself and allowing a stalled transfer to recommence 
</p>
<p>due to a new condensation heat transfer path opening up between the cold injector and warm 
</p>
<p>ullage. This TVS-assisted NVF still requires further testing to prove to be a viable concept.  
</p>
<p>This paper presents Thermal Desktop (TD) model development and validation against the 
</p>
<p>CRYogenic Orbital Testbed Experiment-2 (CRYOTE-2) 2016 tests, a recent set of experiments 
</p>
<p>conducted at NASA MSFC. In total 53 tests were performed, using three different liquid 
</p>
<p>injection systems, over a wide range of thermodynamic conditions and flow rates. The focus of 
</p>
<p>this paper is on model validation against a single particular spray device for 8 validation cases. 
</p>
<p>The outline of the paper is as follows: First a background section is given to briefly discuss 
</p>
<p>previous chill and fill testing and modeling. Next, the description of the CRYOTE-2 system, 
</p>
<p>hardware, experiment, and instrumentation is presented. Then, a brief description of the Thermal 
</p>
<p>Desktop model is given, along with test-specific assumptions, boundary conditions, initial 
</p>
<p>conditions, and final conditions. Finally, Thermal Desktop model results are compared with test 
</p>
<p>data along with some discussion. 
</p>
<p>2.0 Background 
</p>
<p>1-g cryogenic transfer experiments have been conducted for decades. What follows is a 
</p>
<p>brief summary of those tests; for a detailed history of cryogenic chill and fill testing and 
</p>
<p>modeling, the reader is referred to [4]. Fester [7] performed NVF tests with Freon-113 on a </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>5 
 
</p>
<p>vertical cylinder using a bottom fill method. Chato et al. [8] and [9] then used a top spray nozzle 
</p>
<p>injector on a cylindrical tank using liquid nitrogen (LN2) and LH2. [10] subsequently conducted 
</p>
<p>NVF tests on the same tank using LH2, but tested the performance of a top spray nozzle, upward 
</p>
<p>pipe discharger, and bottom diffuser. The last was used to simulate low-g cases where the 
</p>
<p>injector would be submerged. Chato [11] conducted NVFs on an aluminum sphere using LH2 
</p>
<p>with a top spray nozzle and bottom jet. Chato and Sanabria [1] conducted CHV tests on the same 
</p>
<p>tank. Moran and Nyland [12] investigated the performance of a spray bar using LN2 on a vertical 
</p>
<p>cylinder. Chato [13] tested a spray bar using LH2. Wang and Wang [14], Wang et al. [15], and 
</p>
<p>Kim et al. [16] conducted parametric NVF tests using multiple injectors. Flachbart et al. [17] 
</p>
<p>conducted NVF tests on the thick-walled Multi-purpose Hydrogen Testbed using LH2. Hartwig 
</p>
<p>et al. [4] reported results of the CRYOTE-2 test program while [5] and [6] reported test results of 
</p>
<p>the TVS-assisted injector test data analysis and the original rapid chill and fill tests, respectively. 
</p>
<p>Analysis performed in the current work falls under the Reduced Gravity Cryogenic 
</p>
<p>Transfer (RGCT) project at NASA whose goals are (1) to investigate efficient cryogenic 
</p>
<p>chilldown and transfer methods in a reduced gravity environment to enable future flight transfer 
</p>
<p>systems, and (2) to develop and validate improved empirical, analytical, lumped capacitance, and 
</p>
<p>computational fluid dynamics models for propellant transfer operations in terrestrial and reduced 
</p>
<p>gravity environments. RGCT is producing direct-cryogenic data anchored propellant transfer 
</p>
<p>models at multiple levels, including fundamental cryogenic boiling heat transfer and pressure 
</p>
<p>drop correlations [18-20], a first-principles analytical model that predicts the failure of a NVF 
</p>
<p>with 100% accuracy [21], various design tools for cryogenic tank chilldown and fill injection 
</p>
<p>methods, including (TVS)-assisted injectors, development and validation of lumped capacitance 
</p>
<p>codes Thermal Desktop and the Generalized Fluid System Simulation Program (GFSSP) for tank </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>6 
 
</p>
<p>chilldown and tank fill [22-24], CFD of tank chilldown using FLUENT [25]and CFD of the fluid 
</p>
<p>dynamics inside a propellant tank subject to sloshing and non-inertial forces in reduced gravity 
</p>
<p>[26]. Analysis in the current work is focused on validation of Thermal Desktop for the 
</p>
<p>CRYOTE-2 tank chilldown and fill experiments. This test series represents the hardest validation 
</p>
<p>case performed to-date. Relative to previous Thermal Desktop validation exercises against larger 
</p>
<p>tanks with smaller temperature differences and longer transfer time scales, the chilldown and fill 
</p>
<p>process for CRYOTE-2 transfers is relatively faster here, the injectors are the most complicated, 
</p>
<p>and the temperature gradients between wall and fluid are the highest studied.  
</p>
<p>3.0 Experimental Description 
</p>
<p>As stated in [4], “CRYOTE was first envisioned by the United Launch Alliance and 
</p>
<p>initial concepts were first reviewed in 2008 [27]. CRYOTE was designed to test a completely 
</p>
<p>integrated cryogenic test bed for testing fuel storage and transfer in as close to a flight 
</p>
<p>environment as possible. Subsystems of CRYOTE include multi-layer insulation (MLI) to reduce 
</p>
<p>parasitic heat leak, a support skirt, a TVS for managing pressure in the tank through 
</p>
<p>cooling/venting the fuel, a vapor cooled heat exchanger running through the skirt for maximizing 
</p>
<p>the usefulness of the TVS, and a liquid level sensing system.” This paper specifically focuses on 
</p>
<p>the chill and fill tests that occurred in 2016, where the emphasis was on obtaining data for 
</p>
<p>simple, higher performing injectors, as well as examining the effect of different thermodynamic 
</p>
<p>parameters. 
</p>
<p>3.1 Hardware Description 
</p>
<p>The CRYOTE test series was conducted at NASA MSFC in the Exploration Systems 
</p>
<p>Test Facility (ESTF). ESTF is a 6.10 m long, 2.74 m diameter multi-purpose vacuum chamber 
</p>
<p>that has been used for numerous cryogenic fluid management experiments at the Marshall Space </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>7 
 
</p>
<p>Flight Center. The test set up consisted of a supply tank, receiver tank, transfer line, vent line, 
</p>
<p>and various instrumentation inside a vacuum chamber shown in Figure 1. The Vibro-Acoustic 
</p>
<p>Test Article (VATA) is the supply tank that feeds the CRYOTE receiver tank inside of the 
</p>
<p>ESTF. During the CRYOTE tests, VATA is filled with LN2 initially at 77K and 1 atm and then 
</p>
<p>pressurized to the desired supply tank pressure utilizing gaseous nitrogen (GN2).  
</p>
<p>

</p>

</p>
<p>wall thickness, and an MAWP of 5.7 MPa (827) psi at cryogenic temperatures. The volume of

</p>

</p>
<p>masses do not include the small masses of the injectors and connectors into the lid. An MLI skirt 
</p>

<p>3.2 Instrumentation 
</p>
<p>The receiver tank, MLI skirt, and feed lines were fitted with various thermocouples and 
</p>
<p>to measure the temperature inside and outside the tank as shown in Figure 2 and outlined in 
</p>
<p>Table 1. Of particular interest are thermocouples TC73-TC96 which were placed on the outside 
</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

</p>

<p>5.0 Description of the Model 
</p>
<p>Thermal Desktop is a multi-node, solid-fluid solver modeling tool that can provide 
</p>
<p>relatively high-fidelity solutions without the high computational cost of higher order models such </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>14 
 
</p>
<p>as CFD. CAD models can be imported and meshed to create a network of finite elements to 
</p>
<p>represent solid geometry [32]. TD interfaces with SpaceClaim where the CAD geometry 
</p>
<p>designed in another program can be simplified and then meshed when imported into TD’s 
</p>
<p>AutoCAD interface. The user has a significant amount of control over the mesh creation 
</p>
<p>(maximum number of nodes, allowable curvature, allowable scaling) both globally and at the 
</p>
<p>local level for individual surfaces or solids.  For the CRYOTE-2 Receiver Tank, the default 
</p>
<p>values were used, because this is a relatively simple spherical geometry without small features or 
</p>
<p>sharp edges. The resulting mesh contained 530 solid nodes.  
</p>
<p>SINDA/FLUINT is the thermal/fluid solution engine used within Thermal Desktop. 
</p>
<p>SINDA solves the conduction and thermal capacitance of the thermal network in the model in 
</p>
<p>addition to outside heat loads or radiation [32]. Within SINDA, the network consists of nodes 
</p>
<p>and conductors. Energy is stored in the nodes and transferred via the conductors between nodes 
</p>
<p>to create the network. FLUINT solves the thermohydraulic network in the model, both fluid flow 
</p>
<p>through a network or accumulation in a tank [32]. The two solvers work simultaneous to 
</p>
<p>converge on a solution. Within FLUINT, the fluid network consists of fluid lumps and fluid 
</p>
<p>paths. In this model, two types of fluid lumps were used: tanks and plena. A tank is a lump 
</p>
<p>representing a finite control volume governed by differential equations of mass conversation and 
</p>
<p>energy conservation. In contrast a plenum is a lump with an infinite volume and represents a 
</p>
<p>boundary condition, in this case the inlet flow conditions into the receiver tank. Fluid paths are 
</p>
<p>governed by differential equations for momentum conservation and connect two fluid lumps. 
</p>
<p>Heat transfer between the fluid lumps and solid nodes is governed by a tie.  
</p>
<p>Only the CRYOTE-2 receiver tank was constructed in this Thermal Desktop model. The 
</p>
<p>tank wall and lid were modeled using finite elements with the material properties of stainless </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>15 
 
</p>
<p>steel (SS304) and Ti applied. The fluid inside the tank is represented using twin lumps, with a 
</p>
<p>single lump representing the vapor and liquid phases that are at the same pressure but different 
</p>
<p>temperatures. The quality of each lump is also tracked independently, meaning that the “vapor” 
</p>
<p>and “liquid” lump can either contain subcooled liquid, saturated liquid, two-phase fluid, 
</p>
<p>saturated vapor, or superheated vapor. Three different paths represent the inlet flow into the tank, 
</p>
<p>each representing one of the three nozzle injectors. 
</p>
<p>5.1 Compartments and Bays 
</p>
<p>Previous work on modeling tank chilldown has focused on a simple dip tube or bottom-
</p>
<p>fill injector. A compartment is used to represent the location of the internal volume that is 
</p>
<p>occupied by the fluid. The volume of the compartment does not change with time and is 
</p>
<p>occupied by twin lumps so that the volume of the two lumps combined at any point in time is 
</p>
<p>always equal to the internal volume of the tank. The compartment will also track the location of 
</p>
<p>the liquid-vapor interface as the tank drains or fills over time.  In these models, a single inlet 
</p>
<p>flow path is directed to the bottom of the tank to represent a bottom-fill scenario. As liquid 
</p>
<p>begins to accumulate in the bottom of the tank during a fill, the flow is directed into the liquid 
</p>
<p>lump first and only enters the vapor lump through evaporation across the liquid-vapor interface. 
</p>
<p>All mass transfer across the interface is tracked by a separate flow path in TD.   
</p>
<p>Due to the different injection patterns of the 2016 injector from Figure 3, the tank wall 
</p>
<p>was sprayed unevenly and therefore chilled down at the different rates. This resulted in variation 
</p>
<p>in wall temperature at different tank wall heights at the start of fill. For example, as shown in 
</p>
<p>Figure 4 for Test 20160921, most of the flow was directed at the level of TC92 and TC93, and 
</p>
<p>these regions are significantly colder than the surrounding areas at the start of NVF. To represent 
</p>
<p>these different cooling rates, the compartment in the tank was split into various bays based on the </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>16 
 
</p>
<p>height of the respective thermocouple. With these different sections, the inlet flow path can be 
</p>
<p>split between the different tank heights to represent where more fluid is directly in contact with 
</p>
<p>the wall. This effectively separated the tank horizontally into different bays with fluid being able 
</p>
<p>to freely pass between them without a separate interface. The distribution of the flow for the 
</p>
<p>three different injector types is shown in Table 4.

<p>5.2 Inlet Flow Rate Control 
</p>
<p>Because of the lack of inlet flow rate data, two different methods were used to model 
</p>
<p>each test case. The first, hereafter referred to as “Mass Controlled,” fed the flow rate data backed 
</p>
<p>out from the load cells directly into the model as an input. This was the first method used but 
</p>
<p>resulted in overprediction of the initial pressure spike inside the receiver tank likely attributed to 
</p>
<p>a possible overprediction of wall boiling or an underprediction of condensation. In order to 
</p>
<p>attempt to combat this issue, the second method was developed. This second method, hereafter </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>17

<p>referred to as “Pressure Controlled,” used the pressure difference between the inlet pressure and 
</p>
<p>tank pressure with the known coefficient of discharge to determine a flow rate using Equation 1. 
</p>
<p>Both of these methods result in different flow rates and therefore different pressure curves and 
</p>
<p>fill levels. Results for these two methods will be discussed subsequently.

<p>5.3 Wall Temperatures 
</p>
<p>The original test dataset used a mass-averaged temperature reading to represent the 
</p>
<p>overall wall outside wall temperature. However, the Thermal Desktop model calculates average 
</p>
<p>wall temperature by using the arithmetic average of all finite element nodes in the model, 
</p>
<p>meaning all nodes were summed (weighted equally) and divided by the total number of nodes to 
</p>
<p>get the average. Because all nodes are weighted equally, this arithmetic average gives a different 
</p>
<p>result than the mass-averaged temperature from the data. Even though there is generally good 
</p>
<p>agreement between these two metrics, the difference in methodology may explain some of the 
</p>
<p>error.

<p>6.3 Consolidated Results and General Discussion 
</p>
<p> To quantify error between data and model, a mean absolute error (MAE) value is 
</p>
<p>computed for pressure, fill level, and wall temperature data point time step and summed using 
</p>
<p>Equation 9: 
</p>

</p>

</p>
<p>To convert to a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), the MAE value was multiplied by 100. 
</p>
<p>MAPE results are shown in Table 5. Overall, pressure is predicted within 29% for all cases and 
</p>
<p>average wall temperature within 20% of the data for all 8 cases. Error is as high as 79% for fill 
</p>
<p>level.  
</p>
<p>There were several very apparent differences between the Mass and Pressure Controlled 
</p>
<p>results. The fill level from the model always matched within 5% from the data for the Mass 
</p>
<p>Controlled method for all cases. This agreement is expected because the load cell data is fed 
</p>
<p>directly into the model as an input. Any discrepancy between the two numbers is likely due to 
</p>
<p>slightly different physics e.g., more or less evaporation in the tank resulting in a lower or higher 
</p>
<p>mass. However, for the Pressure Controlled cases there were significantly higher MAPEs for the 
</p>
<p>fill level. This large discrepancy is due to the difference in inlet flow rate between the two 
</p>
<p>methods. However, for several cases the final fill level, and therefore the total mass injected into 
</p>
<p>the tank, was very similar to the Mass Controlled result.  
</p>
<p>Conversely, the opposite trend holds for comparing the pressure rise in the tank between 
</p>
<p>the two modeling methods although the trend is not as significant as the difference in fill levels. 
</p>
<p>The Mass Controlled method often resulted in lower MAPEs for the pressure than the Pressure 
</p>
<p>Controlled method. Again, this difference can be attributed to the different flow rates. The </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>26 
 
</p>
<p>Pressure Controlled method is directly tied to the reported pressure in the receiver tank, and 
</p>
<p>therefore gives better agreement with the test data.

<p>7.0 Conclusion 
</p>
<p> This paper presented validation of a Thermal Desktop model against the 2016 CRYOTE-
</p>
<p>2 liquid nitrogen tank transfer experiments. This test series represents the most difficult model 
</p>
<p>validation case performed for Thermal Desktop to-date, due to the rapid transients and high 
</p>
<p>delta-T flow physics and heat transfer. Two modeling schemes were presented, one that 
</p>
<p>controlled the mass flow rate as the boundary condition, and one that relied on inlet pressure as </p>
<p></p>
</div>
<div class="page"><p></p>
<p>28 
 
</p>
<p>the boundary condition. Both schemes do a reasonable job at predicting pressure within 29% and 
</p>
<p>wall temperature within 21% of the data across the 8 test cases examined. the largest disparity 
</p>
<p>between the two methods is in predicting fill level, which is off by as much as 79% of the da. 
</p>
<p>Nonetheless, Thermal Desktop does a very reasonable job at predicting performance for this 
</p>
<p>difficult validation case and such high initial wall temperature NVFs and will continue to be a 
</p>
<p>useful tool for predicting various cryogenic fluid management phenomena. 
</p>
