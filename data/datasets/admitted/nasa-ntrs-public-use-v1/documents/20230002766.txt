<p>An Experimental Study on Low Pressure Frost Formation 
</p>
<p>for Lunar Polar Water Capture 
</p>
<p>Beau M. Compton1, Timothy S. Krause2, and Leah M. Struchen Deans3 
</p>
<p>NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, 44135 
</p>
<p>In-situ resource utilization (ISRU) is a vital component of NASA’s mission to the Moon 
</p>
<p>and beyond, as the extraction of resources from the environment can reduce payload weight 
</p>
<p>and the frequency of resupply missions. Since the discovery of water in the regolith of the 
</p>
<p>permanently shadowed regions (PSR) of the Moon, its extraction and transport has become 
</p>
<p>an area of increasing interest for NASA. One proposed method is heating icy regolith to free 
</p>
<p>the water before desublimating and transporting it as ice in a tanker. However, little is known 
</p>
<p>regarding the dynamics of frost growth at low pressures, and an understanding of the heat 
</p>
<p>transfer process is required to properly size the heat exchanger for the tanker. To investigate 
</p>
<p>this phenomenon, a cold plate was placed in a vacuum chamber with water vapor directed at 
</p>
<p>its surface. The chamber pressure (300 and 500 Pa) and cold plate temperature (–18 to –5 °C) 
</p>
<p>were kept below the triple point and varied throughout the experiment to examine their 
</p>
<p>impact on frost layer heat transfer. As water flowed into the chamber and deposited on the 
</p>
<p>cold plate surface, the heat flux and temperature were measured along with the frost layer 
</p>
<p>thickness and/or mass. Density calculated at the conclusion of each test suggests the frost layer 
</p>
<p>is denser than that of frost grown at atmospheric pressure. The results demonstrate 
</p>
<p>unexpected density and heat transfer characteristics and require development of a new model 
</p>
<p>of frost growth for lunar conditions. 
</p>

<p>I. Introduction 
</p>
<p>HE concept of prospecting, extracting, processing, and exploiting local resources off-Earth is known as in-situ 
</p>
<p>resource utilization (ISRU). NASA and private industry are interested in using ice from lunar regolith at the 
</p>
<p>Moon’s permanently shadowed regions (PSR) to produce water for hydrogen-oxygen rocket propellant and life 
</p>
<p>support services. Given the potential for 5% water by mass in the regolith,1 a study by NASA Glenn Research Center 
</p>
<p>(GRC) developed a concept by which this ice can be excavated, heated to vapor, stored in a tanker as ice, and brought 
</p>
<p>to sun-lit regions of the Moon for electrolysis.2 This concept is shown in Fig. 1.  
</p>
<p> 
</p>
<p>As envisioned, the ice tanker would receive water vapor from the processing 
</p>
<p>system and deposit it as frost, taking advantage of the cold surrounding 
</p>
<p>environment.2 The term “frost” denotes high-porosity (low-density) ice. The 
</p>
<p>tanker’s finned inner surfaces would be coated with frost by an insulated 
</p>
<p>distribution pipe. As a frost layer grows, however, so does its insulating effect, 
</p>
<p>meaning the reduced heat flux must be characterized; there will be a point 
</p>
<p>where the heat flux is low enough that the vapor no longer desublimes at the 
</p>
<p>given flow rate (i.e. there is a critical heat flux that determines the tanker’s 
</p>
<p>capacity). The behavior of frost growth in these conditions will control the 
</p>
<p>internal surface area needed to ensure total desublimation.  
</p>
<p> Water vapor undergoes a phase change to ice via two modes: 1) above the 
</p>
<p>triple point, condensation followed by freezing, and 2) below the triple point, 
</p>
<p>desublimation (gas to solid). While both conditions require humid air to be 
</p>
<p>cooled below the dew point, a local condition below the triple point leads to 
</p>
<p>local desublimation (moving from right to left across Fig. 2). The vapor 
</p>
<p>becomes super-saturated above the cold surface where the phase change 
</p>
<p>occurs. The water partial pressure becomes lower than that of the ambient air, 
</p>
<p>creating a difference in concentration that drives frost formation.3 Surface 
</p>
<p>roughness, surface impurities, air velocity, and humidity levels also affect 
</p>
<p>frost nucleation and growth.4  
</p>
<p> The thermophysical properties of water during desublimation are not fully 
</p>
<p>understood. Most studies do not evaluate frost growth under near-vacuum 
</p>
<p>conditions and do not grow frost beyond a centimeter.5-8 The majority of frost

</p>
<p>indicates a significant difference in frost growth dynamics5,12. Reference 5 demonstrates that frost grows more slowly 
</p>
<p>at 10 kPa than at atmospheric pressure (~100 kPa) due to a smaller phase transition driving force. At 10 kPa, the lower 
</p>
<p>partial pressure creates a smaller vapor concentration gradient that transfers less vapor to the frost layer. The frost 
</p>
<p>crystals transform morphology under different pressures (i.e. dendritic to columnar), and the bulk frost layer density 
</p>
<p>therefore is less when formed under lower pressure. These effects alone would have important implications for the 
</p>
<p>thermal conductivity and heat transfer through the frost layer. There are likely other effects as well that were not 
</p>
<p>studied. Reference 5 operated above the triple point, though, meaning that condensation was part of the phase 
</p>
<p>transition path, which implies very different results from the proposed desublimation path of frost growth in near 
</p>
<p>vacuum.

<p>II. Experimental Tests 
</p>
<p>To understand the fundamental mechanisms of the heat transfer involved in ice tanker loading, an aluminum, flat, 
</p>
<p>horizontal plate was used to grow ice in one dimension in vacuum. It simulates an interior wall or fin of the conceptual 
</p>
<p>water tanker. The plate was chilled to the ice tanker’s radiator operating temperatures described in Ref. 2, and water 
</p>
<p>vapor was introduced into the vacuum chamber at a controlled pressure. The frost layer height, δf, and mass, mf, were 
</p>
<p>measured throughout the test durations, which lasted as long as 300 hours. The bulk frost layer density, ρf, was 
</p>
<p>calculated from mass and volume measurements of frost samples.  
</p>
<p>A NASA GRC architecture study had previously estimated that to desublime 54 kg of water, a heat flux, q, of 
</p>
<p>approximately 116 W/m2 would be required to capture a continuous stream of vapor at 0.22 kg/hr from the pilot plant 
</p>
<p>over 10.8 days at the given radiator operating temperature.2 Creating ice at pressures that are below but near the triple 
</p>
<p>point (300 to 500 Pa) requires a smaller radiator than at lower pressures due to the higher desublimation temperatures. 
</p>
<p>To maintain the expected 10.8-day duration for the smaller scale tests, a scaled-down flow rate was calculated for an 
</p>
<p>energy balance at the selected test conditions. In a closed system such as the vacuum chamber or the ice tanker, either 
</p>
<p>pressure or flow rate can be controlled, but not both, so one must be prioritized in the design of experiment. Because 
</p>
<p>the accuracy of flow controller technology is insufficient for the scaled-down flow rates, pressure of the incoming 
</p>
<p>water vapor stream was selected as the controlling variable. The water vapor pressures, and therefore the vacuum 
</p>
<p>chamber pressures, were selected to match the pressures expected in the ice tanker. The mass change in the frost layer 
</p>
<p>was assumed to approximate the flow rate of vapor after the pressure setpoint is reached. Each test was concluded 
</p>
<p>when the water could no longer freeze at the desired flow rate due to insulating effects, or the frost ran out of space to 
</p>
<p>continue growing.  
</p>

<p>B. Experimental hardware 
</p>
<p>The set up for the experiment is 
</p>
<p>diagrammed in Fig. 4. Water vapor was 
</p>
<p>introduced by low pressure boil-off of a liquid 
</p>
<p>water reservoir. The flow rate was driven by 
</p>
<p>the vapor pressure. The main chamber 
</p>
<p>roughing pump was routed to this reservoir 
</p>
<p>through a series of valves to initially pump it 
</p>
<p>below 22 torr (2900 Pa), and it was heated 
</p>
<p>with a heat lamp to prevent the water from 
</p>
<p>freezing due to the energy loss of 
</p>
<p>vaporization. A cast acrylic insulation box 
</p>
<p>was placed around the cold plate and several 
</p>
<p>layers of Kapton tape were wrapped around 
</p>
<p>the flex lines to reduce unwanted frost growth 
</p>
<p>in unmonitored locations.  
</p>
<p>The vapor introduction system is 
</p>
<p>diagrammed in Fig. 5. Deionized water was 
</p>
<p>manually poured into the supply reservoir 
</p>
<p>prior to pumpdown and was periodically 
</p>
<p>refilled when the water level was low. An 
</p>
<p>upstream pressure control valve (Alicat PC3 series) regulated pressure via a feedback loop such that water was 
</p>
<p>introduced to the main chamber up to 300 or 500 Pa (depending on the desired test conditions). When water entered 
</p>
<p>the chamber and subsequently froze, the pressure decreased, effectively acting as a cryopump that caused the pressure 
</p>
<p>control valve to open again and allow vapor to enter.  
</p>
<p>The plate was cooled with polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) silicone fluid flowing through an Advance Thermal 
</p>
<p>Solutions cold plate to simulate radiator operating conditions. The chiller used a type T thermocouple from the growth 
</p>
<p>plate as an external feedback source. The cold plate had 
</p>
<p>four tube passes and was connected to chamber 
</p>
<p>feedthroughs via flex hoses which then connected to the 
</p>
<p>recirculated chiller.  
</p>
<p>The aluminum 6061 test plate used for the experiment 
</p>
<p>is a 100 cm2 square plate with 0.95 cm thickness. Several 
</p>
<p>type T thermocouples, shown in Fig. 6, were drilled and 
</p>
<p>spring loaded in the plate to measure wall temperature, Tf, 
</p>
<p>and track any thermal gradients. Attached to the bottom of 
</p>
<p>the plate was an Omega UHFS-09 heat flux sensor with a 
</p>
<p>52 cm2 sensing area and a 1500 mV/W/cm2 sensitivity. A 
</p>
<p>high precision Interface MB-5 beam load cell was placed 
</p>
<p>below the cold plate to measure the changing mass of the 
</p>
<p>frost layer, although this load cell failed during the first test 
</p>
<p>and was not replaced. The accuracy of experimental 
</p>
<p>equipment and uncertainty of δf and ρf (calculated by

<p>Measuring the height of ice growth required 
</p>
<p>a high precision ruler placed behind the cold plate 
</p>
<p>and a Logitech BRIO 4K webcam aligned

</p>

</p>

</p>

<p>III. Results &amp; Discussion 
</p>
<p>A. Characteristics of low-pressure frost: Density 
</p>
<p>Unexpected phenomena were observed in this low-pressure, long-
</p>
<p>duration frost growth test campaign. First, the ice density, ρf, was very 
</p>
<p>high, greater than that of ice formed at equivalent temperature but 
</p>
<p>ambient pressure. Density was calculated by chipping three to four 
</p>
<p>samples of ice off the cold plate at completion of a test for six of the seven 
</p>
<p>tests, when the vacuum was released, and measuring mass and volume 
</p>
<p>displacement. Density was averaged among all sample points and found 
</p>
<p>to be 953 ± 22 kg/m3. At ambient pressure and -10°C, ice is expected to 
</p>
<p>be 919 kg/m3. While the samples were not necessarily removed from the 
</p>
<p>middle of the cold plate nor the same location within the chamber, the 
</p>
<p>low variation in the calculated value (standard deviation = 26 kg/m3) 
</p>
<p>suggests this dense ice grew everywhere.  
</p>
<p> There appeared to be little porosity in the frost layer. Figure 8, which 
</p>
<p>compares frost growth at the same plate and operating temperatures, 
</p>
<p>shows a different transparency and growth pattern between atmospheric 
</p>
<p>and low-pressure (300 Pa) conditions.  Though ρf was calculated only at 
</p>
<p>the end of the experiment, the frost appears consistently translucent 
</p>
<p>(more like ice) through the desublimation process, as shown in Fig. 7, 
</p>
<p>suggesting it was dense from beginning of testing and that it did not 
</p>
<p>collapse or densify over time. This unexpectedly high density had drastic 
</p>
<p>consequences on the frost layer height as the expected temperature-
</p>
<p>insulating effects were reduced; insulative capacity increases with 
</p>
<p>porosity.  
</p>

<p>B. Characteristics of low-pressure frost: Mass and height 
</p>
<p>To investigate the behavior of the frost layer according to 
</p>
<p>the operating conditions described in Table 2, frost mass and 
</p>

<p>D. Experiment discrepancies and anomalies 
</p>
<p>Unexpectedly productive frost/ice growth was a noticeable aspect of the experiments. Frost grew in the interstices 
</p>
<p>of the acrylic insulation box and the cold plate and affected the mass data. Despite Kapton insulation on the flex lines, 
</p>
<p>a thick layer of frost grew throughout, though with low impact on the mass measurements due to their distances from 
</p>
<p>the load cell and the spring-like nature of the lines. Both locations of exuberant frost growth impacted the flow rate 
</p>
<p>estimations, meaning flow rate was under-estimated. The exact partial pressure of water vapor in the chamber was not 
</p>
<p>monitored. Therefore, there is some degree of error of the sublimation temperature. To mitigate the impacts of this, 
</p>
<p>the chamber was pumped down to approximately 100 mTorr (13.3 Pa) before water was introduced, limited by the 
</p>
<p>capacity of the roughing pump. Assuming there is no significant leak in the chamber, it is reasonable to assume the 
</p>
<p>water vapor partial pressure adequately represented the overall chamber pressure. 
</p>
<p>At the beginning of the test, the rate of water vapor entering the chamber outpaced the rate of freezing on the test 
</p>
<p>plate (and the regions of parasitic growth), shown in the increasing pressure data (Fig. 11), therefore causing some 
</p>
<p>difference from the theoretical growth at a constant mass flow rate. The initial lower pressure of the chamber may 
</p>
<p>have altered the frost structure or other key aspects early in the test and impacted later results. 
</p>
<p>Due to the high density of the frost layer, mass was larger than expected and exceeded the capacity of the load cell 
</p>
<p>and damaged it. Therefore, most tests recorded no corresponding mass data. 
</p>
<p>Height growth was measured using a stationary webcam with a fisheye lens, which introduces parallax and 
</p>
<p>distortion when processing the images of ice. Because the view angle constantly changed as the surface of the ice 
</p>
<p>grew by 1 to 8 cm, error is introduced in the image processing measurement. For this reason, a separate scaling factor 
</p>
<p>was calculated in both the x and the y directions. This additional uncertainty, however, is much lower than the value 
</p>
<p>reported in Table 1.   
</p>
<p>There was a slight leak in the chamber which allowed lab air to enter the chamber. This means that nitrogen, 
</p>
<p>oxygen and other contaminants entered and created some uncertainty of the partial pressure of water vapor. This effect 
</p>
<p>is notable, but ultimately has a low expected effect on final results 
</p>

<p>IV. Conclusion 
</p>
<p>Water vapor forms denser, clearer, lower-porosity ice than expected under low-pressure (300 – 500 Pa) and low-
</p>
<p>temperature (-18 to -3°C) conditions over industrially long periods (50 – 300 hr). This will significantly impact the 
</p>
<p>design of ISRU technology for use on the Moon, especially if these results extend to more complex geometries and a 
</p>
<p>wider range of conditions.  
</p>
<p>The temperature difference between the cold plate (standing in for the water tanker internal wall) and the pressure- 
</p>
<p>and temperature-controlled sublimation temperature affected the ice growth height more strongly than did the chamber 
</p>
<p>pressure. The wider the temperature difference, the higher the ice formation rate. Regardless of temperature and 
</p>
<p>pressure values, the tests showed a similar critical heat flux through the ice and cold plate/tanker wall, below which 
</p>
<p>conditions are not conducive to water ice collection from vapor.  
</p>
<p>Various lessons can be drawn from this ongoing research.  The test facility, experimental hardware, and unexpected 
</p>
<p>results made it difficult to directly measure the amount of frost. Mass flow of the water was too small to measure with 
</p>
<p>available sensors and difficult to infer because of parasitic ice growth on the hardware. Height was calculated from 
</p>
<p>images with low pixel resolution throughout the region of interest. The unexpected amount of frost was greater than 
</p>
<p>the load cell could handle, so a consistent mass measurement was not possible with the available sensors. When 
</p>
<p>planning future test campaigns, ample programmatic resources should be allotted for sensor iterations.  The facility 
</p>
<p>could not provide cold enough temperatures to desublime water vapor at more-representative lunar atmospheric 
</p>
<p>pressures. Future tests should be conducted at such pressures to increase understanding of frost properties, including 
</p>
<p>the density trend, at true lunar surface conditions where the ice tanker and other ISRU hardware would operate.  
</p>
<p>A first principles numerical model with temporal resolution is under development. The unexpectedly high density 
</p>
<p>and resulting higher heat flux have not been captured adequately in previously published models.9- 11,13 The model will 
</p>
<p>be validated using this experimental data and will be described in an upcoming paper. 
</p>
<p>Future work is needed to fully understand and quantify the evolving heat transfer through an ice layer at low 
</p>
<p>pressures and temperatures. Understanding the morphology of the crystalline frost structure is of primary interest due 
</p>
<p>to its effect on density and bulk thermal conductivity. Investigation of the material structure of the ice, such as whether 
</p>
<p>it is cubic structure as opposed to the standard hexagonal lattice structure in atmospheric conditions, is necessary to 
</p>
<p>determine any changes in thermal conductivity or other thermophysical aspects of the frost structure.14 More complex 
</p>
<p>growth plate geometries are needed to validate the model and design a full-scale lunar ice tanker as a cylindrical 
</p>
<p>geometry may have a more complex frost growth due to gravity and dynamic surface area effects. While out of scope 
</p>
<p>for this test effort, some other studies to be investigated are the impacts of dust particulate in water vapor and the 
</p>
<p>presence of non-desublimating species in the water vapor stream. 
</p>
