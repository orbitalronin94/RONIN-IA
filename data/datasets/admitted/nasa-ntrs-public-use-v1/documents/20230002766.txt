An Experimental Study on Low Pressure Frost Formation 



for Lunar Polar Water Capture 



Beau M. Compton1, Timothy S. Krause2, and Leah M. Struchen Deans3 



NASA Glenn Research Center, Cleveland, OH, 44135 



In-situ resource utilization (ISRU) is a vital component of NASA’s mission to the Moon 



and beyond, as the extraction of resources from the environment can reduce payload weight 



and the frequency of resupply missions. Since the discovery of water in the regolith of the 



permanently shadowed regions (PSR) of the Moon, its extraction and transport has become 



an area of increasing interest for NASA. One proposed method is heating icy regolith to free 



the water before desublimating and transporting it as ice in a tanker. However, little is known 



regarding the dynamics of frost growth at low pressures, and an understanding of the heat 



transfer process is required to properly size the heat exchanger for the tanker. To investigate 



this phenomenon, a cold plate was placed in a vacuum chamber with water vapor directed at 



its surface. The chamber pressure (300 and 500 Pa) and cold plate temperature (–18 to –5 °C) 



were kept below the triple point and varied throughout the experiment to examine their 



impact on frost layer heat transfer. As water flowed into the chamber and deposited on the 



cold plate surface, the heat flux and temperature were measured along with the frost layer 



thickness and/or mass. Density calculated at the conclusion of each test suggests the frost layer 



is denser than that of frost grown at atmospheric pressure. The results demonstrate 



unexpected density and heat transfer characteristics and require development of a new model 



of frost growth for lunar conditions.

I. Introduction 



HE concept of prospecting, extracting, processing, and exploiting local resources off-Earth is known as in-situ 



resource utilization (ISRU). NASA and private industry are interested in using ice from lunar regolith at the 



Moon’s permanently shadowed regions (PSR) to produce water for hydrogen-oxygen rocket propellant and life 



support services. Given the potential for 5% water by mass in the regolith,1 a study by NASA Glenn Research Center 



(GRC) developed a concept by which this ice can be excavated, heated to vapor, stored in a tanker as ice, and brought 



to sun-lit regions of the Moon for electrolysis.2 This concept is shown in Fig. 1. 



 



As envisioned, the ice tanker would receive water vapor from the processing 



system and deposit it as frost, taking advantage of the cold surrounding 



environment.2 The term “frost” denotes high-porosity (low-density) ice. The 



tanker’s finned inner surfaces would be coated with frost by an insulated 



distribution pipe. As a frost layer grows, however, so does its insulating effect, 



meaning the reduced heat flux must be characterized; there will be a point 



where the heat flux is low enough that the vapor no longer desublimes at the 



given flow rate (i.e. there is a critical heat flux that determines the tanker’s 



capacity). The behavior of frost growth in these conditions will control the 



internal surface area needed to ensure total desublimation. 



 Water vapor undergoes a phase change to ice via two modes: 1) above the 



triple point, condensation followed by freezing, and 2) below the triple point, 



desublimation (gas to solid). While both conditions require humid air to be 



cooled below the dew point, a local condition below the triple point leads to 



local desublimation (moving from right to left across Fig. 2). The vapor 



becomes super-saturated above the cold surface where the phase change 



occurs. The water partial pressure becomes lower than that of the ambient air, 



creating a difference in concentration that drives frost formation.3 Surface 



roughness, surface impurities, air velocity, and humidity levels also affect 



frost nucleation and growth.4 



 The thermophysical properties of water during desublimation are not fully 



understood. Most studies do not evaluate frost growth under near-vacuum 



conditions and do not grow frost beyond a centimeter.5-8 The majority of frost

thermal conductivity and heat transfer through the frost layer. There are likely other effects as well that were not 



studied. Reference 5 operated above the triple point, though, meaning that condensation was part of the phase 



transition path, which implies very different results from the proposed desublimation path of frost growth in near 



vacuum. 



II. Experimental Tests 



To understand the fundamental mechanisms of the heat transfer involved in ice tanker loading, an aluminum, flat, 



horizontal plate was used to grow ice in one dimension in vacuum. It simulates an interior wall or fin of the conceptual 



water tanker. The plate was chilled to the ice tanker’s radiator operating temperatures described in Ref. 2, and water 



vapor was introduced into the vacuum chamber at a controlled pressure. The frost layer height, δf, and mass, mf, were 



measured throughout the test durations, which lasted as long as 300 hours. The bulk frost layer density, ρf, was 



calculated from mass and volume measurements of frost samples. 



A NASA GRC architecture study had previously estimated that to desublime 54 kg of water, a heat flux, q, of 



approximately 116 W/m2 would be required to capture a continuous stream of vapor at 0.22 kg/hr from the pilot plant 



over 10.8 days at the given radiator operating temperature.2 Creating ice at pressures that are below but near the triple 



point (300 to 500 Pa) requires a smaller radiator than at lower pressures due to the higher desublimation temperatures. 



To maintain the expected 10.8-day duration for the smaller scale tests, a scaled-down flow rate was calculated for an 



energy balance at the selected test conditions. In a closed system such as the vacuum chamber or the ice tanker, either 



pressure or flow rate can be controlled, but not both, so one must be prioritized in the design of experiment. Because 



the accuracy of flow controller technology is insufficient for the scaled-down flow rates, pressure of the incoming 



water vapor stream was selected as the controlling variable. The water vapor pressures, and therefore the vacuum 



chamber pressures, were selected to match the pressures expected in the ice tanker. The mass change in the frost layer 



was assumed to approximate the flow rate of vapor after the pressure setpoint is reached. Each test was concluded 



when the water could no longer freeze at the desired flow rate due to insulating effects, or the frost ran out of space to 



continue growing.

Measuring the height of ice growth required 



a high precision ruler placed behind the cold plate 



and a Logitech BRIO 4K webcam aligned 



parallel to the ruler taking pictures at 5-minute 



intervals. The images were processed to separate 



the ice layer pixels from the background pixels 



by defining an image threshold based on pixel 



intensity. In order to calculate the average height, 



the cross-sectional area of the ice layer was first 



calculated using the ruler as a scaling factor for 



the camera. That area divided by the 10-cm width 



of the plate gives the average one-dimensional 



height of the frost layer. Only the ice directly 



above the plate was used for the height analysis. 



Height and macroscopic shape of the ice are

Although the ice was intended to grow only on 



the test plate, parasitic ice grew beyond the edge

not a uniform height across the width of the plate; 



the taller ice to the right of the image is

The experimental procedure for observation of frosting behavior is summarized as follows: 



 Step 1: Use the roughing pump to pump down the chamber (in the mTorr range to purge unwanted molecules) 



 Step 2: Set the chiller to operating temperature and turn on. Allow plate to reach desired temperature 



 Step 3: Pump down the small water chamber and turn heat lamp on 



 Step 4: Turn off roughing pump, turn on pressure control valve to introduce water vapor up to the desired P 



 Step 5: Observe frost growth during the experiment 



 Step 6: Terminate the experiment and perform defrosting

III. Results & Discussion 



A. Characteristics of low-pressure frost: Density 



Unexpected phenomena were observed in this low-pressure, long-



duration frost growth test campaign. First, the ice density, ρf, was very 



high, greater than that of ice formed at equivalent temperature but 



ambient pressure. Density was calculated by chipping three to four 



samples of ice off the cold plate at completion of a test for six of the seven 



tests, when the vacuum was released, and measuring mass and volume 



displacement. Density was averaged among all sample points and found 



to be 953 ± 22 kg/m3. At ambient pressure and -10°C, ice is expected to 



be 919 kg/m3. While the samples were not necessarily removed from the 



middle of the cold plate nor the same location within the chamber, the 



low variation in the calculated value (standard deviation = 26 kg/m3) 



suggests this dense ice grew everywhere. 



 There appeared to be little porosity in the frost layer. Figure 8, which 



compares frost growth at the same plate and operating temperatures, 



shows a different transparency and growth pattern between atmospheric 



and low-pressure (300 Pa) conditions. Though ρf was calculated only at 



the end of the experiment, the frost appears consistently translucent 



(more like ice) through the desublimation process, as shown in Fig. 7, 



suggesting it was dense from beginning of testing and that it did not 



collapse or densify over time. This unexpectedly high density had drastic 



consequences on the frost layer height as the expected temperature-



insulating effects were reduced; insulative capacity increases with 



porosity.

B. Characteristics of low-pressure frost: Mass and height 



To investigate the behavior of the frost layer according to 



the operating conditions described in Table 2, frost mass and 



height were measured. The frost mass throughout the first 



chronological test but last test in the matrix (Test 7) is shown 



in Fig. 9 on the left axis. Unfortunately, because the frost 



growth was much larger than anticipated, the mass exceeded 



the limits of the load cell, damaging it and rendering it 



unusable for subsequent tests. Height was used as a proxy to 



understand the amount of ice growth because the load 



increases at the same rate as the height as shown in Fig. 9 on 



the right axis. Both load and height are nearly linear at the 



beginning of the test before beginning to asymptote near the 



end of the test as the frost growth slowed. Additionally, while 



measuring mass growth rate would have been useful, the 



parasitic ice growth shown in Fig. 7 increased the total mass 



measurement, preventing direct measurement of the amount 



of ice grown on the cold plate. 



Figure 10 shows the frost layer growth through time per 



chamber pressure. Tests lasted between 50 and 300 hours to reach the critical heat flux. Two tests (Test 3 and Test 6) 



had to be stopped before reaching the intended heat flux rate because the ice grew as tall as the water vapor line and 



blocked it. The ice grew often over the expected maximum height, 2.5 cm, because the denser ice allowed more heat 



flux than expected when designing this experiment. The occasional gaps in the measurements were an artifact of the 



camera being repositioned during the long test duration. 



Tests with greater ΔT grew more ice for both pressure conditions. Tests at a higher pressure grew more ice at the 



same ΔT. The linear portion of the ice growth, shown in Fig. 10, aligns with the amount of time required for the 



chamber pressure, seen in Fig. 11, to increase from near vacuum to its test condition (P=300 Pa or 500 Pa). The slope 



of the linear region is nearly identical among all tests, and the varied temperature and pressure conditions appear to 



have affected only the time and height at which the frost growth rate levels out.

C. Characteristics of low-pressure frost: Chamber pressure, heat flux, and wall temperature 



The pressure evolution in the vacuum chamber is shown in Fig. 11. The initial stage where the pressure has not 



reached the setpoint indicates that the initial frost growth occurs at conditions closer to vacuum than the intended 



setpoint. In some of the lower temperature tests, the nominal chamber pressure was never achieved. In general, it took 



more time to reach the higher pressure setpoints. Furthermore, lower wall temperatures take more time to reach the 



setpoint pressure. This is an artifact of the experiment design to control water vapor introduction with pressure rather 



than mass flow rate. While doing so prevented over-pressurization within the chamber, it lengthened the test duration 



as the water vapor mass flow was outpaced by the freezing rate in the early stages of the test.

the test plate, when the ice height is small, the heat flux through the plate is not negligible. As the ice thickens, the 



measured heat flux value becomes more representative of steady state. After the heat flux reaches its maximum value, 



it slowly decreases as the ice layer thickens. Tests with lower temperatures show a higher heat flux value through the

D. Experiment discrepancies and anomalies 



Unexpectedly productive frost/ice growth was a noticeable aspect of the experiments. Frost grew in the interstices 



of the acrylic insulation box and the cold plate and affected the mass data. Despite Kapton insulation on the flex lines, 



a thick layer of frost grew throughout, though with low impact on the mass measurements due to their distances from 



the load cell and the spring-like nature of the lines. Both locations of exuberant frost growth impacted the flow rate 



estimations, meaning flow rate was under-estimated. The exact partial pressure of water vapor in the chamber was not 



monitored. Therefore, there is some degree of error of the sublimation temperature. To mitigate the impacts of this, 



the chamber was pumped down to approximately 100 mTorr (13.3 Pa) before water was introduced, limited by the 



capacity of the roughing pump. Assuming there is no significant leak in the chamber, it is reasonable to assume the 



water vapor partial pressure adequately represented the overall chamber pressure. 



At the beginning of the test, the rate of water vapor entering the chamber outpaced the rate of freezing on the test 



plate (and the regions of parasitic growth), shown in the increasing pressure data (Fig. 11), therefore causing some 



difference from the theoretical growth at a constant mass flow rate. The initial lower pressure of the chamber may 



have altered the frost structure or other key aspects early in the test and impacted later results. 



Due to the high density of the frost layer, mass was larger than expected and exceeded the capacity of the load cell 



and damaged it. Therefore, most tests recorded no corresponding mass data. 



Height growth was measured using a stationary webcam with a fisheye lens, which introduces parallax and 



distortion when processing the images of ice. Because the view angle constantly changed as the surface of the ice 



grew by 1 to 8 cm, error is introduced in the image processing measurement. For this reason, a separate scaling factor 



was calculated in both the x and the y directions. This additional uncertainty, however, is much lower than the value 



reported in Table 1. 



There was a slight leak in the chamber which allowed lab air to enter the chamber. This means that nitrogen, 



oxygen and other contaminants entered and created some uncertainty of the partial pressure of water vapor. This effect 



is notable, but ultimately has a low expected effect on final results

IV. Conclusion 



Water vapor forms denser, clearer, lower-porosity ice than expected under low-pressure (300 – 500 Pa) and low-



temperature (-18 to -3°C) conditions over industrially long periods (50 – 300 hr). This will significantly impact the 



design of ISRU technology for use on the Moon, especially if these results extend to more complex geometries and a 



wider range of conditions. 



The temperature difference between the cold plate (standing in for the water tanker internal wall) and the pressure- 



and temperature-controlled sublimation temperature affected the ice growth height more strongly than did the chamber 



pressure. The wider the temperature difference, the higher the ice formation rate. Regardless of temperature and 



pressure values, the tests showed a similar critical heat flux through the ice and cold plate/tanker wall, below which 



conditions are not conducive to water ice collection from vapor. 



Various lessons can be drawn from this ongoing research. The test facility, experimental hardware, and unexpected 



results made it difficult to directly measure the amount of frost. Mass flow of the water was too small to measure with 



available sensors and difficult to infer because of parasitic ice growth on the hardware. Height was calculated from 



images with low pixel resolution throughout the region of interest. The unexpected amount of frost was greater than 



the load cell could handle, so a consistent mass measurement was not possible with the available sensors. When 



planning future test campaigns, ample programmatic resources should be allotted for sensor iterations. The facility 



could not provide cold enough temperatures to desublime water vapor at more-representative lunar atmospheric 



pressures. Future tests should be conducted at such pressures to increase understanding of frost properties, including 



the density trend, at true lunar surface conditions where the ice tanker and other ISRU hardware would operate. 



A first principles numerical model with temporal resolution is under development. The unexpectedly high density 



and resulting higher heat flux have not been captured adequately in previously published models.9- 11,13 The model will 



be validated using this experimental data and will be described in an upcoming paper. 



Future work is needed to fully understand and quantify the evolving heat transfer through an ice layer at low 



pressures and temperatures. Understanding the morphology of the crystalline frost structure is of primary interest due 



to its effect on density and bulk thermal conductivity. Investigation of the material structure of the ice, such as whether 



it is cubic structure as opposed to the standard hexagonal lattice structure in atmospheric conditions, is necessary to 



determine any changes in thermal conductivity or other thermophysical aspects of the frost structure.14 More complex 



growth plate geometries are needed to validate the model and design a full-scale lunar ice tanker as a cylindrical 



geometry may have a more complex frost growth due to gravity and dynamic surface area effects. While out of scope 



for this test effort, some other studies to be investigated are the impacts of dust particulate in water vapor and the 



presence of non-desublimating species in the water vapor stream.
